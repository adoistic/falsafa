DICAEOPOLIS
How my own heart has been bitten and bitten!
My pleasures have been few — very few indeed: four.
But my pains? Heaps of them, a hundred thousand grains of sand.
Come now, let me see — what pleasure have I had that was worth my joy?
I know what I was glad to see, what cheered my heart:
those five talents that Cleon had to vomit up.
How I beamed at that — and I love the Knights
for that deed; for it was worthy of Hellas.
But then I had another pain, a tragic one,
the day I sat gaping in expectation of Aeschylus,
and the crier called out, "Bring on your chorus, Theognis!"
Imagine how that shook my heart!
Yet another pleasure: that time, after Moschus,
Dexitheus came on to sing his Boeotian air.
But this very year I nearly died, my neck wrenched round,
the day Chaeris popped up to play the high-pitched march.
Yet never, since the first time I washed myself,
have my eyebrows smarted so from the soap
as now, when there is a sovereign Assembly due
at dawn, and the Pnyx here stands deserted,
while they gossip in the marketplace, up and down,
dodging the vermilion-painted rope.
Even the Prytaneis have not come; they arrive late,
and then they jostle — you can't imagine how —
crowding in against each other for the front bench,
streaming down in a flood. But of peace,
how it might come to be, they care not at all. O city, my city!
But I am always the very first to come to the Assembly,
and I sit down. And then, when I am all alone,
I groan, I yawn, I stretch, I break wind,
I fidget, I scribble, I pluck my hairs, I do my sums,
gazing off toward my farm, in love with peace,
loathing the town and longing for my own deme,
which never said, "Buy coals!"
nor "vinegar," nor "oil," and never knew the word "buy,"
but produced everything itself, and the buying-man was gone.
So now I have come, prepared in earnest
to shout, to interrupt, to abuse the speakers,
if any of them speaks of anything but peace.
But here come the Prytaneis — at midday!
Didn't I say so? It's just as I told you:
every man elbows his way to the front seats.

HERALD
Move forward, all of you,
move forward, so that you are within the purified ground.

AMPHITHEUS
Has anyone spoken yet?

HERALD
Who wishes to address the Assembly?

AMPHITHEUS
I do.

HERALD
Who are you?

AMPHITHEUS
Amphitheus.

HERALD
Not a mortal?

AMPHITHEUS
No,
but immortal. For Amphitheus was the son of Demeter
and Triptolemus; and his son was Celeus;
and Celeus married Phaenarete, my grandmother,
and from her Lycinus was born; and from him I,
and so I am immortal. And to me the gods have entrusted
the making of a treaty with the Lacedaemonians — to me alone.
But, immortal though I am, gentlemen, I have no travel-money;
for the Prytaneis won't give me any.

HERALD
Officers! The archers!

AMPHITHEUS
O Triptolemus, O Celeus, will you stand by and watch?

DICAEOPOLIS
Gentlemen of the Prytaneis, you wrong the Assembly
in hauling this man away, who only wanted
to make a treaty for us and hang up our shields.

HERALD
Sit down. Be quiet.

DICAEOPOLIS
By Apollo, I will not,
unless you put the question of peace before me.

HERALD
The ambassadors from the King!

DICAEOPOLIS
What King? I am sick of ambassadors,
and of peacocks, and of all their swagger.

HERALD
Be quiet!

DICAEOPOLIS
Whew! Ecbatana, what a getup!

AMBASSADOR
You sent us to the Great King,
drawing a salary of two drachmas a day,
in the archonship of Euthymenes.

DICAEOPOLIS
Ah me, the drachmas!

AMBASSADOR
And indeed we wore ourselves out, journeying
across the Cayster plains, sheltering under awnings,
lolling at our ease on cushioned carriages,
dying of it.

DICAEOPOLIS
And I, of course, was kept very safe,
bedded down in the rubbish along the rampart.

AMBASSADOR
And as their guests we were forced to drink
from cups of crystal and of gold
unmixed sweet wine.

DICAEOPOLIS
O city of Cranaus,
do you see how these ambassadors mock you?

AMBASSADOR
For the barbarians count as real men
only those who can devour and drink the most.

DICAEOPOLIS
And we count cock-suckers and butt-boys.

AMBASSADOR
In the fourth year we came to the palace;
but the King had gone off to the privy with an army,
and sat there relieving himself for eight months on the Golden Hills.

DICAEOPOLIS
And how long did it take him to close up his backside again?

AMBASSADOR
By the full moon; and then he came home.
After that he entertained us, and set before us
whole oxen out of the oven.

DICAEOPOLIS
And who ever saw
oven-baked oxen? What humbug!

AMBASSADOR
And, by Zeus, a bird three times the size of Cleonymus
he served us; its name was Cheat.

DICAEOPOLIS
So that's how you were cheating us, drawing two drachmas a day.

AMBASSADOR
And now we have come bringing Pseudartabas,
the King's Eye.

DICAEOPOLIS
May a crow strike and peck it out,
that Eye of his, and yours too, ambassador.

HERALD
The King's Eye!

DICAEOPOLIS
Lord Heracles!
By the gods, man, you've got a face like a warship!
Are you rounding a headland, scanning for a dry-dock?
You've got a leather oar-port there, down around your eye.

AMBASSADOR
Come now, tell the Athenians what the King
sent you to say, Pseudartabas.

PSEUDARTABAS
Iartaman exarxan apissona satra.

AMBASSADOR
Did you understand what he says?

DICAEOPOLIS
By Apollo, not I.

AMBASSADOR
He says the King will send you gold.
Speak up louder and more clearly about the gold.

PSEUDARTABAS
No getti gold, you gape-arse Ionian.

DICAEOPOLIS
Oh, curse it — now that's clear enough.

AMBASSADOR
What's he saying now?

DICAEOPOLIS
What? He says the Ionians are gape-arses
if they expect any gold from the barbarians.

AMBASSADOR
No, no — he's speaking of measures of gold, whole bushels.

DICAEOPOLIS
What bushels? You're a great humbug.
Off with you! I'll cross-examine him myself, alone.
Come now, tell me plainly, in front of this man here —
or I'll dye you a fast Sardian scarlet:
will the Great King send us gold? (Pseudartabas shakes his head.)
Then are we being hoodwinked by our own ambassadors? (Pseudartabas nods.)
These men nodded in the Greek fashion, just now,
and there's no way they aren't from right here at home.
And of these two eunuchs, this one here —
I know who he is: Cleisthenes, son of Sibyrtius.
You, with your hot-blooded, shaven rump —
with a beard like that, you ape,
did you come to us tricked out as a eunuch?
And this other one — who on earth is he? Surely not Straton?

HERALD
Quiet! Sit down.
The Council invites the King's Eye
to the Prytaneum.

DICAEOPOLIS
Isn't this a thing to hang oneself over?
Here am I, kept hanging about,
while for them the door of hospitality is never shut.
Well, I'll do something terrible and great.
But where on earth is my Amphitheus?

AMPHITHEUS
Here I am.

DICAEOPOLIS
Take these eight drachmas for me,
and make a treaty with the Lacedaemonians, for me alone,
and for my little ones and my good wife.
As for you others — go on with your embassies and your gaping.

HERALD
Let Theorus come forward, from Sitalces.

THEORUS
Here.

DICAEOPOLIS
Another humbug being announced.

THEORUS
We should not have spent so long in Thrace —

DICAEOPOLIS
By Zeus, no, not if you hadn't been drawing such a fat salary.

THEORUS
— had it not snowed over all of Thrace
and frozen the rivers, at the very time
that Theognis was competing here.
All that while I was drinking with Sitalces.
And indeed he was a prodigious lover of Athens,
and so truly in love with you that he even wrote
upon the walls, "The Athenians are beautiful."
And his son, whom we had made an Athenian,
yearned to eat sausages at the Apaturia,
and begged his father to come to the aid of his new fatherland;
and the father swore, pouring libation, to help us
with so great an army that the Athenians would say,
"What a swarm of locusts is coming on!"

DICAEOPOLIS
May I die a wretched death if I believe a word
of what you've said here, except the part about the locusts.

THEORUS
And now the most warlike tribe of the Thracians
he has sent to you.

DICAEOPOLIS
That much at least is now clear.

HERALD
Come here, you Thracians whom Theorus brought.

DICAEOPOLIS
What in the world is this monstrosity?

THEORUS
An army of Odomanti.

DICAEOPOLIS
Odomanti — what? Tell me, what is this?
Who has stripped the foreskins off these Odomanti?

THEORUS
If a man gives these fellows two drachmas a day in pay,
they'll overrun all Boeotia with their light shields.

DICAEOPOLIS
Two drachmas to these circumcised wretches?
The lower bench of rowers would groan at that,
the saviors of the city. Oh, wretched me, I'm ruined —
the Odomanti are plundering my garlic!
Won't you put down that garlic?

THEORUS
You scoundrel,
don't go near them now that they're garlic-charged for battle.

DICAEOPOLIS
Did you Prytaneis stand by and watch this done to me
in my own homeland, and by barbarians at that?
But I forbid you to hold any Assembly
about wages for the Thracians. I tell you that
there's an omen from Zeus — a raindrop has struck me.

HERALD
The Thracians are to withdraw, and to come back the day after tomorrow.
For the Prytaneis dissolve the Assembly.

DICAEOPOLIS
Oh, wretched me, what a garlic-relish I've lost!
But here comes Amphitheus, back from Lacedaemon.
Greetings, Amphitheus!

AMPHITHEUS
Not yet — not until I stop running.
For I must flee and outrun the Acharnians.

DICAEOPOLIS
What's the matter?

AMPHITHEUS
I was hurrying here to you,
bringing your treaty; but some old men got wind of it,
men of Acharnae, tough old veterans, hard as holm-oak,
unyielding, men of Marathon, hard as maple.
Then they all shouted out, "You utter scoundrel,
you bring a treaty when our vines have been cut down?"
And they began gathering stones into their cloaks;
so I ran, and they pursued me, shouting.

DICAEOPOLIS
Let them shout. But have you got the treaty?

AMPHITHEUS
I do indeed — here, three samples to taste.
This one is the five-year vintage. Take it and taste.

DICAEOPOLIS
Ugh!

AMPHITHEUS
What's wrong?

DICAEOPOLIS
I don't like it — it smells
of pitch and the fitting-out of ships.

AMPHITHEUS
Then take this ten-year one and taste it.

DICAEOPOLIS
This one too smells of embassies to the cities,
sharp as the dawdling of our allies.

AMPHITHEUS
But this one is a thirty-year treaty,
by land and by sea.

DICAEOPOLIS
O Dionysia!
This one smells of ambrosia and nectar,
and of no more watching out for "three days' rations,"
and on the lips it says, "Go wherever you please."
This one I accept; this one I pour in libation and drink down,
and I bid the Acharnians a long farewell.
And I, set free of war and its evils,
will go in and keep the Rural Dionysia.

AMPHITHEUS
And I will run for it, away from the Acharnians.

CHORUS
This way, everyone! Follow, chase, and ask after the man
from every traveler on the road. For it is worth the city's while
to seize this fellow. Inform me, someone,
if anyone knows which way on earth the bringer of the treaty has turned.

CHORUS-LEADER
He's escaped, he's gone, he's vanished! Alas, poor me,
and alas for my years!
In my younger days it never would have happened — when I, carrying my load of coals,
ran keeping pace with Phayllus, this treaty-bearer would never
have so easily got away from me as he pursued him then,
nor would he have skipped off so lightly.

CHORUS
But now, since my shin is stiff already,
and old Lacratides feels the heaviness in his leg,
he has escaped. Still, we must pursue him; let him never gloat
at having got clean away from us Acharnians, old men though we are.

CHORUS
Whoever it is, O Father Zeus and gods, who has made a treaty with our enemies,
against whom my hateful war keeps growing on account of my ravaged lands —
I will not let up until I am driven into them like a sharp reed,
a painful one . . . tipped with an oar-blade, so that
never again may they trample my vines.

CHORUS-LEADER
But we must seek the man and look toward Ballene,
and chase him from land to land, until at last he's found;
for I could never have my fill of pelting him with stones.

DICAEOPOLIS (within)
Keep holy silence! Keep holy silence!

CHORUS
Quiet, everyone! Did you hear, men, the call for silence?
This is the very man we're looking for. This way, everyone,
out of the road! For the fellow is coming out, it seems, to sacrifice.

DICAEOPOLIS
Phales, comrade of Bacchus,
fellow-reveler, night-wanderer,
adulterer, lover of boys,
after five years I greet you,
come gladly home to my deme,
having made myself a treaty,
delivered from troubles and battles
and from Lamachuses.
For it is far sweeter, O Phales, Phales,
to catch some pretty Thracian girl, Strymodorus's slave,
the wood-gatherer, thieving from Phelleus,
and grab her by the middle, lift her up, throw her down,
and crush her grape, O
Phales, Phales!
If you drink with us, then, out of your hangover
at dawn you'll gulp down a bowl of peace,
and the shield will hang on the rafter in the soot.

CHORUS
There he is, that's him!
Pelt him, pelt him, pelt him, pelt him,
strike, strike the scoundrel!
Won't you pelt him? Won't you pelt him?

DICAEOPOLIS
Heracles! What is this? You'll smash my pot!

CHORUS
No — it's you we'll stone to death, you vile head!

DICAEOPOLIS
On what charge, you most venerable Acharnians?

CHORUS
You ask that? You are shameless and disgusting,
you traitor to your fatherland — you, alone of us,
who made a treaty, and then can look me in the face!

DICAEOPOLIS
But you don't know why I made the treaty. Hear me out!

CHORUS
Hear you out? You shall die! We'll bury you under the stones.

DICAEOPOLIS
Not before you've heard me! Bear with me, good men.

CHORUS
I will not bear with you. Don't say a word to me.
For I hate you even more than Cleon — Cleon, whom I
will one day cut up into shoe-soles for the Knights.

CHORUS
I will not listen to your long-winded speeches,
you who made a treaty with the Laconians; I'll have my revenge.

DICAEOPOLIS
Good men, leave the Laconians out of it,
and listen to my treaty — whether I did right to make it.

CHORUS
How could you ever speak of doing right, if once you've made a treaty
with men for whom no altar, no faith, no oath holds firm?

DICAEOPOLIS
I know that the Laconians, on whom we lay all the blame,
are not the cause of all our troubles.

CHORUS
Not of all of them, you villain? You dare to say that
openly now, to our faces? And shall I then spare you?

DICAEOPOLIS
Not of all of them, not of all of them. I tell you, if I speak,
I could show on many points that they too have been wronged.

CHORUS
This word is now a dreadful thing, a heart-troubling thing,
if you will dare to speak on behalf of our enemies.

DICAEOPOLIS
And if I do not speak justly, and the people do not agree,
I am willing to speak with my head laid on the chopping-block.

CHORUS
Tell me, fellow-demesmen, why do we spare our stones,
why not card this man into a scarlet rag?

DICAEOPOLIS
What a black coal of rage has boiled up in you again!
Won't you listen? Won't you listen, men of Acharnae, truly?

CHORUS
We will not listen, no.

DICAEOPOLIS
Then I'll suffer dreadful things.

CHORUS
May I perish if I listen!

DICAEOPOLIS
No, no, men of Acharnae!

CHORUS
Know that you're a dead man now.

DICAEOPOLIS
Then I'll bite you back!
For in return I'll kill the dearest of your dear ones;
since I hold hostages of yours, whom I'll seize and slaughter.

CHORUS
Tell me, fellow-demesmen, what does this threat mean,
spoken against us Acharnians? Has he some child of ours
shut up inside? Or what makes him so bold?

DICAEOPOLIS
Pelt me, if you like — for I'll destroy this one here.
I'll soon find out which of you cares anything for coals.

CHORUS
We're done for! This coal-basket is my own demesman!
But don't do what you mean to — no, no, by no means!

DICAEOPOLIS
I'll kill it — keep shouting. For I won't listen.

CHORUS
Then you'll be the death of this coal-loving fellow of my own age?

DICAEOPOLIS
You wouldn't listen to me just now when I spoke.

CHORUS
Well, then, speak now, if you like — both about the Lacedaemonian
and how, in whatever way, he is your friend;
for I will never betray this dear little coal-basket.

DICAEOPOLIS
First, then, shake the stones out onto the ground.

CHORUS
There they are, on the ground for you; and you, put your sword away again.

DICAEOPOLIS
But mind there aren't any stones still lurking in your cloaks.

CHORUS
They've been shaken out onto the ground. Don't you see it shaking?
Come, no more excuses — put away your weapon.
See, the cloak is shaken with each turn of the dance.

CHORUS
Why then don't you speak? Bring out the chopping-block to the door,
and tell us, you wretch, what this great thing is you've got.
For a powerful longing holds me to know your mind.
But, just as you laid down the terms of the contest yourself,
set the block out here and begin to speak.

DICAEOPOLIS
Look then, behold: here is the chopping-block,
and here's the little fellow who is going to speak — this tiny thing.
Never fear, by Zeus, I won't shield myself behind a buckler;
I'll say what I think on behalf of the Lacedaemonians.
And yet I'm very much afraid. For I know the temper
of the country folk, how mightily they delight
when some braggart praises them and their city,
whether justly or unjustly;
and there they are, sold out without ever noticing it.
And the old men, too — I know their souls,
how they look for nothing but to sting with a jury-vote.
And I myself know what I suffered at Cleon's hands
on account of last year's comedy.
For he dragged me into the Council-chamber,
slandered me, and tongue-lashed me with lies,
and roared like the Cycloborus, and drenched me, so that I very nearly
perished, fouled in his dirty business.
So now, before I speak, first let me
dress myself up to look as wretched as can be.

CHORUS
Why all this twisting and scheming and contriving of delays?
Take, for all I care, from Hieronymus
some shaggy, thick-haired, dark cap of Hades;
unfold the cunning devices of Sisyphus —
for this contest will admit no excuse.

DICAEOPOLIS
It is high time now to take a stout heart,
and I must go to Euripides.
Boy! Boy!

CEPHISOPHON
Who's there?

DICAEOPOLIS
Is Euripides at home?

CEPHISOPHON
He's not at home, and yet at home — if you understand me.

DICAEOPOLIS
How "at home" and yet "not at home"?

CEPHISOPHON
Rightly put, old man.
His mind is outside, gathering up little verses,
and so not at home; but he himself is at home, aloft,
composing a tragedy.

DICAEOPOLIS
O thrice-blessed Euripides,
when even your slave answers so cleverly!
Call him out.

CEPHISOPHON
But it's impossible.

DICAEOPOLIS
But all the same —
for I won't go away; I'll knock on the door.
Euripides! Little Euripides!
Hear me, if ever you heard any man.
It is Dicaeopolis who calls you, of Cholleidae — I.

EURIPIDES
But I have no time.

DICAEOPOLIS
Then have yourself wheeled out.

EURIPIDES
But it's impossible.

DICAEOPOLIS
But all the same —

EURIPIDES
Well, I'll be wheeled out; but I've no time to come down.

DICAEOPOLIS
Euripides!

EURIPIDES
Why are you bawling?

DICAEOPOLIS
You compose aloft,
when you might do it on the ground? No wonder you make your characters lame.
But why have you got those rags from your tragedy,
that pitiable clothing? No wonder you make your characters beggars.
But I beseech you by your knees, Euripides,
give me some rag from your old drama.
For I must speak a long speech to the chorus,
and it brings death if I speak it badly.

EURIPIDES
What tatters? Is it those in which Oeneus here,
the ill-starred old man, played his part?

DICAEOPOLIS
Not Oeneus's; someone still more wretched.

EURIPIDES
The blind Phoenix's?

DICAEOPOLIS
Not Phoenix, no;
there was another, more wretched than Phoenix.

EURIPIDES
What shreds of robes is the man asking for?
Surely you mean the beggar Philoctetes's?

DICAEOPOLIS
No, but someone far, far more beggarly than him.

EURIPIDES
Is it the squalid garments you want,
those that Bellerophon wore, the lame one here?

DICAEOPOLIS
Not Bellerophon; and yet that one too was
lame, a beggar, a glib fellow, a terror at speaking.

EURIPIDES
I know — Telephus the Mysian.

DICAEOPOLIS
Yes, Telephus!
Give me, I beg you, his swaddling-rags.

EURIPIDES
Boy, give him Telephus's tatters.
They lie up above the rags of Thyestes,
between those of Ino.

CEPHISOPHON
There — take these.

DICAEOPOLIS
O Zeus, who look down and look through everything,
let me dress up to look as wretched as can be.
Euripides, since you've been so generous with these,
give me the things that go along with the rags:
the little Mysian cap for around my head.
For I must seem to be a beggar today —
to be the man I am, but not to appear so;
so that the audience may know who I am,
but the chorus-men stand by like idiots,
that I may flout them with my little phrases.

EURIPIDES
I'll give it; for you contrive subtle things with your shrewd mind.

DICAEOPOLIS
Bless you — and to Telephus my thoughts!
Good — how I'm filling up already with little phrases!
But I do need a beggar's staff.

EURIPIDES
Take this and be off from these stone halls.

DICAEOPOLIS
O my soul — you see how I'm being thrust from his house,
still needing many props. Now, then, become
clinging, importunate, persistent. Euripides,
give me a little basket charred through with a lamp.

EURIPIDES
Why, you poor wretch, what need have you of this wicker-work?

DICAEOPOLIS
No need at all; but I want it all the same.

EURIPIDES
You're a nuisance — be off from my house.

DICAEOPOLIS
Ah!
Bless you — as your mother once was blessed.

EURIPIDES
Now go away from me.

DICAEOPOLIS
No — give me just one thing more,
a little cup with its lip knocked off.

EURIPIDES
Go to ruin and take it; know you're a pest to my house.

DICAEOPOLIS
By Zeus, not yet! You don't know what mischief you cause yourself.
But, O sweetest Euripides, just this one thing more —
give me a little pot stuffed up with a sponge.

EURIPIDES
Man, you'll rob me of my whole tragedy!
Take this and be off.

DICAEOPOLIS
I'm going.
And yet — what shall I do? For there's one thing more I need,
and without it I'm done for. Listen, sweetest Euripides:
let me take this and be off, and I'll come back no more.
Into the little basket give me some withered leaves.

EURIPIDES
You'll be the death of me! There — my dramas are gone.

DICAEOPOLIS
No more, then; I'm going. For I am indeed
too much of a nuisance, not knowing that princes hate me.
Oh, wretched me, I'm done for! I've forgotten
the one thing on which all my fortunes hang.
O dearest little Euripides, sweetest,
may I die most wretchedly if I ask you for anything more,
except this one thing only, this one thing only:
give me some chervil, an heirloom from your mother.

EURIPIDES
The man is insolent! Bolt the doors of the house.

DICAEOPOLIS
O my soul, we must go on our way without chervil.
Do you know how great a contest you're about to fight,
when you mean to speak on behalf of the Lacedaemonians?
Forward now, my soul; here is the starting-line.
Are you standing still? Won't you go, now that you've swallowed Euripides?
Well done. Come now, poor heart,
go yonder, and there lay down your head
and speak whatever seems good to you.
Take courage! Go! March on! I admire my heart.

CHORUS
What will you do? What will you say? Know well now,
you are a shameless, iron-nerved man,
who, offering your neck to the city,
mean, alone, to speak against them all.
The man does not tremble at the thing. Come then,
since you've chosen it yourself — speak.

DICAEOPOLIS
Bear me no grudge, spectators,
if, beggar though I am, I yet mean to speak
before the Athenians about the city, while making comedy.
For even comedy knows what is just.
And I shall say things harsh, but just.
For now, at least, Cleon won't slander me
on the ground that I speak ill of the city in the presence of strangers.
For we are by ourselves; it is the contest at the Lenaea,
and no foreigners are here yet; for neither the tribute
has come, nor the allies from the cities.
No, we're by ourselves now, threshed clean of chaff —
for the resident aliens I call the bran of the citizens.
Now I myself hate the Lacedaemonians intensely,
and may Poseidon, the god of Taenarus,
shake their houses down upon them all;
for I too have had my vines cut down.
But, since those present in this discourse are friends,
why do we lay all this to the charge of the Laconians?
For it was men of ours — and I do not mean the city,
remember this, that I do not mean the city,
but worthless little men, counterfeit coin,
dishonored and base-stamped and foreign-stamped,
who kept denouncing the Megarians' cloaks;
and if anywhere they spied a cucumber or a leveret
or a piglet or garlic or lumps of salt,
these were "Megarian goods," and were confiscated on the spot.
Now these were small things, and homely.
But some young men, drunk with the cottabus-game,
went off to Megara and stole the whore Simaetha;
and then the Megarians, garlic-stung with grief,
stole in turn two whores of Aspasia's.
And from this the beginning of the war broke out
upon all the Greeks — out of three sluts.
Then, in his wrath, Pericles the Olympian
lightened and thundered and threw all Hellas into turmoil,
and made laws written like drinking-songs,
that the Megarians must not abide on land, nor in the market,
nor on the sea, nor in the sky.
After that the Megarians, since they were starving step by step,
begged the Lacedaemonians to get the decree about the three sluts
reversed; but we refused, though they begged us often.
And from that the clashing of shields began.
Someone will say, "They shouldn't have." But what should they have done? Tell me.
Come — suppose some Lacedaemonian had sailed out in a skiff
and denounced and confiscated a Seriphian's puppy,
would you have sat quietly at home? Far from it.
No, indeed — you'd at once have launched
three hundred ships, and the city would have been full
of the din of soldiers, of shouting about a trierarch,
of pay being handed out, of figureheads being gilded,
of the colonnade groaning, of rations being measured out,
of wineskins, of oar-thongs, of men buying jars,
of garlic, of olives, of onions in nets,
of garlands, of sprats, of flute-girls, of black eyes;
and the dockyard, again, full of oar-spars being shaped,
of pegs being hammered, of oar-loops being fitted,
of pipes, of boatswains, of trills, of whistling.
This, I know, is what you would have done. And Telephus —
do we suppose he would not? Then we've no sense in us.

CHORUS

Muttering with age, we stand by the witness-stone,

CHORUS
But this man here is winged for his dinner and thinks great thoughts,
and as a sample of his life has thrown out these feathers before his door.
O Reconciliation, foster-sister of fair Cypris and the dear Graces,
how fair your face was, and I never knew it!
Would that some Eros might take and bring you and me together,
like the one in the painting, wreathed with a garland of flowers!
Or do you, perhaps, think me quite an old fellow?
Yet if I had you, I think I could still drive three furrows:
first I'd run a long row of vine-shoots,
then beside it young slips of fig-trees,
and third, the row of cultivated vine — this old man here —
and round the whole plot, olives in a circle,
so that you and I might anoint ourselves from them at each new moon.

CHORUS
I envy you your good counsel,
and still more your good cheer,
fellow, that you have here at hand.

DICAEOPOLIS
What will you say, then, when you see the thrushes roasting?

CHORUS
I think you're right in that too.

DICAEOPOLIS
Stir up the fire.

CHORUS
Did you hear how cookishly,
how daintily and dinner-wise,
he serves himself?

FARMER
Oh, wretched me!

DICAEOPOLIS
Heracles! Who's this?

FARMER
A luckless man.

DICAEOPOLIS
Then keep your troubles to yourself.

FARMER
O dearest one — since you alone have the treaty —
measure me out a little peace, even five years' worth.

DICAEOPOLIS
What's happened to you?

FARMER
I'm ruined; I've lost my pair of oxen.

DICAEOPOLIS
How?

FARMER
The Boeotians took them from Phyle.

DICAEOPOLIS
O thrice-luckless man — and yet you wear white?

FARMER
And, by Zeus, those very oxen used to keep me
in dung up to the eyes.

DICAEOPOLIS
And what do you want now?

FARMER
I've ruined my eyes weeping for the two oxen.
But if you care anything for Dercetes of Phyle,
anoint my eyes quickly with peace.

DICAEOPOLIS
But, you poor fool, I'm not in public practice.

FARMER
Come, I beseech you — see if I can get my oxen back.

DICAEOPOLIS
It can't be done; go howl to Pittalus's people.

FARMER
Just one drop of peace, then,
trickle it for me into this little reed here.

DICAEOPOLIS
Not so much as a smidgen. Go off and whine somewhere.

FARMER
Oh, wretched me, and my two farm-oxen!

CHORUS
The man has found something sweet
in the treaty, and it seems he won't
share it with anyone.

DICAEOPOLIS
Pour the honey over the sausage; grill the cuttlefish.

CHORUS
Did you hear his shrill cries?

DICAEOPOLIS
Roast the eel-steaks!

CHORUS
You'll kill me with hunger, and the
neighbors too, with the savor and
with shouting such things.

DICAEOPOLIS
Roast these, and brown them nicely.

BRIDESMAN
Dicaeopolis!

DICAEOPOLIS
Who's this? Who's this?

BRIDESMAN
A certain bridegroom sent you this meat
from his wedding-feast.

DICAEOPOLIS
That was kind of him, whoever he was.

BRIDESMAN
And he asked you, in return for the meat,
to pour him one ladle of peace into this perfume-flask,
so that he needn't go on campaign but could stay home and make love.

DICAEOPOLIS
Take it away, take the meat away, don't give it to me;
I wouldn't pour him any for ten thousand drachmas.
But who's this?

BRIDESMAN
The bridesmaid;
she wants to say something to you, from the bride, in private.

DICAEOPOLIS
Come, what is it you have to say? — How comical, O gods,
is the bride's request, the thing she so urgently asks of me:
that the bridegroom's prick may keep house at home.
Bring the treaty here, that I may give to her alone,
since she's a woman and not to blame for the war.
Hold the flask out this way, here, woman.
Do you know how to manage it? Tell this to the bride:
when they're enrolling soldiers, let her
anoint the bridegroom's prick with this by night.
Take away the treaty. Bring the wine-ladle,
that I may take it and pour out wine for the Pitcher Feast.

CHORUS
Go, then, and good cheer to you, off to the army.
How unlike are the roads you two are taking:
for the one, to drink, garlanded;
for you, to shiver and stand guard;
for the one, to sleep
beside the loveliest young girl,
and have his you-know-what rubbed.

CHORUS
Antimachus, son of Spittle, the writer, the maker of songs —
may Zeus, to put it plainly, blot him out wretchedly;
the man who, when he was choregus at the Lenaea, sent me, poor wretch, away without my dinner.
May I yet see him longing for a cuttlefish,
and may it, roasted,
sizzling, fresh from the sea, lie waiting on the table —
and just as he's about to
take it, may a dog
snatch it up and run off.

CHORUS
That's one ill for him; and may a second come by night.
For, feverish, as he walks home from his riding,
may some drunken, raving Orestes
crack him over the head; and he, wanting
to throw a stone in the dark, may grope
and grab with his hand a fresh-dropped turd,
and rush on with the
piece of marble, and then, missing his aim,
hit Cratinus instead.

LAMACHUS
Ah, ah! Oh, ah!
Hateful these woes, and chilling! Wretched me!
I am undone, struck down by an enemy spear.
But this would be the lamentable thing,
if Dicaeopolis should see me wounded
and gloat over my misfortunes.

DICAEOPOLIS
Ah, ah! Oh, ah!
The little breasts — how firm and quince-like!
Kiss me softly, my golden girls,
with the wide-open and the deep-clinging kiss.
. . .
For I'm the first to have drained my pitcher.

LAMACHUS
O wretched calamity of my woes!
Ah, ah, the agony of my wounds!

DICAEOPOLIS
. . .
Hey there, greetings, little Lamachus-on-horseback!

LAMACHUS
Hateful is my lot!

DICAEOPOLIS
Why do you kiss me?

LAMACHUS
Wretched is my lot!

DICAEOPOLIS
Why do you bite me?

LAMACHUS
Wretched me, for the heavy reckoning!

DICAEOPOLIS
Was someone charging a reckoning at the Pitcher Feast?

LAMACHUS
Oh, oh, Paean, Paean!

DICAEOPOLIS
But there's no Paean-festival today.

LAMACHUS
Hold me, hold my leg — ah! —
take hold of me, friends.

DICAEOPOLIS
And you two, take hold of me
by the middle of my prick — both of you, dear girls.

LAMACHUS
I'm dizzy, my head struck with a stone,
and I'm faint with darkness.

DICAEOPOLIS
And I want to go to bed, and I'm stiff,
and I'd grope my way to a girl in the dark.

LAMACHUS
Carry me out of doors to Pittalus's
with healing hands.

DICAEOPOLIS
Carry me to the judges! Where is the King-archon?
Give me back the wineskin!

LAMACHUS
A spear has pierced me through the bones — agony!

DICAEOPOLIS
You see this wineskin here, empty. Hail, hero of victory!

CHORUS
Hail indeed, since you call for it, old man — hero of victory!

DICAEOPOLIS
And what's more, I poured it neat and drained it at one gulp.

CHORUS
Hail then, noble fellow! Go, take your wineskin.

DICAEOPOLIS
Follow me now, singing "Hail, hero of victory!"

CHORUS
We'll follow, for your sake,
singing "Hail, hero of victory!" —
you and the wineskin.
