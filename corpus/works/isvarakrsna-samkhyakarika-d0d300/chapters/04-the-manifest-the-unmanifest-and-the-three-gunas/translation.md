**SK 10**

The vyakta / avyakta (manifest) is caused, non-eternal, non-pervasive, active, manifold, dependent, mergent [resolvable into its cause], composite, and subordinate. The vyakta / avyakta (unmanifest) is the opposite [of all these].

**SK 11**

Both the manifest and prakṛti / pradhāna / mūlaprakṛti (the primary nature) have the three guṇa (sattva, rajas, tamas) (constituent strands), are non-discriminating, are objects [of experience], are common [to all selves], are unconscious, and are productive. puruṣa (The self), however, is the opposite of these.

**SK 12**

The guṇa (sattva, rajas, tamas) (guṇas) have the natures of pleasure, pain, and dejection respectively; they serve the purposes of illumination, activity, and restraint; and their modes of operation are mutual domination, mutual support, mutual production, and mutual coupling.

**SK 13**

Sattva is held to be light and illuminating; rajas to be stimulating and mobile; tamas verily to be heavy and enveloping. Their functioning, [though their natures conflict,] is for a [single] purpose, like [the parts of] a lamp.

**SK 14**

That [the manifest] is non-discriminating and the rest is established by [its] having the three guṇas, and by the absence [in it] of what is contrary to this. And because the effect is of the nature of the qualities of its cause, the vyakta / avyakta (unmanifest) too is [thereby] established [as triguṇa].
