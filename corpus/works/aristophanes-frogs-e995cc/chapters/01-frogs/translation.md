FROGS
Brekekekex koax koax,
brekekekex koax koax!
Children of the marsh and springs,
let us raise the tuneful cry of our hymns,
my sweet-voiced song,
koax koax,
which around the Nysaean
Dionysus, son of Zeus,
in the Marshes we shrieked,
when the hung-over revel-crowd
of the people streams
through my sacred precinct on the holy Feast of Pots.
Brekekekex koax koax!

DIONYSUS
But I'm beginning to ache
in the rump, O koax koax;
to you, no doubt, it's no concern.

FROGS
Brekekekex koax koax!

DIONYSUS
But to ruin with you and your koax!
For there's nothing in you but koax.

FROGS
Naturally, you busy-meddler!
For the lyre-loving Muses have loved me,
and horn-footed Pan who plays his piping reed;
and the harper Apollo takes delight in me too,
for the sake of the reed that I nourish
underwater in the marshes as a bridge for the lyre.
Brekekekex koax koax!

DIONYSUS
But I've got blisters,
and my arse has been sweating this long while,
and any moment now it'll peep out and say—

FROGS
Brekekekex koax koax!

DIONYSUS
But O song-loving tribe,
stop.

FROGS
No — rather
we'll cry the louder, if ever
on sunny days
we leapt through the galingale
and the rushes, delighting in song
with our many-diving strains,
or, fleeing the rain of Zeus, in the depths
underwater we sang our dappled
dancing-tune
with bubble-bursting plashings.

DIONYSUS
Brekekekex koax koax!
That I take from you.

FROGS
Then we'll suffer something dreadful.

DIONYSUS
And I something more dreadful, rowing,
if I'm to burst.

FROGS
Brekekekex koax koax!

DIONYSUS
Go howl — it's no concern of mine.

FROGS
But indeed we'll keep on croaking,
as loud as our throat
can gape, all day long.

DIONYSUS
Brekekekex koax koax!
With this you'll never beat me.

FROGS
Nor will you us, in any way.

DIONYSUS
Nor will you me,
ever — for I'll keep on croaking,
even if I must all day,
brekekekex koax koax,
until I master you with my koax.
Brekekekex koax koax!

DIONYSUS
I was bound to put a stop to your koax at last!

CHARON
Whoa, stop, stop! Pull her in with the oars.
Step out; pay the fare.

DIONYSUS
Here, then — the two obols.
Xanthias! Where's Xanthias? Hey, Xanthias!

XANTHIAS
Halloo!

DIONYSUS
Come here.

XANTHIAS
Greetings, master.

DIONYSUS
What's it like over there?

XANTHIAS
Darkness and mud.

DIONYSUS
Did you see anywhere over there the father-beaters
and perjurers that he told us about?

XANTHIAS
Didn't you?

DIONYSUS
By Poseidon, I did — and I see them now.
Come, what shall we do?

XANTHIAS
Best for us to go forward,
since this is the place where the dreadful
beasts are, he said.

DIONYSUS
He'll be sorry.
He was bragging to make me afraid,
knowing I'm a fighting-man, out of jealousy.
For nothing's so swaggering as Heracles.
But I'd pray to meet some creature
and win a contest worthy of the journey.

XANTHIAS
By Zeus, and in fact I hear some noise.

DIONYSUS
Where, where is it?

XANTHIAS
Behind.

DIONYSUS
Get behind, then.

XANTHIAS
No, it's in front.

DIONYSUS
Then get in front.

XANTHIAS
And now I see, by Zeus, a great beast.

DIONYSUS
What sort?

XANTHIAS
A dreadful one. At any rate it keeps changing into all shapes:
now an ox, now a mule, now again a woman,
a most lovely one.

DIONYSUS
Where is she? Let me go at her.

XANTHIAS
But she's no longer a woman — now she's a dog.

DIONYSUS
Then it's Empusa.

XANTHIAS
At any rate her whole face
is ablaze with fire.

DIONYSUS
And has she a leg of bronze?

XANTHIAS
By Poseidon, yes — and the other of cow-dung,
be sure of it.

DIONYSUS
Where, then, can I turn?

XANTHIAS
Where can I?

DIONYSUS
Priest, protect me, so I may be your drinking-companion!

XANTHIAS
We're done for, lord Heracles!

DIONYSUS
Don't call me that,
man, I beg you — don't pronounce that name.

XANTHIAS
Dionysus, then.

DIONYSUS
That's even worse than the other.

XANTHIAS
Go on, the way you're going.

DIONYSUS
Here, here, master!

XANTHIAS
What is it?

DIONYSUS
Take heart — all's gone well with us,
and we can say, as Hegelochus did,
"For out of the storm I see again a fine-weather — weasel."
Empusa's gone.

DIONYSUS
Swear it.

XANTHIAS
By Zeus.

DIONYSUS
Swear it again.

XANTHIAS
By Zeus.

DIONYSUS
Swear.

XANTHIAS
By Zeus.

DIONYSUS
Oh dear, how I went pale at the sight of her.

XANTHIAS
And he here, in his fright, went red on your behalf.

DIONYSUS
Oh, where did these evils fall on me from?
What god shall I blame for destroying me?
"The aether, abode of Zeus," or "the foot of time"?

XANTHIAS
Hey, you!

DIONYSUS
What is it?

XANTHIAS
Didn't you hear?

DIONYSUS
What?

XANTHIAS
The breath of flutes.

DIONYSUS
I did — and a breath of torches
wafted over me, most mystic.

XANTHIAS
Then let's crouch quietly and listen.

CHORUS
Iacchus, O Iacchus!
Iacchus, O Iacchus!

XANTHIAS
That's it, master — the initiates
are sporting somewhere here, the ones he told us of.
At any rate they're singing the Iacchus of Diagoras.

DIONYSUS
I think so too. Best, then, to keep
quiet, until we know for sure.

CHORUS
Iacchus, O greatly-honoured, dwelling here in your seats,
Iacchus, O Iacchus,
come dance across this meadow
to your holy worshippers,
shaking about your head
the fruit-laden, blossoming
wreath of myrtle, and with bold
foot beating out
the unbridled, frolic-loving rite,
that holds the greatest share of grace, the pure and sacred
dance for the holy initiates.

XANTHIAS
O honoured lady, daughter of Demeter,
how sweetly there breathed over me a smell of roast pork!

DIONYSUS
Won't you keep quiet, then, in case you get a bit of sausage too?

CHORUS
Wake the blazing torches, for in your hands he comes brandishing,
Iacchus, O Iacchus,
the light-bringing star of the nocturnal rite.
The meadow shines with flame;
the old men's knees leap up;
they shake off their griefs
and the long years of ancient time,
beneath the sacred worship.
And you, blazing with your torch,
lead forth, stepping on, to the flowery marshy floor,
the dance-rousing, blessed throng of youth.

CHORUS
There must be holy silence, and let him stand aside from our dances,
whoever is unversed in such words, or unclean in mind,
or has neither seen nor danced the rites of the noble Muses,
nor was initiated in the Bacchic mysteries of the bull-eating Cratinus's tongue,
or delights in clownish verses made at the wrong moment,
or does not put an end to hateful faction nor is gracious to his fellow-citizens,
but stirs it up and fans it, lusting after his own private gains,
or, when the city is storm-tossed, takes bribes as a magistrate,
or betrays a fort or ships, or sends out forbidden goods
from Aegina, being a wretched two-percent-collector like Thorycion,
shipping packing-stuffs and linen and pitch across to Epidaurus,
or persuades someone to provide money for the enemy's ships,
or befouls the shrines of Hecate while singing in the circular choruses,
or, being a politician, nibbles away the poets' pay,
having been lampooned in the ancestral rites of Dionysus —
to these I proclaim, and again proclaim against, and a third time again proclaim,
to stand aside from the initiates' choruses; but you, rouse up the song
and our all-night festivals, which befit this feast.

CHORUS
Go now, every man, boldly
into the flowery folds
of the meadows, stamping
and joking
and playing and mocking;
and a sufficient breakfast has been eaten.

CHORUS
But come on, and see that you nobly extol
the Saviour-goddess
with your voice in song,
who declares she'll keep
the land safe through the seasons,
even if Thorycion doesn't want it.

CHORUS
Come now, raise another kind of hymn, the fruit-bearing queen,
the goddess Demeter, adorning her, and sing in sacred strains.

CHORUS
Demeter, mistress of the pure rites,
queen, stand by us,
and keep your own chorus safe,
and let me safely all day long
play and dance;

CHORUS
and let me, having said
many funny things and many serious,
and having played and mocked
worthily of your feast,
win and be crowned with the ribbon.

CHORUS
Come, on now,
call hither too the comely god
with songs, the fellow-traveller of this dancing.

CHORUS
Iacchus, greatly honoured, who found the sweetest
song of the feast, come along with us
to the goddess,
and show how without toil
you make your way over the long road.
Iacchus, lover of the dance, escort me.

CHORUS
For you it was who tore, for laughter
and for cheapness' sake, this little sandal
and this rag,
and found a way for us to play
and dance unpunished.
Iacchus, lover of the dance, escort me.

CHORUS
For just now I caught sight, with a sidelong glance,
of the lovely-faced face of a young girl,
a playmate of mine,
with her little tunic torn,
and a little breast peeping out.
Iacchus, lover of the dance, escort me.

DIONYSUS
And I'm always somehow fond of following,
and I want to play and dance
along with her.

XANTHIAS
And so do I.

CHORUS
Do you want us, then, together
to mock Archedemus,
who at seven years old hadn't yet grown his clansmen-teeth?

CHORUS
But now he's a demagogue
among the corpses up above,
and is the chief of the rascality there.

CHORUS
And Cleisthenes' son I hear
in the graveyard
plucking his backside and tearing his cheeks;

CHORUS
and he beat himself, bent over,
and wept and screamed
for Sebinus, whoever he is, of Anaphlystus.

CHORUS
And they say Callias too,
that son of Hippobinus,
fights at sea wearing a lion-skin of pussy.

DIONYSUS
Could you tell us, then,
where Pluto lives hereabouts?
For we're strangers just arrived.

CHORUS
Don't go far off,
and don't ask me again,
but know you've come right to his door.

DIONYSUS
Pick up the bags again, boy.

XANTHIAS
What was all this business?
It's nothing but "Zeus's Corinth" over and over with the bedding.

CHORUS
Go now
round the sacred circle of the goddess, through the flowery grove,
sporting, you who share in the god-loved feast;
and I'll go with the girls and women,
to where they keep the all-night vigil for the goddess, to bear the holy light.

CHORUS
Let us go to the rosy
meadows, full of flowers,
in our own manner,
the loveliest in the dance,
sporting — the manner the blessed
Fates bring together.

CHORUS
For on us alone the sun
and the cheerful light shine,
all of us who've been initiated
and led a pious
life toward strangers
and ordinary folk.

CHORUS
That's the part of a man
with sense and wits about him,
and much travelled,
to keep rolling himself always
toward the side that's doing well,
rather than to stand like a painted
figure, fixed in one
posture; and to shift round
toward the softer course
is the part of a clever man
and of a natural Theramenes.

DIONYSUS
For wouldn't it be funny if
Xanthias, a slave, on Milesian
blankets sprawled,
were kissing a dancing-girl
and then asked for a chamber-pot, while I,
looking on at him,
were fingering my own privates, and he,
being himself a rascal,
saw it, and then from my jaw
with his fist gave me a blow
and knocked out my front teeth — my chorus-row?

INNKEEPER
Plathane, Plathane, come here! Here's the rascal,
the one who once came into our inn
and ate up sixteen loaves of ours.

PLATHANE
By Zeus,
that's the very man.

XANTHIAS
Trouble's coming to somebody.

INNKEEPER
And besides these, twenty pieces of boiled meat too,
at a half-obol apiece.

XANTHIAS
Someone's going to pay for it.

INNKEEPER
And the garlic, lots of it.

DIONYSUS
You're raving, woman,
and don't know what you're saying.

INNKEEPER
Didn't you expect me,
because you've got those high boots on, to recognize you still?
What now? I haven't even mentioned the heaps of salt-fish.

PLATHANE
By Zeus, no — nor the fresh green cheese, poor me,
which this fellow ate up, baskets and all.

INNKEEPER
And then, when I tried to collect the money,
he looked fiercely at me and bellowed too.

XANTHIAS
That's just his work — that's his way everywhere.

INNKEEPER
And he drew his sword, pretending to be mad.

PLATHANE
By Zeus, poor wretch.

INNKEEPER
And the two of us, in our fright,
jumped up at once onto the loft;
and he made off, snatching up the rush-mats.

XANTHIAS
That's his work too.

PLATHANE
But we ought to do something.

INNKEEPER
Go and call my champion Cleon for me.

PLATHANE
And you call me Hyperbolus, if you happen to meet him,
so we can grind this fellow down.

INNKEEPER
O you filthy gullet,
how gladly I'd smash your grinders with a stone,
the ones you devoured my wares with!

PLATHANE
And I'd toss you into the pit.

INNKEEPER
And I'd cut out your gullet
with a sickle, the one you gulped down my tripe with.

PLATHANE
But I'll go for Cleon, who today
will summons this fellow and reel these charges out of him.

DIONYSUS
May I perish most wretchedly if I don't love Xanthias.

XANTHIAS
I know, I know your meaning. Stop, stop your talk.
I will not become Heracles.

DIONYSUS
Don't say so,
dear little Xanthias.

XANTHIAS
And how could I,
being a slave and a mortal too, become the son of Alcmene?

DIONYSUS
I know, I know you're angry, and you do it justly;
and even if you struck me, I wouldn't answer back.
But if ever in time to come I take it from you,
may I be uprooted, myself, my wife, my children,
and perish most wretchedly — and Archedemus the blear-eyed too.

XANTHIAS
I accept the oath and take it on these terms.

CHORUS
Now it's your task, since
you've taken back the costume
you had at first,
to grow young again . . .
and look the dreadful look once more,
remembering the god
whom you make yourself resemble.
But if you're caught babbling
and let fall anything soft,
you'll have to take up
the bedding again, and that's that.

XANTHIAS
You don't advise badly, men,
but I happen to be
working that out myself just now.
That if there's anything good in it,
he'll try to take this away again,
I know well.
But all the same I'll show
myself manly in spirit
and looking sharp as marjoram.
And it seems I must, for I already hear
a noise at the door.

AEACUS
Tie up this dog-thief, quick,
so he can pay the penalty. Get on with it.

DIONYSUS
Trouble's come to someone.

XANTHIAS
Go to the ravens! Keep off!

AEACUS
Aha, will you fight too?
Ditylas and Sceblyas and Pardocas,
come here and fight this fellow.

DIONYSUS
Isn't it dreadful, that this man should hit you
when he's the thief, and stealing others' goods besides?

AEACUS
No — it's monstrous.

DIONYSUS
Outrageous and dreadful, in fact.

XANTHIAS
And indeed, by Zeus,
if I ever came here before, may I be dead,
or if I stole anything of yours worth even a hair.
And I'll do you a very noble thing:
take this slave of mine here and torture him,
and if you ever catch me doing wrong, lead me off and kill me.

AEACUS
And how shall I torture him?

XANTHIAS
Every way: tying him on a ladder,
hanging him up, flogging him with a bristle-whip, flaying him,
racking him, and pouring vinegar up his nose besides,
piling bricks on him, everything else — only don't beat
this man with a leek or a fresh green onion.

AEACUS
A fair offer. And if I cripple your slave
by beating him, the money's to be set aside for you.

XANTHIAS
Not for me, indeed. Just take him off and torture him.

AEACUS
Right here, rather, so he may speak before your eyes.
Put down your baggage quickly, and mind you tell
no lie here.

DIONYSUS
I forbid anyone
to torture me, since I'm immortal; or if you do,
blame yourself.

AEACUS
What do you say?

DIONYSUS
I say I'm immortal, Dionysus son of Zeus,
and this one's the slave.

AEACUS
Do you hear that?

XANTHIAS
I do.
And so much the more is he to be whipped;
for if he's a god, he won't feel it.

DIONYSUS
Then, since you too claim to be a god,
why don't you take the same blows as I?

XANTHIAS
A fair offer. And whichever of us two you see
crying first, or minding at all
when he's beaten, reckon him to be no god.

AEACUS
There's no way you're not a gentlemanly fellow;
for you proceed to what's just. Strip, then.

XANTHIAS
How will you torture us fairly?

AEACUS
Easily —
blow for blow, each of you.

XANTHIAS
Well said.

AEACUS
There.

XANTHIAS
Watch now, in case you see me flinch.

AEACUS
I've already hit you.

XANTHIAS
By Zeus, you haven't.

AEACUS
I don't think I have either.
But I'll go to this one and hit him.

DIONYSUS
When?

AEACUS
There, I've hit you.

DIONYSUS
Then how come I didn't even sneeze?

AEACUS
I don't know. I'll have another go at this one.

XANTHIAS
Won't you get on with it, then? Attataî!

AEACUS
Why the "attataî"?
You weren't hurt, were you?

XANTHIAS
By Zeus, no — but I was thinking
when the Festival of Heracles at Diomeia comes round.

AEACUS
A holy man. I must go back to this one.

DIONYSUS
Ow, ow!

AEACUS
What is it?

DIONYSUS
I see horsemen.

AEACUS
Then why are you crying?

DIONYSUS
I smell onions.

AEACUS
Since you mind not at all.

DIONYSUS
It's nothing to me.

AEACUS
Then I must go to this one again.

XANTHIAS
Oh no.

AEACUS
What is it?

XANTHIAS
Take out the thorn.

AEACUS
What's this business? Back to this one again.

DIONYSUS
"Apollo — who hold Delos or Pytho somewhere—"

XANTHIAS
He was hurt — didn't you hear?

DIONYSUS
Not I — for
I was calling to mind an iambic line of Hipponax.

XANTHIAS
You're getting nowhere. Pound his flanks.

AEACUS
By Zeus, no — come, hold out your belly.

DIONYSUS
"Poseidon—"

XANTHIAS
Someone was hurt.

DIONYSUS
"—who rule the Aegean headlands or the depths of the grey sea."

AEACUS
By Demeter, I can't make out at all
which of you is the god. But go inside;
for the master himself will recognize you,
and Persephone too, since they're gods as well.

DIONYSUS
You're right. But I could have wished you'd thought of that
sooner, before I took the blows.

CHORUS
Muse of the sacred dances, step here and come for the delight of my song,
to see the great crowd of people, where wits
beyond number are sitting,
greedier for fame than Cleophon, on whose ever-babbling lips
there roars terribly
a Thracian swallow,
perched on a barbarous leaf,
and shrills a tearful nightingale-tune, that he'll perish,
even if the votes come out equal.

CHORUS
It is right for the sacred chorus to share in counselling the city
toward good and in teaching it. First, then, it seems good to us
to make the citizens equal and to take away their fears,
and if anyone tripped, brought down by Phrynichus's wrestling-tricks,
I say there should be allowed to those who slipped then
the chance, on stating their case, to undo their former faults.
Next, I say no one in the city should be without rights;
for it's shameful that men who fought a single sea-battle
and Plataeans should at once be masters instead of slaves.
And this I couldn't say isn't well done —
no, I praise it: it's the only sensible thing you've done.
And besides this, it's fitting that you, with whom and whose fathers
many a time fought at sea and who are kindred by birth,
should grant this one favour to those who ask it.
But, relaxing your anger, you who are wisest by nature,
let us willingly make all men our kinsmen
and citizens with full rights, whoever fights at sea with us.
But if we puff ourselves up and stand on our dignity over this,
with our city in the very arms of the waves,
in some later time we'll be thought to have no sense.

CHORUS
And if I'm right in judging the life or character of a man
who'll yet be sorry,
not for long will this ape who now troubles us,
little Cleigenes,
the most rascally bath-attendant of all who rule over the cinder-mixing
adulterated lye-ash
and Cimolian earth,
linger out his time; and seeing this, he's
not peaceful, for fear he might some day be stripped, drunk,
walking without a stick.

CHORUS
Often it has seemed to us the city has been affected
the same way toward the fine, good citizens
as toward the old coinage and the new gold.
For these, which are not debased,
but the finest of all, as it seems, of coins,
and the only ones rightly struck and ringing true
among Greeks and barbarians everywhere,
we make no use of, but use these wretched coppers,
struck yesterday and the day before with the basest stamp.
And of the citizens, those whom we know to be well-born and prudent
men, and just, and fine and good,
and bred in wrestling-schools and choruses and music,
we abuse; while the coppers and foreigners and red-heads
and rascals and sons of rascals, all of them, we employ for everything,
the latest comers, whom the city in former times
would not readily have used even for scapegoats at random.
But even now, you fools, change your ways,
use the good men again. For if you succeed, it's reasonable;
and if you stumble, at least, if you suffer anything,
you'll seem to the wise to suffer from a worthy tree.

AEACUS
By Zeus the Saviour, a gentleman
is this master of yours.

XANTHIAS
Of course he's a gentleman —
he who knows only how to drink and screw.

AEACUS
But that he didn't hit you, when you were plainly proved wrong,
that you, being a slave, claimed to be the master.

XANTHIAS
He'd have been sorry if he had.

AEACUS
Now that's a proper slave's trick
you've done at once, the very thing I delight in doing.

XANTHIAS
You delight in it, do you?

AEACUS
No — I feel I'm in heaven
whenever I curse my master in secret.

XANTHIAS
And what about muttering, when, after getting
many blows, you go out the door?

AEACUS
That pleases me too.

XANTHIAS
And what about meddling?

AEACUS
By Zeus, I know nothing finer.

XANTHIAS
Zeus of kinship! And eavesdropping on the masters
whatever they chatter about?

AEACUS
No — I'm more than mad about it.

XANTHIAS
And blabbing it to those outside?

AEACUS
Me?
By Zeus, when I do that, I positively come off.

XANTHIAS
O Phoebus Apollo, give me your right hand,
and let me kiss you, and you kiss me too, and tell me,
by Zeus, who is our god of common floggings,
what's this uproar inside, and the shouting
and the abuse?

AEACUS
Aeschylus's and Euripides's.

XANTHIAS
Ah.

AEACUS
A great, great business has been stirred up
among the corpses, and a very great faction.

XANTHIAS
From what cause?

AEACUS
There's a law established here,
that among all the arts that are great and clever,
the man who's best of his own craftsmen
should get free meals in the Prytaneum
and a throne next to Pluto's—

XANTHIAS
I understand.

AEACUS
—until someone else more skilled in the art
than he should arrive; and then he must give way.

XANTHIAS
Why, then, has this upset Aeschylus?

AEACUS
He held the tragic throne,
as being supreme in the art.

XANTHIAS
And now who?

AEACUS
When Euripides came down, he kept showing off
to the cloak-snatchers and the cut-purses
and the father-beaters and the burglars,
which is the multitude there in Hades; and they, hearing
his counter-pleadings and twists and turns,
went wild and reckoned him the cleverest;
and then, puffed up, he laid claim to the throne
where Aeschylus sat.

XANTHIAS
And wasn't he pelted?

AEACUS
By Zeus, no — but the people cried out to hold a trial
of which of the two was the more skilled in the art.

XANTHIAS
The people of the rascals?

AEACUS
By Zeus, sky-high in number.

XANTHIAS
And weren't there others as allies of Aeschylus?

AEACUS
The good is scarce — just as it is here.

XANTHIAS
What, then, is Pluto preparing to do?

AEACUS
To hold a contest at once and a trial
and a test of their art.

XANTHIAS
And then how is it
that Sophocles didn't lay claim to the throne too?

AEACUS
By Zeus, not he — but he kissed Aeschylus
when he came down, and held out his right hand,
and Aeschylus yielded the throne to him;
but now he meant, as Cleidemides said,
to sit as a reserve. And if Aeschylus wins,
he'll stay in his place; but if not, he said
he'd contend over the art against Euripides.

XANTHIAS
So it's going to happen?

AEACUS
By Zeus, a little later.
And then the dreadful things will be stirred up.
For poetry will be weighed in the balance—

XANTHIAS
What? They'll weigh tragedy like sacrificial meat?

AEACUS
And they'll bring out rulers and measuring-rods of verses
and folding frames—

XANTHIAS
Are they going to make bricks?

AEACUS
—and diameters and wedges. For Euripides
says he'll test the tragedies word by word.

XANTHIAS
Aeschylus, I imagine, takes it hard.

AEACUS
At any rate he glowered bull-like, ducking his head down.

XANTHIAS
And who will judge all this?

AEACUS
That was the difficulty;
for both of them found a scarcity of clever men.
For Aeschylus didn't get on with the Athenians—

XANTHIAS
Perhaps he thought the burglars were many.

AEACUS
—and he reckoned the rest mere nonsense for judging
the natures of poets. So in the end they entrusted it
to your master, since he was experienced in the art.
But let's go in; for whenever the masters
are in earnest, beatings fall to us.

CHORUS
Surely the loud-thunderer will feel a dreadful anger within,
when he sees his sharp-tongued rival
whetting his tooth; then, under a dreadful madness,
his eyes will roll.

CHORUS
There will be helmet-tossing strife of horse-plumed words,
and splinterings of axle-pegs and chisel-work of deeds,
as a man wards off the horse-prancing
phrases of a mind-building man.

CHORUS
Bristling the shaggy-necked mane of his self-grown crest,
knitting a dreadful frown, roaring, he'll hurl
bolt-fastened phrases, tearing them off plank by plank
with earth-born blast;

CHORUS
and from there the mouth-working, word-testing, smooth
tongue, unrolling itself, moving its envious reins,
splitting hairs over the phrases, will whittle down
a great labour of the lungs.

CHORUS
O nine holy maiden
Muses of Zeus, who look upon the subtle, shrewd minds
of sentence-coining men, when into strife with sharp-witted
twisting wrestling-tricks they come, contending,
come and behold the power
of two most dreadful mouths to furnish
phrases and shavings of words.
For now the great contest of wisdom moves to its work at last.

DIONYSUS
You two, pray something also, before you speak your lines.

AESCHYLUS
Demeter, you who nourished my mind,
let me be worthy of your mysteries.

DIONYSUS
And you too, take the frankincense and put it on.

EURIPIDES
No, thanks;
for there are other gods to whom I pray.

DIONYSUS
Private ones of your own, a new minting?

EURIPIDES
Just so.

DIONYSUS
Go on, then, pray to your private gods.

EURIPIDES
Aether, my pasture, and you, pivot of the tongue,
and wit, and nostrils that scent things out,
grant that I rightly refute whatever arguments I touch.

CHORUS
And indeed we long
to hear from these two clever men
what eloquence of words
you'll set on its hostile road.
For the tongue is wild,
and the spirit of both not without daring,
nor are their minds unmoved.
So it's reasonable to expect
the one will say something witty
and finely polished,
the other, tearing up his arguments roots and all,
falling on him,
will scatter many
tumbling-grounds of words.

DIONYSUS
But you must speak as quick as you can; and so see that you
both say witty things, and no comparisons, nor such as another might say.

EURIPIDES
Well, as for myself and what sort of poet I am,
I'll explain at the very end; but him I'll refute first,
showing how he was a braggart and a cheat, and with what tricks
he deceived the spectators, taking them as fools, bred on Phrynichus.
First of all he'd seat some single character, veiled,
an Achilles or a Niobe, not showing the face,
a mere figurehead of tragedy, not grunting even this much.

DIONYSUS
By Zeus, that's true.

EURIPIDES
And the chorus would drive home
strings of songs, four in a row, right on end; and the characters were silent.

DIONYSUS
But I enjoyed the silence, and it pleased me
no less than the chatterers do now.

EURIPIDES
For you were a simpleton,
be sure of it.

DIONYSUS
I think so myself. But why did the fellow do this?

EURIPIDES
Out of charlatanry, so the spectator would sit expecting
when Niobe would say something; and the play would drag on.

DIONYSUS
Oh, the utter villain — how I was hoaxed by him!
Why do you fidget and fret?

EURIPIDES
Because I'm refuting him.
And then, when he'd babbled that sort of thing and the play
was already halfway through, he'd say a dozen ox-sized words,
with eyebrows and crests, dreadful bogey-faced things,
unknown to the audience.

AESCHYLUS
Poor wretched me!

DIONYSUS
Be quiet.

EURIPIDES
And he'd say nothing clear, not one thing—

DIONYSUS
Don't grind your teeth.

EURIPIDES
—but Scamanders, or trenches, or eagle-griffins
of beaten bronze on shields, and horse-cliff phrases,
which weren't easy to make out.

DIONYSUS
By the gods, I at any rate
once lay awake a long while in the night
trying to figure out what kind of bird his tawny horse-cock is.

AESCHYLUS
It was a device painted on the ships, you utter ignoramus.

DIONYSUS
And I thought it was Eryxis, Philoxenus's son.

EURIPIDES
And then was it right to put a cock into tragedies?

AESCHYLUS
And you, god-forsaken man, what sort of things did you write?

EURIPIDES
Not horse-cocks, by Zeus, nor goat-stags, like you,
the things they paint on the Persian tapestries;
but as soon as I first took over the art from you,
swollen with bombast and burdensome phrases,
I first of all slimmed it down and took off the weight
with little verses and walks and white beets,
giving it a juice of chatter strained from books;
then I nourished it up with solo-songs—

DIONYSUS
Mixing in Cephisophon.

EURIPIDES
—and then I didn't babble whatever came, nor plunge in and confuse things,
but the first character to come on would tell me at once the lineage
of the play.

DIONYSUS
For that, by Zeus, was better than telling your own.

EURIPIDES
Then from the very first lines I'd leave nothing idle,
but the woman would speak to me, and the slave no less,
and the master, and the maiden, and the old woman.

AESCHYLUS
And then, indeed,
shouldn't you have died for daring such things?

EURIPIDES
By Apollo, no;
for I did it for democracy's sake.

DIONYSUS
Let that go, my friend;
for that's not your finest stroll-and-talk topic.

EURIPIDES
Then I taught these people here to chatter—

AESCHYLUS
So I say too.
And before you taught them, you should have burst in two.

EURIPIDES
—and to apply subtle rules and squarings of verses,
to think, to see, to understand, to twist, to love, to scheme,
to suspect mischief, to consider everything all round—

AESCHYLUS
So I say too.

EURIPIDES
—bringing in household matters, the ones we deal with, that we live among,
which I could be tested on; for these people, being in the know,
could examine my art. But I didn't talk big,
dragging them away from thinking, nor did I astound them
by making Cycnuses and Memnons with bells on their horses' cheek-pieces.
And you'll know his pupils and mine, each lot.
His are Phormisius and Megaenetus the Mane,
trumpet-spear-and-beard men, sneer-and-pine-tree-benders;
but mine are Cleitophon and the smart Theramenes.

DIONYSUS
Theramenes? A clever man, and shrewd in everything,
who, if he falls in with troubles somewhere and stands close beside them,
comes out clear of the troubles — no Chian but a Ceian.

EURIPIDES
Such things, indeed, I taught
these people to consider,
putting reasoning into the art
and reflection, so that now they conceive
everything and discern it,
all else, and how to manage their houses
better than before,
and to look closely — "How does this stand?
Where's this of mine? Who took that?"

DIONYSUS
By the gods, yes — now at any rate every Athenian,
when he comes in,
bawls at his servants
and asks, "Where's the pot?
Who's eaten the head off
this sprat? The bowl
from last year's dead and gone.
Where's yesterday's garlic?
Who's nibbled the olive?"
Whereas before, the most utterly witless,
gaping Mammacythus-dolts,
Melitides-simpletons, used to sit there.

CHORUS
You see all this, glorious Achilles;
but come, what will you say to it?
Only see . . .
that your spirit doesn't snatch you
and carry you outside the olives;
for he's accused you dreadfully.
But see, noble sir,
that you don't answer in anger,
but reefing your sails, using
only the tips of them,
and then gradually bear on
and watch,
when you find the breeze
smooth and steady.

DIONYSUS
But O first of the Greeks to tower up stately phrases
and adorn tragic balderdash, take heart and let your stream flow.

AESCHYLUS
I'm angry at the encounter, and my innards are vexed,
that I should have to answer this man; but so he can't say I'm at a loss,
answer me — for what reason should one admire a poet?

EURIPIDES
For cleverness and good counsel, because we make better
the people in the cities.

AESCHYLUS
So if you haven't done that,
but instead from good and noble men have turned out the basest,
what penalty will you say you deserve to suffer?

DIONYSUS
Death — don't ask him.

AESCHYLUS
Consider, then, what sort of men he received from me at first,
whether noble and four cubits tall, and not city-shirkers,
nor loungers about the market nor knaves as now, nor villains,
but breathing spear and lance and white-crested helmets
and casques and greaves and seven-oxhide-thick courage.

DIONYSUS
And now this trouble's coming on; he'll wear me out with his helmet-making.

EURIPIDES
And what did you do to teach them to be so noble?

DIONYSUS
Aeschylus, speak, and don't grow vexed, standing on your dignity so stubbornly.

AESCHYLUS
By making a play full of Ares.

DIONYSUS
What sort?

AESCHYLUS
The Seven Against Thebes;
seeing which, every man longed to be warlike.

DIONYSUS
That's a bad thing you've done, then; for you've made the Thebans
braver for war — and for that, take a beating.

AESCHYLUS
But you could have practised it yourselves; you just didn't turn to that.
Then, producing the Persians after this, I taught them to long
to conquer their enemies always, adorning a most excellent deed.

DIONYSUS
I delighted, at any rate, when I heard of the dead Darius,
and the chorus at once clapped their hands like this and cried, "Iauoî!"

AESCHYLUS
For these are the things poets should practise. Consider, from the start,
how useful the noble poets have been.
For Orpheus revealed to us the mysteries and abstinence from murder;
Musaeus, cures of diseases and oracles; Hesiod,
the working of the land, the seasons of crops, ploughings; and the divine Homer —
from what did he get honour and glory, except that he taught useful things,
formations, virtues, the arming of men?

DIONYSUS
And yet he didn't teach Pantacles,
clumsiest of men; for the other day, when he was marching in a procession,
he'd first tied on his helmet and then was going to tie on the crest.

AESCHYLUS
But many other good men, of whom Lamachus the hero was one;
from these my mind took the impress and made many men of valour,
Patrocluses, lion-hearted Teucers, so I might rouse a citizen
to stretch himself to match these men, whenever he hears the trumpet.
But, by Zeus, I didn't make Phaedras into whores, nor Stheneboeas,
nor does anyone know of any woman in love that I ever put on stage.

EURIPIDES
By Zeus, no — for there was nothing of Aphrodite in you.

AESCHYLUS
Nor may there be.
But on you and yours she sat heavily, in great force,
so that she knocked you down yourself.

DIONYSUS
By Zeus, yes, that's true.
For the things you did to other men's wives, you yourself were struck with.

EURIPIDES
And what harm, you wretch, do my Stheneboeas do the city?

AESCHYLUS
Because you persuaded noble wives of noble men
to drink hemlock, in shame over your Bellerophons.

EURIPIDES
But did I make up a tale that wasn't true about Phaedra?

AESCHYLUS
By Zeus, no, it was true; but the poet should hide what's base,
and not bring it forward nor teach it. For little children
have a teacher who instructs them, but grown men have poets.
So we must say good things, surely.

EURIPIDES
So if you speak of Lycabettuses
and the bulk of Parnassuses to us, is that teaching good things,
when you ought to speak in human terms?

AESCHYLUS
But, you wretch, of necessity
great thoughts and ideas must give birth to phrases their equal.
And besides, it's fitting that demigods should use grander phrases;
for they wear far statelier garments than ours, too.
Where I set a good example, you spoiled it.

EURIPIDES
By doing what?

AESCHYLUS
First, by dressing kings in rags, so they'd seem
pitiable to men.

EURIPIDES
And what harm did I do by that?

AESCHYLUS
Because of it no rich man is willing to serve as trierarch,
but he wraps himself in rags and weeps and says he's poor.

DIONYSUS
By Demeter, yes — with a tunic of soft wool underneath.
And if he deceives them by saying this, he bobs up among the fishmongers.

AESCHYLUS
Then again you taught the practice of chatter and prating,
which emptied the wrestling-schools and wore down the backsides
of the prating young men, and persuaded the Paralus crew
to talk back to their officers. And yet in those days, when I was alive,
they knew nothing but to call for barley-cake and to cry "yo-heave-ho."

DIONYSUS
By Apollo, yes — and to fart into the mouth of the man at the lower oar,
and to befoul his messmate, and going ashore to mug somebody.
But now he argues back and rows no more, and sails this way and that.

AESCHYLUS
Of what evils is he not the cause?
Didn't he show procuresses,
and women giving birth in the temples,
and mingling with their brothers,
and saying that living is not living?
And then, from these, our city
was filled to the brim with under-clerks
and clownish demagogue-apes
forever deceiving the people,
and no one can carry the torch any more
through lack of training, even now.

DIONYSUS
By Zeus, no indeed — so that I shrivelled up
laughing at the Panathenaea, when
some slow fellow ran along bent over,
pale and fat, falling behind
and in a dreadful state; and then the Potters
at the gates beat his
belly, ribs, flanks, rump,
and he, struck with the flats of their hands,
farting a little,
blew on his torch and fled.

CHORUS
Great is the matter, much the strife, mighty the war that comes.
A hard task, then, to decide,
when one strains violently,
and the other can turn round and bear down keenly.
But don't stay sitting in the same spot;
for there are many openings, and other clever tricks besides.
Whatever, then, you have to wrangle over,
speak, go at it, lay bare
the old and the new,
and risk saying something subtle and clever.

CHORUS
And if you're afraid of this, that some ignorance may attend
the spectators, so they won't grasp
the subtleties as you speak,
have no dread of that; for it's no longer so.
For they're veterans of campaigns,
and each one has a book and learns the clever points;
their natures are excellent anyway,
and now they're whetted besides.
So fear nothing, but
go through everything — for the spectators' sakes, since they're wise.

CHORUS
Whatever will come of it?
For I, for one, am full of worry,
what fault he'll find
with the man who has made by far the most
and finest songs
of all up to now.
For I wonder how
he'll ever find fault with this one,
the Bacchic lord,
and I fear for him.

EURIPIDES
Marvellous songs indeed — it'll soon be shown.
For I'll cut down all his songs into one.

DIONYSUS
And I'll keep count, taking these pebbles.
(Someone plays a little flute-interlude.)

EURIPIDES
"Phthian Achilles, why, hearing the man-slaying
— alas, the blow! — do you not draw near to the rescue?
Hermes our ancestor we honour, we of the lake.
Alas, the blow! Do you not draw near to the rescue?"

DIONYSUS
That's two blows for you, Aeschylus.

EURIPIDES
"Most glorious of the Achaeans, far-ruling son of Atreus, learn from me.
Alas, the blow! Do you not draw near to the rescue?"

DIONYSUS
That's a third blow for you, Aeschylus.

EURIPIDES
"Keep holy silence; the bee-keepers are near to open the house of Artemis.
Alas, the blow! Do you not draw near to the rescue?
I have authority to proclaim the auspicious power of men on the road.
Alas, the blow! Do you not draw near to the rescue?"

DIONYSUS
O King Zeus, what a quantity of blows!
For my part, then, I want to go to the bath-house;
for from the blows my kidneys are swollen.

EURIPIDES
Not before you hear another batch of songs
worked up from the lyre-singing modes.

DIONYSUS
Get on, then, finish; and don't add any more blows.

EURIPIDES
"How the twin-throned power of the Achaeans, of the youth of Hellas,
tophlattothrat, tophlattothrat,
sends the Sphinx, presiding bitch of evil days,
tophlattothrat, tophlattothrat,
with spear and avenging hand, a rushing bird,
tophlattothrat, tophlattothrat,
granting it to meet the bold air-roving hounds,
tophlattothrat, tophlattothrat,
and the host that leans against Ajax,
tophlattothrat, tophlattothrat."

DIONYSUS
What's this "phlattothrat"? From Marathon, or
where did you collect these well-rope-twister's songs?

AESCHYLUS
But at least I brought them from a fair source to a fair end,
so I shouldn't be seen plucking the same sacred
meadow of the Muses as Phrynichus;
but he brings his from everywhere — whores' tunes,
Meletus's drinking-songs, Carian flute-pieces,
dirges, dance-songs. It'll soon be shown.
Someone bring me the little lyre. And yet what need
of a lyre for these? Where's the girl who beats time
with the castanets? Come here, Muse of Euripides,
the one fit to sing such songs to.

DIONYSUS
This Muse, I'm sure, never went whoring on Lesbos, no.

AESCHYLUS
"Halcyons, who beside the ever-flowing waves
of the sea babble on,
wetting the skin of your wings
with watery drops, dewed with spray;
and you spiders under the roof, along the corners,
twirl-i-i-irl with your fingers
the loom-wrought weavings,
the labours of the singing shuttle,
where the flute-loving dolphin leaped
about the dark-prowed beaks,
oracles and race-courses,
the gladness of the vine-blossom,
the pain-stilling tendril of the grape.
Throw your arms about me, child."
You see this foot?

DIONYSUS
I see it.

AESCHYLUS
What about this one? Do you see it?

DIONYSUS
I see it.

AESCHYLUS
And you, doing such stuff,
dare to find fault with my songs,
you who make your songs
in the twelve-postured manner of Cyrene?
Those are your lyric songs. But I still want
to go through the manner of your solo-songs.
"O dark-gleaming gloom
of the night, what
ill-starred dream
do you send me from the unseen,
a servant of Hades,
having a lifeless life,
child of black Night,
a shuddering, dreadful sight,
clad in black corpse-shrouds,
glaring murder, murder,
with great talons.
But, handmaids, light me a lamp,
and in pitchers draw dew from the rivers, and heat water,
that I may wash away the god-sent dream.
Ho, sea-spirit!
There it is! Ho, housemates,
behold these portents!
Glyce has snatched my cock
and is gone.
O mountain-born Nymphs!
O Mania, seize her!
And I, poor wretch, happened to be busy
at my own work,
twirl-i-i-irling in my hands
a spindle full of flax,
making a thread, so that
in the dim dawn to the market
I might carry it and sell it;
but he flew up, flew up to the aether
on the lightest tips of his wings,
and to me left woes, woes,
and tears, tears from my eyes
I shed, I shed, the wretched one.
But O Cretans, children of Ida,
take up your bows and come to my aid,
and ply your limbs, circling the house.
And together let Dictynna, the fair child Artemis,
come with her hound-bitches all through the house, everywhere;
and you, daughter of Zeus, holding aloft
the double-fired torches, sharpest in your hands, Hecate, light my way
to Glyce's, so that
going in I may search out the theft."

DIONYSUS
Stop now, the two of you, with the songs.

AESCHYLUS
I've had enough too.
For I want to bring him to the scales themselves,
which alone will test our poetry.
For the weight of the phrases will put us to the test.

DIONYSUS
Come here, then, if I really must
also peddle the art of poets like cheese.

CHORUS
Toilsome, these clever men!
For here's yet another marvel,
a new one, full of strangeness;
who else would have thought of it?
. . .
By heaven, if anyone of the men I chanced to meet
had told me of it,
I'd not have believed him, but would have thought
he was talking nonsense.

DIONYSUS
Come now, stand beside the pans of the scales,

AESCHYLUS and EURIPIDES
There.

DIONYSUS
And each of you, taking hold, speak your line,
and don't let go before I cuckoo at you.

AESCHYLUS and EURIPIDES
We're holding on.

DIONYSUS
Now each speak your line into the scale.

EURIPIDES
"Would that the Argo's hull had not winged its way across—"

AESCHYLUS
"River Spercheius, and you cattle-grazing haunts—"

DIONYSUS
Cuckoo — let go. And this one's pan
goes far the lower.

EURIPIDES
And what's the reason for it?

DIONYSUS
Because he put in a river, dampening his line
wool-merchant-style, like the wool,
while you put in a winged line.

EURIPIDES
But let him speak another and set it against mine.

DIONYSUS
Take hold again, then.

AESCHYLUS and EURIPIDES
There.

DIONYSUS
Speak.

EURIPIDES
"There is no other shrine of Persuasion but Speech."

AESCHYLUS
"For Death alone of the gods loves not gifts."

DIONYSUS
Let go, let go. And this one's again sinks down;
for he put in Death, the heaviest of evils.

EURIPIDES
And I put in Persuasion, a line most finely said.

DIONYSUS
But Persuasion is light and has no sense.
Look for something else, then, of the heavy-weighted kind,
something that'll drag the scale down for you, strong and great.

EURIPIDES
Come, where have I anything like that? Where?

DIONYSUS
I'll tell you:
"Achilles has thrown two dice and a four."
Speak, then, since this is your last bout.

EURIPIDES
"And in his right hand he took the iron-laden club."

AESCHYLUS
"For chariot upon chariot, and corpse on corpse."

DIONYSUS
He's tricked you again, even now.

EURIPIDES
How?

DIONYSUS
He put in two chariots and two corpses,
which not even a hundred Egyptians could lift.

AESCHYLUS
And let him no longer match me line by line, but let him
himself, his children, his wife, Cephisophon,
get into the scale, taking up his books;
while I'll say just two lines of mine.

DIONYSUS
The men are my friends, and I won't judge them.
For I won't make myself an enemy to either.
For one I think clever, and in the other I delight.

PLUTO
Then you'll accomplish nothing of what you came for?

DIONYSUS
And if I do judge?

PLUTO
Take whichever you judge and go off,
so you won't have come in vain.

DIONYSUS
Bless you. Come, hear this from me.
I came down for a poet. To what end?
So the city, saved, might keep up its choruses.
So whichever of you gives the city
more useful counsel — that one, I think, I'll bring back.
First, then, what opinion does each of you
hold about Alcibiades? For the city is in hard labour over him.

EURIPIDES
And what opinion does it hold about him?

DIONYSUS
What?
It longs for him, and hates him, and wants to have him.
But say what you both think about this.

EURIPIDES
I hate a citizen who is by nature slow
to help his country and swift to harm it greatly,
resourceful for himself but helpless for the city.

DIONYSUS
Well said, by Poseidon. And you, what opinion do you hold?

AESCHYLUS
One should not rear a lion's cub in a city,
but if someone does rear one, he must serve its ways.

DIONYSUS
By Zeus the Saviour, I'm hard put to decide;
for the one spoke cleverly, the other clearly.
But let each of you speak one more opinion
about the city — what salvation you each have for it.

EURIPIDES
If someone gave Cleocritus wings of Cinesias,
and the breezes lifted them over the level of the sea—

DIONYSUS
It would look funny; but what sense is in it?

EURIPIDES
If, in a sea-battle, they then held vinegar-cruets
and sprinkled it into the enemy's eyes.
I know, and I'm willing to tell.

DIONYSUS
Speak.

EURIPIDES
When we consider trustworthy what is now untrusted,
and what's trusted, untrustworthy—

DIONYSUS
How? I don't understand.
Speak somewhat less learnedly and more clearly.

EURIPIDES
If, in the citizens we now trust,
we put no trust, and those we don't use,
those we used, perhaps we'd be saved.
If now we fare ill with these men, how
could we, doing the opposite, not be saved?

DIONYSUS
Well said, Palamedes, O cleverest of natures.
Did you find this out yourself, or Cephisophon?

EURIPIDES
I alone; the vinegar-cruets were Cephisophon.

DIONYSUS
What about you? What do you say?

AESCHYLUS
Tell me first
whom the city employs: is it the good men?

DIONYSUS
How could it?
It hates them most.

AESCHYLUS
And delights in the bad?

DIONYSUS
Not it, indeed, but it uses them under compulsion.

AESCHYLUS
How then could anyone save such a city,
which neither a fine cloak nor a coarse one suits?

DIONYSUS
Find a way, by Zeus, if you're to come up again.

AESCHYLUS
I'd say it there; here I don't want to.

DIONYSUS
Don't, then; but send up your good things from here.

AESCHYLUS
When they reckon the enemy's land
to be their own, and their own the enemy's,
and the ships their resource, and their resource a want of resource.

DIONYSUS
Good — except that the juror swallows it all alone.

PLUTO
Make your judgment, then.

DIONYSUS
This shall be my judgment of you two:
I'll choose the one my soul desires.

EURIPIDES
Then, remembering the gods by whom you swore
you'd take me home, choose your friends.

DIONYSUS
"My tongue has sworn" — but I'll choose Aeschylus.

EURIPIDES
What have you done, you filthiest of men?

DIONYSUS
Me?
I've judged Aeschylus the winner. Why not?

EURIPIDES
You look me in the face after the foulest deed?

DIONYSUS
What's foul, if it doesn't seem so to the spectators?

EURIPIDES
You wretch, will you really leave me dead?

DIONYSUS
Who knows if living is dying,
and breathing is dining, and sleeping a coverlet?

PLUTO
Come inside, then, Dionysus.

DIONYSUS
What for?

PLUTO
So I may entertain you both before you sail off.

DIONYSUS
Well said,
by Zeus; for I've no quarrel with the business.

CHORUS
Blessed is the man who has
keen understanding perfected.
There's much to learn from many cases.
For this man, thought to have good sense,
goes back home again,
for the good of his fellow-citizens,
and for the good of his own
kinsmen and friends,
through his being shrewd.

CHORUS
So it's a fine thing not to sit
beside Socrates and chatter,
casting off music
and neglecting the greatest things
of the tragic art.
And to idle away one's time
on solemn-seeming words
and the scratchings of trifles,
is the part of a man out of his wits.

PLUTO
Go now with joy, Aeschylus, on your way,
and save our city
with good counsels, and educate
the senseless — there are many of them.
And take this and carry it to Cleophon,
and this to the revenue-collectors,
Myrmex and Nicomachus together,
and this to Archenomus;
and tell them to come quickly
to me here and not to delay;
and if they don't come quickly, I,
by Apollo, will brand them
and tie them up
together with Adeimantus, son of Leucolophus,
and send them quickly underground.

AESCHYLUS
I'll do this; and you hand over my seat
to Sophocles to keep
and preserve, in case I should ever
come here again. For I judge him
to be second in wisdom.
And remember that the rascal,
the false-speaker and the buffoon,
never sits on my seat,
not even against his will.

PLUTO
Light him on his way, then,
with the sacred torches, and escort him
with his own songs
and chants, singing aloud.

CHORUS
First, good speed on the road give to the poet departing,
rising into the light, you gods beneath the earth,
and to the city good thoughts of great blessings.
For so we'd be wholly freed from great griefs
and from grievous armed encounters. And let Cleophon do the fighting,
and any other of these who wishes, in his ancestral fields.
