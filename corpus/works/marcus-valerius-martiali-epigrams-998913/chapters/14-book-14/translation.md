1. I
While the knight and his lord the senator delight in their dinner-suits,
And the freedman's cap suits our Jove well;
While the slave fears no aedile as he rattles the dice-box,
Seeing the icy ponds so near at hand:
Take the alternating lots of rich and poor:
Let each guest give his own gifts to his own.
"These are trifles and trumpery, and things cheaper still."
Who doesn't know it? Or who denies what's so plain?
But what better to do, Saturn, on your soggy days,
Which your own son gave you in place of heaven?
Would you have me write of Thebes, or Troy, or wicked Mycenae?
"Play," you say, "with nuts." I've no wish to lose my nuts.

2. II
At whatever point you like you can end this little book:
Every piece is wrapped up in two lines.
You ask why headings are written in? I'll explain:
So that, if you prefer, you may read the headings alone.

3. III. Citron-Wood Writing-Tablets
Had we not been cut into thin leaves of wood,
We'd be the noble burden of a Libyan tusk.

4. IV. Five-Leaved Tablets
The lord's happy threshold steams with slaughtered bullocks
When high honor is granted on the fivefold wax.

5. V. Ivory Writing-Tablets
Lest the dull wax darken your tired eyes,
Let black lettering paint the snow-white ivory.

6. VI. Three-Leaved Tablets
Then you'll think our triple tablets no cheap gift,
When your girl writes on them that she will come.

7. VII. Parchment Tablets
Take these for wax, though it be called parchment:
You'll erase them whenever you want to write afresh.

8. VIII. Vitellian Tablets
Though a girl has not yet read these,
She knows what the Vitellian tablets want.

9. IX. The Same
Because you see them so small, you think they're sent to a girl.
You're wrong: this tablet asks for money too.

10. X. Larger Sheets
There's no reason to think the gift a paltry one,
When a poet gives you blank sheets of paper.

11. XI. Letter-Paper
Whether sent to a slight acquaintance or a dear comrade,
This paper is wont to call them all its own.

12. XII. Ivory Money-Boxes
These boxes it is wrong to fill with anything
But yellow coin: let cheap wood carry silver.

13. XIII. Wooden Money-Box
If anything is still left in the dregs of our little box,
It will be a gift. There's nothing? Then the box itself will be.

14. XIV. Ivory Dice
When no die has shown you the same face twice,
You'll say I have given you a great gift.

15. XV. Gaming-Dice
Let me, the die, not equal the dice in number, so long
As my throw is often bigger than theirs.

16. XVI. The Dice-Tower
The cheating hand that knows how to throw loaded dice,
If it throws through me, will gain nothing but wishes.

17. XVII. The Gaming-Board
On this side the counter is scored by twice-six pips;
On this the discolored piece dies to a twin foe.

18. XVIII. Counters
If you play the wars of the cunning brigands,
This piece of glass will be your soldier and your foe.

19. XIX. Nuts
Gambling with nuts seems small and harmless;
Yet often it has stripped boys of their backsides.

20. XX. A Pen-Case
Drawing the case, remember to arm it with pens:
We've given the rest; you supply the lighter things.

21. XXI. A Stylus-Case
Here you'll have a stylus-case armed with its own iron:
Give it to a boy, and it will be no slight gift.

22. XXII. A Toothpick
Mastic is better: but if you lack a leafy point,
A quill can clear your teeth.

23. XXIII. An Ear-Pick
If your ear is troubled by a fretful itch,
We give you arms suited to such cravings.

24. XXIV. A Golden Hairpin
Lest your damp hair stain the bright silk,
Let a pin fix and hold up your twisted locks.

25. XXV. Combs
What will this boxwood do, which will find no hair,
Given to you with its many-cleft teeth?

26. XXVI. Hair-Dye
Chattian foam fires the Teutonic hair:
You can be smarter with captive locks.

27. XXVII. Soap
If, gray-haired, you mean to change your long-lived locks,
Take Mattiac balls — but why, when you're bald?

28. XXVIII. A Sunshade
Take this parasol to beat the fierce sun:
Even if there's wind, your own canopy will shield you.

29. XXIX. A Broad-Brimmed Hat
I'll watch with you in the Pompeian theater:
For often the man in charge denies the people the awnings.

30. XXX. Hunting-Spears
They'll meet the boars and wait for the lions,
They'll go in against bears, if only the hand be steady.

31. XXXI. A Hunting-Knife
If you groan that your spear, with its long shaft, is knocked aside,
This short blade will go at the huge boar hand to hand.

32. XXXII. A Belt-Dagger
This will be the soldier's glory and an omen of welcome honor,
A weapon fit to gird a tribune's side.

33. XXXIII. A Dagger
A dagger, which a short groove marks with a curving vein.
Salo dipped it, hissing, in his icy waters.

34. XXXIV. A Sickle
A leader's settled peace bent me to gentle uses.
Now I belong to the farmer; before, I was the soldier's.

35. XXXV. A Little Axe
When the sad auction was held to pay off debts,
This was bought for four hundred thousand.

36. XXXVI. Barber's Tools
These tools are fit for trimming your hair;
This one's good for long nails, that one for cheeks.

37. XXXVII. A Book-Box
Unless you give me choice little books,
I'll let in the moths and the savage worms.

38. XXXVIII. A Bundle of Reed-Pens
The Memphian land gives reeds handy for paper;
Let the rest of the marsh be woven into roofing for you.

39. XXXIX. A Bedroom Lamp
The sweet lamp, confidante of your couch:
Do whatever you please, I'll keep silent.

40. XL. A Tallow Candle
Fortune gave you the lamp's handmaid,
Who, wakeful, sees the whole darkness through.

41. XLI. A Many-Nozzled Lamp
Since I light the whole banquet with my flames
And carry so many nozzles, I am called a single lamp.

42. XLII. A Wax Candle
This candle will give you fire by night:
For your boy has filched away the lamp.

43. XLIII. A Corinthian Candelabrum
Candles gave us our ancient name:
The oily lamp knew nothing of our thrifty fathers.

44. XLIV. A Wooden Candelabrum
You see it's wood; unless you watch the lights, you'll have
A great blaze made out of your candelabrum.

45. XLV. A Peasant's Ball
This peasant-ball that swells with stiff feathers
Is less loose than the bladder-ball, less tight than the hard.

46. XLVI. A Triangle-Ball
If you know how to drive me back with skilled left hands,
I'm yours. You don't? Then, bumpkin, give back the ball.

47. XLVII. A Bladder-Ball
Off with you, young men: a gentle age suits me:
The bladder-ball befits boys at play, and old men.

48. XLVIII. Hand-Balls
The swift athlete snatches these up in Antaeus' dust,
Who makes his great neck big with futile toil.

49. XLIX. Dumb-Bells
Why do strong arms waste themselves on the foolish dumb-bell?
Digging a vineyard exercises men better.

50. L. An Oil-Cap
Lest the foul wrestler's grease soil your shining hair,
With this cap you can tuck away your oiled locks.

51. LI. Strigils
Pergamon sent these. Scrape yourself with the curved iron:
The fuller won't wear out your towels so often.

52. LII. A Horn Oil-Flask
A bullock lately wore me on his brow:
Yet you took me for true rhinoceros-horn.

53. LIII. A Rhinoceros
Lately admired in our lord's Ausonian arena,
Here for you is the one for whom the bull was a ball.

54. LIV. A Baby's Rattle
If some wailing little home-born slave hangs at your neck,
Let this chattering rattle shake in his tender hand.

55. LV. A Whip
You'll get nowhere with this whip, lash as you will,
If a horse of the purple team runs for you.

56. LVI. Tooth-Powder
What have I to do with you? Let a girl take me:
I'm not used to polishing bought-in teeth.

57. LVII. Behen-Nut Oil
What neither Virgil nor Homer names in song,
This is made of perfume and of the behen-nut.

58. LVIII. Saltpetre-Foam
Are you a rustic? You don't know my Greek name:
I'm called "foam of nitre." Are you Greek? Then aphronitrum.

59. LIX. Balsam-Oils
Balsams enchant me; these are the perfumes of men:
Reek of Cosmus' luxuries, you young wives.

60. LX. Bean-Meal Wash
A welcome gift it will be, and not useless to a chafed belly,
If you seek Stephanus' baths by broad daylight.

61. LXI. A Horn Lantern
I, the road's guide, am carried golden, my flame shut in,
And the small lamp is safe in my bosom.

62. LXII. A Bladder-Lantern
If I'm not horn, am I any dingier? Or does the man
Who comes against me take me for a bladder?

63. LXIII. Pipes
The drunken flute-girl splits us with her dripping cheeks:
Often she has two at once, often a single pipe.

64. LXIV. A Reed-Pipe
Why do you laugh at me, joined of wax and reed?
Such was the first pipe ever built.

65. LXV. Woolen Slippers
If by chance there's no slave-boy and you fancy
Putting on slippers, the foot will be its own boy.

66. LXVI. A Breast-Band
You could have bound your chest with a bull's hide:
For that skin cannot hold those breasts of yours.

67. LXVII. A Peacock Fly-Whisk
That which keeps the filthy flies from licking your lunch
Was once the proud tail of an exquisite bird.

68. LXVIII. An Ox-Tail Fly-Whisk
If your clothes get dirtied with yellow dust,
Let this light tail brush it off with gentle strokes.

69. LXIX. A Rhodian Cake
Don't strike your erring slave's teeth with your fist:
Let him eat the cake bright Rhodes sent you.

70. LXX. A Priapus of Wheat-Bread
If you want to be full, you may eat our Priapus:
Though you gnaw his very loins, you'll stay clean.

71. LXXI. A Pig
This pig will make you a good Saturnalia,
Fattened among the foaming boars on acorns.

72. LXXII. A Sausage
The sausage that comes to you in midwinter's season
Had come seven days before Saturn's feast.

73. LXXIII. A Parrot
I, a parrot, will learn from you the names of others:
This I've learned to say of myself — "Hail, Caesar."

74. LXXIV. A Crow
Crow, the greeter — why are you held a fellator?
No member has ever entered your head.

75. LXXV. A Nightingale
Philomela weeps the crime of incestuous Tereus, and she
Who was a speechless girl is now a chattering bird.

76. LXXVI. A Magpie
I, the talking magpie, greet you, my master, with sure voice:
If you didn't see me, you'd deny I'm a bird.

77. LXXVII. An Ivory Cage
If you have such a bird as Catullus' beloved
Lesbia wept for, here it can live.

78. LXXVIII. A Medicine-Case
You see the medicine-case of the healing art: you'll have
Gifts that Paccius would wish were his own.

79. LXXIX. Whips
Play, wanton slaves, but only play:
These are sealed up for me, just for the five days.

80. LXXX. Canes
Hated too much by boys, dear to schoolmasters,
We are the wood made famous by Prometheus' gift.

81. LXXXI. A Beggar's Wallet
Lest it bring a beggar's lunch to the bearded naked man,
And sleep with a surly dog, the wallet asks for alms.

82. LXXXII. Brooms
The palm-broom bears witness it once was prized.
But now the cleaner-up has left the broom at leisure.

83. LXXXIII. An Ivory Back-Scratcher
This hand will guard your shoulders from the biting nuisance
Of the flea, or anything fouler than a flea.

84. LXXXIV. A Book-Rest
Lest your toga or cloak make your books bristle,
This fir will give your scrolls a long span of life.

85. LXXXV. A Peacock-Couch
The bird of Juno gives the bed-frame its most lovely name
With its painted feathers — but it was Argus before.

86. LXXXVI. A Saddle-Cloth
Hunter, take the housing for your girt-up cob:
For a sore tends to rise from a bare horse.

87. LXXXVII. A Dining-Couch
Take the crescent-couch inlaid with horn:
It seats eight; let whoever is your friend come.

88. LXXXVIII. A Relish-Tray
If you believe a female tortoise is set in me,
You're deceived: I'm the male prey of the sea.

89. LXXXIX. A Citron-Wood Table
Take these blessed woods, gifts of Atlas:
Whoever gives gifts of gold will give less.

90. XC. A Maple Table
I'm not curl-grained, nor a daughter of the Moorish wood,
But my wood too has known elegant feasts.

91. XCI. Ivory Tusks
You who carry the bulls' great bodies, you ask
Whether they could have borne up Libya's beams?

92. XCII. A Five-Foot Rule
The holm-oak rule, marked with points and closed with a sharp tip,
Often serves to expose the contractor's frauds.

93. XCIII. Antique Cups
This is no recent glory, nor work of our chisel:
Mentor was the first to drink from these, while he made them.

94. XCIV. Bold Cups
We are the plebeian embossed-ware of bold glass,
And our jewel is not cracked by boiling water.

95. XCV. A Chased Golden Bowl
Though I redden, high-born, with Galician metal,
I glory more in the art: for this is Mys' labor.

96. XCVI. Vatinian Cups
Take the cheap memorial of the cobbler Vatinius —
A cup; but his nose was longer.

97. XCVII. Gold-Inlaid Platters
Don't disgrace the big gold platters with a little mullet:
At the very least it should weigh two pounds.

98. XCVIII. Arretine Ware
We warn you, don't too much scorn Arretine ware:
Porsena was elegant with Tuscan earthenware.

99. XCIX. A Basket
I, a basket, came barbarian from the painted Britons;
But now Rome would rather call me her own.

100. C. A Drinking-Bowl
If the land of learned Catullus is not unknown to you,
You've drunk Raetian wines from my crock.

101. CI. A Mushroom-Dish
Though mushrooms have given me so noble a name,
I serve — for shame, alas! — the first-cut cabbage-shoots.

102. CII. Surrentine Cups
Take cups born from no cheap dust,
But the smooth chiseled work of the Surrentine wheel.

103. CIII. A Snow-Strainer
Break my Setine wine, I advise, with our snow:
With cheaper wine you can wet the linen.

104. CIV. A Snow-Bag
Our linens too know how to thin the snow:
No water leaps from your strainer colder.

105. CV. Serving-Jugs
Cold won't be wanting, nor hot, to him who asks.
But you — spare us your game of fastidious thirst.

106. CVI. An Earthen Pitcher
Here a red pitcher with a splayed handle is given you.
The Stoic Fronto used to ask for cold water in this.

107. CVII. Wine-Baskets
The Satyrs love us, Bacchus loves us, the drunken tigress,
Trained to lick its master's drenched feet.

108. CVIII. Saguntine Cups
What no anxious servant need hold and keep,
Take — cups made of Saguntine clay.

109. CIX. Jeweled Cups
See how the gold gleams jeweled with Scythian fires.
How many fingers this cup has stripped!

110. CX. A Drinking-Flask
From this jewel, which keeps the name of Cosmus,
You may drink, voluptuary, if your thirst is for spikenard.

111. CXI. Crystal Ware
While you fear to break it, you'll break the crystal: hands
Too careless and too anxious both go wrong.

112. CXII. A Glass Storm-Cloud
The cloud that comes from Jove will pour rich waters
For mixing in the cups: this will give you wine.

113. CXIII. Murra-Ware
If you drink hot, the murra suits burning Falernian
And the flavor turns out better than the neat wine.

114. CXIV. A Cuman Bowl
This bowl, ruddy with Cuman clay,
The chaste Sibyl sent you, her fellow townswoman.

115. CXV. Glass Cups
You see the wit of the Nile: how often, while its maker
Longs to add more, ah, he has ruined the work!

116. CXVI. A Snow-Flask
You drink Spoletine, or wine laid down in Marsian cellars:
What do you want with the noble chill of boiled water?

117. CXVII. The Same
Not to drink snow, but to drink fresh water
Brewed from snow — that is ingenious thirst's invention.

118. CXVIII. The Same
Mixing the smoke of Massilia with snow-cooled waves —
Go easy, boy, lest your water cost you more.

119. CXIX. An Earthen Chamber-Pot
While I'm called for with a snap of fingers and the slave delays,
Oh, how often the bolster has become my rival!

120. CXX. A Silver Spoon
Though knights and senators call me a ligula,
The unschooled grammarians call me a lingula.

121. CXXI. Snail-Spoons
I'm handy for snails, but no less useful for eggs.
Do you know, then, why I'm rather called a snail-spoon?

122. CXXII. Rings
Once a frequent, now a rare, friend gives us.
Happy the man whose comrade is a knight not stranger-born.

123. CXXIII. A Ring-Case
Often the heavy ring slips from greasy fingers,
But your gem will be safe in my keeping.

124. CXXIV. A Toga
The Romans, lords of the world, the nation of the gown,
He made, who gave the stars to his great father.

125. CXXV. The Same
If you find it easy to lose your morning sleep,
The dole will often come with a threadbare toga.

126. CXXVI. A Greatcoat
It's a poor man's gift, but no poor man's use:
This greatcoat we send you in place of a mantle.

127. CXXVII. Dark Canusian Wool
This Canusian, just like clouded mead,
Will be your gift. Be glad: it won't soon turn old.

128. CXXVIII. A Hooded Cape
Gaul clothes you in a Santonian hooded cape.
It was lately the cloak of long-tailed monkeys.

129. CXXIX. Red Canusian Wool
Rome dresses more in dark, Gaul in red,
And this color pleases boys and soldiers.

130. CXXX. A Leather Cape
Though you set out on the road under an ever-clear sky,
Let the leather never fail you against sudden rains.

131. CXXXI. Scarlet Cloaks
If you back the Blues or Greens, why take scarlet?
Take care lest that lot make you a turncoat.

132. CXXXII. A Felt Cap
If I could, I'd wish to have sent whole cloaks:
As it is, I send a gift only for your head.

133. CXXXIII. Baetic Cloaks
My wool tells no lies, I'm not changed in the cauldron.
Let Tyrian dyes please thus: my own sheep dyed me.

134. CXXXIV. A Breast-Band
Band, hold in check your mistress' growing breasts,
So that there be something for our hand to grasp and cover.

135. CXXXV. White Cloaks
We commend ourselves by their use at the amphitheater,
When the white cloak covers the chilly togas.

136. CXXXVI. Dinner-Clothes
We know no courts and no court-summonses:
Our task is to recline on painted couches.

137. CXXXVII. A Neck-Wrap
If perhaps I give you a little book to recite,
Let this neck-wrap stand surety for your ears.

138. CXXXVIII. A Frieze-Mantle
In wintertime smooth fabrics are not much use:
My shaggy nap warms your cloaks.

139. CXXXIX. A Hand-Towel
Let shaggy linens better guard your citron table:
There can be a ring-mark on our table-tops.

140. CXL. Liburnian Hoods
You never knew, you fool, how to attach cloaks to us:
You'd put on white — strip off the green ones.

141. CXLI. Cilician Foot-Socks
No wool gave these, but the beard of a smelly he-goat:
The sole can hide in the Cinyphian fold.

142. CXLII. A Dinner-Suit
While the toga is glad to rest through the five days,
You may by your own right put on this attire.

143. CXLIII. Paduan Tunics
Paduan triple-weaves use up much wool,
And a saw could cut the thick tunics.

144. CXLIV. A Sponge
This sponge falls to you by lot, useful for wiping tables,
Light as it is when it swells with squeezed-out water.

145. CXLV. A Frieze Cloak
Such whiteness is in me, such grace of nap,
That you'd want to put me on even in mid-harvest.

146. CXLVI. A Bolster
Dip your head in Cosmus' nard, and the bolster will smell of it:
When the hair loses the perfume, the feather keeps it.

147. CXLVII. Frieze Bed-Coverlets
The shaggy spreads gleam with purple tapestries.
What use, if your old wife freezes you?

148. CXLVIII. Blankets
Lest the bare couch lie open with no spread,
We sisters have come, joined, to you.

149. CXLIX. A Shoulder-Wrap
I fear the big-bosomed; hand me to a tender girl,
That the linen may enjoy a snow-white breast.

150. CL. Damask Bedspreads
This the Memphian land gives you as a gift: Babylon's needle
Is already beaten by the loom of the Nile.

151. CLI. A Girdle
I'm long enough now; but if the belly swells
With a sweet weight, I'll become a short girdle for you.

152. CLII. A Square Coverlet
The land of learned Catullus sends you blankets:
We come from the region of Helicaon.

153. CLIII. A Half-Apron
Let the rich man give a tunic: I can gird you up.
Were I rich, I'd give both gifts.

154. CLIV. Amethystine Wools
Drunk as I am with the blood of the Sidonian shell,
I don't see why I should be called a sober wool.

155. CLV. White Wools
Apulia is noble for first-rate fleeces, Parma for the second;
The Altinum sheep wins praise for the third.

156. CLVI. Tyrian Wools
A shepherd gave us to his Spartan love:
The purple of mother Leda was inferior.

157. CLVII. Pollentine Wools
This land yields not only mourning wools of dark fleece,
But it is wont to give its own cups as well.

158. CLVIII. The Same
A drab wool, true, but made for the sheared attendants —
The sort the table does not summon from the first rank.

159. CLIX. Leuconian Stuffing
Is the cover too close upon the pressed-down feathers?
Take fleece scraped from the Leuconian saddle-cloths.

160. CLX. Circus Stuffing
Chopped marsh-reed is called Circus stuffing.
The poor man buys this litter in place of Leuconian.

161. CLXI. Down
Weary, you can rest on Amyclaean down,
Given you by the inner wool of the swan.

162. CLXII. Hay
Let the frail mattress swell for you, the mule cheated of its feed.
Pale care does not come to a hard bed.

163. CLXIII. A Bell
Give back the ball: the bath-bell sounds. You keep on playing?
You want to go home washed by the Virgin spring alone.

164. CLXIV. A Discus
When the gleaming weights of the Spartan discus fly,
Keep away, boys: let it harm only once.

165. CLXV. A Lyre
It gave Eurydice back to the bard: but he lost her,
Trusting himself too little, loving without patience.

166. CLXVI. The Same
Often it was thrown out of the Pompeian theater —
The lyre that led the woods and held the beasts spellbound.

167. CLXVII. A Plectrum
Lest a hot blister rise on your rubbed thumb,
Let a white plectrum adorn the docile lyre.

168. CLXVIII. A Hoop
The wheel must be set rolling: you give us a useful gift:
This hoop is for boys, but for me it will be music.

169. CLXIX. The Same
Why does the chattering ring wander round the loose circle?
So the crowd it meets may yield to the tinkling hoops.

170. CLXX. A Golden Statue of Victory
This is given him without a lot, on whom the Rhine
Bestowed a true name. Pour ten cups of Falernian, boy.

171. CLXXI. An Earthenware "Boy of Brutus"
The glory of so small a statuette is not obscure:
Brutus was the lover of this boy.

172. CLXXII. A Corinthian "Lizard-Slayer"
Spare the lizard creeping toward you, treacherous boy:
It longs to perish by your fingers.

173. CLXXIII. A Painted Hyacinthus on a Panel
The Oebalian boy turns his dying eyes from the hated discus —
Phoebus' fault and grief.

174. CLXXIV. A Marble Hermaphroditus
Male he entered the spring, and rose up both:
One part has the father's, the rest the mother's.

175. CLXXV. A Painted Danaë
Why did Danaë take a price from you, ruler of Olympus,
If Leda gave herself to you for nothing?

176. CLXXVI. A German Mask
I am a potter's joke, the mask of a redheaded Batavian.
The face you laugh at, a child fears.

177. CLXXVII. A Corinthian Hercules
The infant crushes the twin snakes and does not even look:
Already the Hydra might have feared his tender hands.

178. CLXXVIII. An Earthenware Hercules
I'm fragile: but you, I warn you, don't scorn the statuette:
Alcides is not ashamed to bear my name.

179. CLXXIX. A Silver Minerva
Tell me, fierce maiden, since you have helmet and spear,
Why have you no aegis? "Caesar has it."

180. CLXXX. A Painted Europa
You could have done better to change into a bull, best father of gods,
Back then, when Io was a heifer to you.

181. CLXXXI. A Marble Leander
Bold Leander cried out amid the swelling waves:
"Drown me, you floods, when I am on my way back."

182. CLXXXII. An Earthenware Figure of a Hunchback
Drunk, I think, Prometheus made these monsters on earth:
He too played with the Saturnalia's clay.

183. CLXXXIII. Homer's Battle of the Frogs
Read right through the frogs sung in Maeonian song,
And learn to unknit your brow over my trifles.

184. CLXXXIV. Homer on Parchment Tablets
The Iliad and Ulysses, foe to Priam's realm,
Lie hidden, stored together in many a fold of skin.

185. CLXXXV. Virgil's Gnat
Take, eager reader, the Gnat of eloquent Virgil,
Lest, the nuts put aside, you read "Arms and the man."

186. CLXXXVI. Virgil on Parchment
How small a parchment holds the boundless Virgil!
The first leaf bears his very face.

187. CLXXXVII. Menander's Thaïs
With this play he first toyed with the wanton loves of youth;
Glycera was no boy's mistress — Thaïs was.

188. CLXXXVIII. Cicero on Parchment
If this parchment is your companion, imagine
You're traveling long roads in Cicero's company.

189. CLXXXIX. Propertius' Monobiblos
Cynthia — the youthful song of eloquent Propertius —
Won fame, and gave no less than she received.

190. CXC. Livy on Parchment
Vast Livy is compressed into scanty skins,
Whom my library cannot hold whole.

191. CXCI. Sallust
This man, as the hearts of learned men declare,
Crispus, will be the first in Roman history.

192. CXCII. Ovid's Metamorphoses on Parchment
This mass, built up of many-folded leaves,
Carries Naso's fifteen books of song.

193. CXCIII. Tibullus
Wanton Nemesis fired her lover Tibullus,
Whom it pleased to be nothing in his whole house.

194. CXCIV. Lucan
There are some who say I am no poet:
But the bookseller who sells me thinks I am.

195. CXCV. Catullus
Great Verona owes as much to her Catullus
As little Mantua to her Virgil.

196. CXCVI. Calvus' "On the Use of Cold Water"
This paper, which tells you of springs and the names of waters,
Itself swam better in its own waters once.

197. CXCVII. Dwarf Mules
You need fear no fall from these mules:
You often sit nearly higher on the ground.

198. CXCVIII. A Gallic Lapdog
If you want to hear the charms of the little lapdog,
The whole page is too short for my telling.

199. CXCIX. An Asturian Pony
This little Asturian, who gathers his swift hoof to the beat,
Came from the gold-bearing tribes.

200. CC. A Greyhound
The keen greyhound hunts not for himself but for his master,
And will bring you the hare unharmed in his teeth.

201. CCI. A Wrestling-Master
I don't love him for winning, but because he knows how to lose
And has better learned the art of the bedroom-bout.

202. CCII. An Ape
The clever ape, dodging the spears flung at me —
If I had a tail, I'd be a long-tailed monkey.

203. CCIII. A Girl from Cadiz
She wriggles so quivering, itches so caressing, that she'd make
A masturbator of Hippolytus himself.

204. CCIV. Cymbals
The bronze that mourns the Celaenaean loves of the Mother,
The hungry Gallus is often wont to sell.

205. CCV. A Boy
Let mine be a boy smooth by his years, not by pumice,
For whose sake no girl can please me.

206. CCVI. A Love-Girdle
Bind to your neck, boy, pure love itself,
A girdle warm from Venus' bosom.

207. CCVII. The Same
Take the girdle steeped in Cytherean nectar:
This belt fired even Jove, the lover.

208. CCVIII. A Shorthand-Writer
Though the words run fast, the hand is faster still:
The tongue has not yet finished, the right hand has.

209. CCIX. A Cuttlefish-Shell
Let the light Mareotic bark be made of a sea-shell:
The reed-pen will run an unhindered course.

210. CCX. A Fool
His stupor is not feigned, nor cunningly counterfeited.
Whoever is no wiser than he ought, he is wise.

211. CCXI. A Ram's Head
You cut the soft neck of Phrixus' woolly mate.
Did he deserve this, the cruel man who gave you a tunic?

212. CCXII. A Dwarf
If you look only at the man's head, you'd think him Hector:
If you see him standing, you'd take him for Astyanax.

213. CCXIII. A Shield
This shield, often vanquished, rarely victorious,
Will be your buckler, a dwarf's.

214. CCXIV. Boy Comic Actors
None in the troupe will play your "Hated Man":
But anyone can be your "Double Deceiver."

215. CCXV. A Buckle
Tell me plainly, buckle, what you do for comic actors
And lyre-singers. "Make them screw at a higher price."

216. CCXVI. A Fowler
The bird is fooled not only by limed reeds but by song,
While the cunning reed grows up in a silent hand.

217. CCXVII. A Hawk
He was the robber of birds: now, the fowler's slave, the same one
Decoys them, and grieves at the birds he catches not for himself.

218. CCXVIII. A Caterer
Say how many you want for dinner and at what price, and don't add
A single word: the dinner is ready for you.

219. CCXIX. An Ox-Heart
Poor pleader, since you write poems that bring back
No money, take the heart you have.

220. CCXX. A Cook
Art alone is not enough for a cook: I don't want
A slavish palate: a cook should have his master's taste.

221. CCXXI. A Grill with Spits
Let the thin tidbit sweat for you on the curved grill;
Let the foaming boar steam on the long spit.

222. CCXXII. A Pastry-Cook
This man's hand will raise for you a thousand sweet
Shapes of his craft: for him alone the thrifty bee toils.

223. CCXXIII. Lard-Cakes
Get up: the baker is already selling boys their breakfasts,
And the crested birds of dawn are crowing on every side.
