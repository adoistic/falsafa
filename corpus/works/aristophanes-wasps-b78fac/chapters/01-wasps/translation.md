SOSIAS
Hey there, what's the matter with you, poor Xanthias?

XANTHIAS
I'm being trained to drop a night watch.

SOSIAS
Then your ribs must owe somebody a big beating.
Do you even know what kind of monster we're guarding?

XANTHIAS
I know — but I'd like to doze off my worries for a bit.

SOSIAS
Take the risk, then, since over my own eyes too
something sweet is being poured out of sleep.

XANTHIAS
Are you really out of your mind, or possessed like a Corybant?

SOSIAS
No — but a kind of sleep from Sabazius has hold of me.

XANTHIAS
Then you and I tend the same Sabazius,
for just now a sleep dropped on me too — a sentry-
Mede laid siege to my eyelids and made me nod.
And just now I saw a marvellous dream.

SOSIAS
So did I, truly — one such as never before.
But tell yours first.

XANTHIAS
I dreamed I saw an eagle,
a very big one, swoop down into the market square,
snatch up a bronze-faced shield in its talons,
and carry it up high into the sky —
and then Cleonymus threw it away.

SOSIAS
So Cleonymus is no different from a riddle.

XANTHIAS
How so?

SOSIAS
Someone will say to his drinking-companions:
"What creature is it that, on land and in the sky
and in the sea alike, threw away its shield?"

XANTHIAS
Oh no — what evil is going to come to me
for having seen a dream like that?

SOSIAS
Don't worry.
Nothing terrible will come of it, by the gods, none.

XANTHIAS
It is a terrible thing, a man throwing away his arms.
But now tell yours.

SOSIAS
Well, it's a big one.
It's about the whole hull of the ship of state.

XANTHIAS
Tell me, then — quick — the very keel of the matter.

SOSIAS
In my first sleep I dreamed that on the Pnyx
a flock of sheep sat in assembly together,
holding staffs and little threadbare cloaks;
and then before these sheep, I dreamed,
a greedy she-whale was haranguing them,
with a voice like a pig being roasted.

XANTHIAS
Ugh.

SOSIAS
What's wrong?

XANTHIAS
Stop, stop, say no more —
that dream stinks horribly of rotten leather.

SOSIAS
Then the foul she-whale took a balance
and weighed out ox-fat.

XANTHIAS
Poor me — she wants
to divide our People into factions.

SOSIAS
Then I dreamed Theorus was sitting near her
on the ground, with the head of a raven.
And then Alcibiades said to me, with his lisp,
"Do you thee? Theoruth hath the head of a waven."

XANTHIAS
That's the right way for Alcibiades to lisp it.

SOSIAS
Isn't it odd, though — Theorus turning
into a raven?

XANTHIAS
Not at all — it's excellent.

SOSIAS
How?

XANTHIAS
How? A man, and then suddenly a raven —
isn't it plain to guess from that
that he'll be carried off from us and go to the ravens — to ruin?

SOSIAS
Then shouldn't I pay two obols to hire you,
seeing how cleverly you play the dream-interpreter?

XANTHIAS
Come now, let me explain the plot to the spectators,
after first telling them just these few things:
expect nothing too grand from us,
and no laughter pilfered from Megara.
For we have no slaves throwing out nuts
from a basket to the audience,
no Heracles cheated of his dinner,
nor any fresh outrage on Euripides;
and not even if Cleon shone, thanks to a stroke of luck,
will we mince up the same man over again.
No — we have a little tale with a point,
not over your own heads, no,
but cleverer than vulgar comedy.
You see, we have a master — that one up there,
asleep on top of the roof, the great man.
He's set the two of us to guard his father,
shut up indoors, so he won't get out the door.
For the father suffers a strange disease,
one no man would ever know or guess at
unless he heard it from us — so make your guesses.
Amynias, son of Pronapus, here says
the man's a dice-addict — but he's talking nonsense.

SOSIAS
By Zeus, no — he's guessing the disease from his own case.

XANTHIAS
No — though "addict-of-" is the start of the trouble.
And here's Sosias telling Derkylos
the man's a drink-addict.

SOSIAS
Not at all, since
that's the disease of honest men.

XANTHIAS
And Nikostratos of Skambonidae says
the man's a sacrifice-addict, or a hospitality-addict.

SOSIAS
By the Dog, Nikostratos, no, not hospitality —
since Philoxenus is a hospitable bugger of a sort.

XANTHIAS
You're all babbling pointlessly — you'll never find it.
If you really want to know, be quiet now.
For I'll tell you the master's disease right away.
He's a trial-addict, like no other man,
in love with it — with judging — and he groans
if he doesn't get to sit on the front bench.
At night he doesn't see a wink of sleep.
And if he does shut his eyes even a speck, even so
his mind flies there, all night, around the water-clock.
And because he's used to holding the voting-pebble,
he gets up clutching three fingers together,
like a man laying incense on the altar at the new moon.
And by Zeus, if he sees written up somewhere
on a door "Demos is beautiful, Pyrilampes' son,"
he goes and writes beside it "the ballot-funnel is beautiful."
The cock that crowed from evening on, he said
was rousing him late, having been bribed
and paid by the men under audit.
Straight from supper he's bawling for his shoes,
then off he goes there, lies down very early,
clinging to the column like a limpet.
And from sheer bad temper he votes the long line — the harsh sentence — for everyone;
and like a bee or a bumblebee he comes home
with wax plastered under his fingernails.
And afraid that he might one day run short of pebbles
to do his judging with, he keeps a whole beach indoors.
So he raves. And the more he's reproached,
the more he judges. So we guard this one,
chaining him in with bolts, so he won't get out.
For his son takes the disease hard.
First he tried by gentle words to coax
and persuade him not to wear the threadbare cloak
nor go out the door — but he wouldn't be persuaded.
Then he washed and purified him — and he wouldn't have it.
After that he treated him with Corybant rites —
and the old man, drum and all, rushed in
to the New Court and went on judging.
And when these rituals were no help,
he sailed across to Aegina, and then seized him
and laid him down by night in the temple of Asclepius —
and the man showed up at the rail at first light.
After that we let him out no more,
but he'd slip away through the drains
and the spouts; and we stuffed up every hole there was
with rags and plugged them tight;
but he, like a jackdaw, knocked in pegs
for himself along the wall, and then leapt out.
So we have spread nets over the whole courtyard
all the way round, and we keep watch.
The old man's name is Philocleon —
yes, by Zeus — and the son's here is Bdelycleon,
a fellow with rather snooty, high-and-mighty ways.

BDELYCLEON
Hey, Xanthias and Sosias — are you asleep?

XANTHIAS
Oh no.

SOSIAS
What is it?

XANTHIAS
Bdelycleon's getting up.

BDELYCLEON
Won't one of you run round here quick?
My father's gone into the kitchen
and is rooting about in there like a mouse. Watch —
see he doesn't slip out through the drain-hole of the basin.
And you, lean against the door.

SOSIAS
Yes, master.

BDELYCLEON
Lord Poseidon, what's that noise in the chimney?
Hey — who are you?

PHILOCLEON
It's me — coming out as smoke.

BDELYCLEON
Smoke? Let me see — from what wood?

PHILOCLEON
Fig-wood.

BDELYCLEON
By Zeus, the sharpest, most acrid of all smokes.
But you won't slip out. Where's the chimney-board?
Get back down. Here, let me put a log on you too.
Now look for some other scheme in there.
But I'm the most wretched man alive — no one more —
the man who'll now be called "son of Smoky."

SOSIAS
He's pushing at the door.

BDELYCLEON
Press hard, then,
well and like a man — I'm coming there too.
And mind the latch, and watch the bar
so he doesn't gnaw the pin right out.

PHILOCLEON
What are you doing? Won't you let me out, you scum,
to go judge? Is Dracontides to get off scot-free?

BDELYCLEON
And you'd take that hard?

PHILOCLEON
Yes — for the god,
when I once consulted him at Delphi, told me
that whenever a defendant escapes me, I'll wither away.

BDELYCLEON
Apollo, avert that oracle!

PHILOCLEON
Come, I beg you, let me out, or I'll burst.

BDELYCLEON
By Poseidon, Philocleon, never.

PHILOCLEON
Then I'll gnaw through the net with my teeth.

BDELYCLEON
But you've no teeth.

PHILOCLEON
Poor wretched me!
How could I kill you? How? Give me a sword,
quick — or a tablet for fixing penalties.

BDELYCLEON
This fellow means to do some great mischief.

PHILOCLEON
By Zeus, not at all — I only want to sell
the donkey, panniers and all —
it's the new moon, you know, market day.

BDELYCLEON
Then couldn't I
sell it myself just as well?

PHILOCLEON
Not the way I would.

BDELYCLEON
By Zeus, better.

PHILOCLEON
Well then, bring the donkey out.

SOSIAS
What an excuse he let down — how slyly —
to get you to send him out!

BDELYCLEON
But he hauled in nothing
that way — I saw what he was scheming.
But I think I'll go in and bring the donkey out myself
so the old man can't even peep through again.
Donkey, why are you crying? Because you'll be sold today?
Walk faster. Why groan, unless you're carrying
some Odysseus?

SOSIAS
But by Zeus, it is carrying one —
look, here's a man slipped under it down below.

BDELYCLEON
What man? Let me see. This here — what was it?
Who in the world are you, fellow, really?

PHILOCLEON
Noman, by Zeus.

BDELYCLEON
Noman, you? From where?

PHILOCLEON
Ithacan, son of Runaway-horse.

BDELYCLEON
Noman — by Zeus, you won't get any joy of it.
Drag him out quick. The filthiest of men,
where he's crept under! He looks to me
most like the foal of a process-server.

PHILOCLEON
If you don't leave me in peace, we'll fight.

BDELYCLEON
And what'll we be fighting about, exactly?

PHILOCLEON
About a donkey's shadow.

BDELYCLEON
You're a sorry sort, far gone in cunning, and reckless.

PHILOCLEON
Me, sorry? By Zeus, no — but you don't know
how good I am right now. Maybe you will, once you've eaten
the paunch of an old juryman.

BDELYCLEON
Shove the donkey and yourself back into the house.

PHILOCLEON
Fellow-jurors! Cleon! Help!

BDELYCLEON
Bawl indoors, with the door shut.
You, push a lot of stones against the door,
and ram the pin back into the bar,
and set the big mortar against the beam
and roll it up, quick.

SOSIAS
Poor wretched me!
Where on earth did this clod fall on me from?

BDELYCLEON
Maybe a mouse threw it at you from above somewhere.

SOSIAS
A mouse? By Zeus, no — it's a juryman of the rafters
here, slipping out from under the tiles.

BDELYCLEON
Oh no, the man's turning into a sparrow.
He'll fly off. Where, where's my net?
Shoo, shoo — back again, shoo! By Zeus, it really would
have been better to guard Scione than this father of mine.

SOSIAS
Come now — since we've scared him off,
and there's no way he can slip past us unseen again,
why don't we sleep just the tiniest scrap?

BDELYCLEON
But you poor fool, in a little while they'll come —
the fellow-jurors — calling out for this man,
my father.

SOSIAS
What do you mean? But it's deep dawn now.

BDELYCLEON
By Zeus, they've got up late this time, then.
For from the middle of the night they always come calling,
carrying lamps and humming old tunes,
honey-sweet songs of Phrynichus the Sidonian,
to call him out.

SOSIAS
Well then, if need be,
we'll soon pelt them with stones.

BDELYCLEON
But you poor fool, that tribe of old men,
if anyone rouses them to anger, is like a wasps' nest.
For they have a sting too, out of the rump,
very sharp, with which they prick; and with a shout
they leap and pelt like sparks of fire.

SOSIAS
Don't worry. If I've got stones,
I'll scatter a whole swarm of jurymen.

CHORUS
March on, step out stoutly. Comias, you're lagging.
By Zeus, you weren't like this before — you were a strap of dog-leather.
But now Charinades is a better walker than you.
Strymodorus of Conthyle, best of fellow-jurors,
is Euergides here anywhere, or Chabes of Phlya?
They're here — what's left of that prime, alas, alas,
of those days of youth when we were together in Byzantium
on garrison duty, you and I; and then, walking about by night,
we filched the bread-woman's kneading-trough without her knowing,
and split it up and boiled our pot-herbs on it.
But let's hurry, men, for today it'll be Laches' turn;
they all say he's got a beehive stuffed with money.
So yesterday Cleon, our guardian, ordered us
to come in good time, bringing three days' worth of bitter anger
against him, to punish him for the wrongs he's done. But come,
let's make haste, men of our years, before day breaks.
Let's go, and with the lamp let's peer all around,
in case some stone in the path does us a mischief.

BOY
Father, oh father, watch out for this mud here.

CHORUS
Pick up a twig from the ground and trim the lamp-wick with it.

BOY
No — I think I'll trim the lamp with this finger.

CHORUS
What got into you, poking the wick with your finger,
and that when oil's scarce, you fool?
It doesn't pinch you, does it, when we have to buy it dear.

BOY
By Zeus, if you correct us again with your knuckles,
we'll put out the lamps and go home by ourselves;
and then, perhaps, robbed of this in the dark,
you'll go stirring up the mud as you walk, like a snipe.

CHORUS
For sure I punish bigger fellows than you.
But here's some muck I seem to be treading in;
and there's no way but that within four days at most
the god must of necessity make rain.
These mushroom-snuffs have crept up on the lamps here, anyway;
and when that happens, it's especially apt to bring rain.
And the crops too, the ones not yet ripe, need
rain to come, and a north wind to breathe on them.

But what's happened to the juror from this house here,
that he doesn't appear out here to join the crowd?
He wasn't a laggard before — no, he'd lead us off,
the first of us, singing Phrynichus; for the man's
a lover of song. But I think, men, we should stand here
and sing to call him out, in case somehow, hearing
my song, for the pleasure of it he'll creep out the door.

CHORUS
Why doesn't the old man show himself at his door, or answer us?
Has he lost his shoes,
or stubbed his toe somewhere
in the dark,
and so his ankle swelled, being an old man?
And maybe he's got a swollen groin.
He was by far the sharpest of us all,
and the only one who wouldn't be persuaded;
but whenever anyone pleaded with him,
he'd bend his head down like this
and say, "You'll boil a stone."

CHORUS
Or maybe, because of yesterday's man, who slipped through us,
deceiving and saying
that he loved Athens and
was the first to denounce the doings in Samos —
maybe because of that he's in pain
and now lies feverish.
For that's the sort of man he is.
But come, good fellow, get up, and don't
eat yourself up so, or be vexed.
For a fat man has come,
one of the traitors over Thrace;
see you pot him for it.
On, boy, on.

BOY
Will you do something for me then, father,
if I ask you for something?

CHORUS
Of course, my little boy. But tell me,
what fine thing do you want me
to buy you? I suppose you'll say
knucklebones, no doubt, boy.

BOY
By Zeus, no — figs, daddy,
they're nicer.

CHORUS
Not a chance,
by Zeus, even if you both hang for it.

BOY
By Zeus, then I won't escort you any more.

CHORUS
For out of this little wage of mine,
the third part, I have to find barley-meal and firewood and relish.
And you ask me for figs!

BOY
Come now, father, if the archon
doesn't seat the court today,
where shall we buy our
lunch? Have you any
good hope for us, or
some sacred Hellespont-passage to find?

CHORUS
Alas, alas, alas, alas,
by Zeus, I don't know myself
where our dinner is to come from.

BOY
Why did you bear me, miserable mother?

CHORUS
So you could give me trouble to feed.

BOY
Then you were a useless ornament, my little pouch.
Alas, alas. There's nothing for us but to groan.

PHILOCLEON
Friends, I've been melting away
this long while, through the chink,
listening to you.
But I just can't —
sing. What shall I do?
I'm watched by these two, since
I've longed this long while to go with you
to the voting-urns
and do some mischief.
But, O Zeus, you who thunder mightily,
either turn me suddenly into smoke,
or into Proxenides, or that son of Sellus,
that lying braggart of a vine-prop.
Dare, lord, to grant me this favour,
pitying my plight; or with your white-hot
thunderbolt burn me to ashes, quick,
and then snatch me up, blow me out,
and plunge me into hot brine-and-vinegar;
or else make me the stone on which
they count out the voting-shells.

CHORUS
Who is it that shuts you in
and bars you behind the door? Tell us —
you'll be speaking to friends.

PHILOCLEON
My own son. But don't shout —
for he happens to be
sleeping just there in front. So lower your pitch.

CHORUS
But on what pretext, you fool, does he want to do this to you?
And what excuse has he?

PHILOCLEON
He won't let me judge, men, nor do any mischief at all,
but is ready to feast me well — and I don't want it.

CHORUS
He dared to gape out that, the foul wretch,
this Demagogue-Cleon-fellow,
just because you say something true
about the ships. For this man would never
have dared to say that
unless he were one of the conspirators.

CHORUS
Well, in view of this it's high time you sought some new device
to let you come down here, unknown to this man.

PHILOCLEON
What could it be? Look for it, you — for I'd do anything;
I'm so itching to make my way through the planking with my voting-shell.

CHORUS
Is there any hole you could dig through from inside,
and then slip out, hidden in rags like crafty Odysseus?

PHILOCLEON
Everything's fenced off, and there's no hole even for a gnat to get through.
You must look for something else: it's no use becoming an opening.

CHORUS
Do you remember when, on campaign, you once filched the spits
and let yourself down the wall quick, when Naxos was taken?

PHILOCLEON
I know — but what of it? That's nothing like this.
For I was young then, and able to steal, and master of myself;
and no one was guarding me, but I could
run away with no fear. Now men under arms
are drawn up along the passages, keeping watch,
and two of them at the very doors
are watching me, holding spits,
as you'd watch a cat that's filched the meat.

CHORUS
But even now, find some scheme
as quick as you can, for it's dawn,
little honey-bee.

PHILOCLEON
The best thing for me, then, is to gnaw through the net.
And may Dictynna, lady of the net, forgive me the net.

CHORUS
That's the act of a man set on his salvation.
Now put your jaw to it.

PHILOCLEON
It's gnawed through, that. But don't shout at all,
let's take care Bdelycleon doesn't notice.

CHORUS
Fear nothing, my good man, nothing.
For if he so much as grunts,
I'll make him bite his
heart and run the race
for his life, so he'll learn
not to trample on
the decrees of the Two Goddesses.
But tie the cord to the window and let yourself down,
fastening yourself, and filling your soul with Diopeithes.

PHILOCLEON
Come now — if those two notice and try to fish me up
and haul me back inside, what will you do? Tell me now.

CHORUS
We'll defend you, calling up our oak-tough spirit, all of us,
so it won't be possible to shut you in. That's what we'll do.

PHILOCLEON
I'll do it then, trusting in you, and — do you hear? — if anything happens to me,
take me up, weep over me, and lay me beneath the courtroom rails.

CHORUS
You'll come to no harm — fear nothing. Come, good man, let yourself down,
take heart, and pray to your fathers' gods.

PHILOCLEON
O lord Lykos, hero and neighbour — for you delight in the very things I do,
in the tears of defendants always, and in their laments;
you came and settled here on purpose, to hear these things,
and you alone of heroes chose to sit beside the weeping man.
Pity and save now your own near neighbour;
and I'll never piss or fart by your reed-fence again.

BDELYCLEON
Hey you, wake up!

XANTHIAS
What's the matter?

BDELYCLEON
A sort of voice has wreathed itself around me.

XANTHIAS
Is the old man slipping out somewhere again?

BDELYCLEON
By Zeus, no — he's lowering himself,
tied to a rope.

XANTHIAS
You scoundrel, what are you doing? You won't get down!
Climb up quick, the other way, and beat him with the branches,
in case, struck with the harvest-wreaths, he'll back water — back his stern off.

PHILOCLEON
Won't you help me, all of you who have lawsuits coming on this year —
Smikythion and Teisiades and Chremon and Pheredeipnus?
When, if not now, will you stand by me, before I'm dragged further in?

CHORUS
Tell me, why do we delay to stir up that bile
which, whenever someone provokes our wasps' nest, we feel?
Now that, now that
sharp-tempered weapon with which we punish,
our sting, is stretched out keen.
Come, throw off your cloaks as quick as you can, children,
and run and shout, and report this to Cleon,
and bid him come
against a man who hates the city
and deserves to die, because
he brings in this proposal,
that we should not try lawsuits.

BDELYCLEON
Good fellows, hear the matter out — but don't keep bawling.

CHORUS
By Zeus, we will — to high heaven.

BDELYCLEON
For I won't let go of this man.

CHORUS
Isn't this terrible — open tyranny?
O city! O Theorus, hated of the gods,
and any other flatterer who stands at our head!

XANTHIAS
Heracles, they've got stings too! Don't you see, master?

BDELYCLEON
The ones they destroyed Philippus, son of Gorgias, with, in a trial.

CHORUS
And with these same we'll destroy you. Wheel round, every man,
this way, and stick your stings out, then fling yourselves at him,
drawn up close, in good order, full of anger and rage,
so he'll know well henceforth what a swarm he's angered.

XANTHIAS
This really is terrible now, by Zeus, if we're to fight;
for I'm afraid of their stings, seeing them.

CHORUS
Let the man go — and if not, I tell you,
you'll be calling the tortoises blessed for their shells.

PHILOCLEON
On then, fellow-jurors, sharp-hearted wasps,
some of you fly at their backsides in your wrath,
others, sting them round the eyes and on their fingers.

BDELYCLEON
Midas and Phryx, help here! And Masyntias!
Lay hold of this man and don't let him go for anyone;
or else you'll get no lunch in heavy fetters.
For I've heard the crackle of many a roasting fig-leaf.

CHORUS
If you don't let this man go, something will get stuck in you.

PHILOCLEON
O Cecrops, hero and lord, in your snake's lower parts a Dracontides,
do you look on while I'm overpowered by barbarian men,
men I taught to weep four to the quart-measure?

CHORUS
Then aren't there many terrible evils in old age?
Clearly. And now these two manhandle their old master
by force, remembering nothing of the old
leather jerkins and sleeveless coats he used to buy them,
and the caps; and how he warmed their feet in winter
so they shouldn't freeze each time. But in these there's not,
not even in their eyes, any shame for the old shoes.

PHILOCLEON
Won't you even now let me go, you vilest of beasts,
nor remember the time I caught you stealing the grapes,
led you to the olive-tree, and flogged you well and like a man,
so all envied you? But you, it seems, were ungrateful.
But let me go, you and you, before my son runs out.

CHORUS
You two will soon pay us a fine penalty for this,
before long — so you'll learn what sort of men they are,
sharp-tempered and just and looking cress.

BDELYCLEON
Hit, hit them, Xanthias — the wasps — away from the house!

XANTHIAS
I'm doing it. But you too — smoke them with a lot of smoke.

SOSIAS
Won't you scram? Won't you go to the ravens? Won't you be off? Hit them with the stick!

XANTHIAS
And you, throw on some Aeschines, son of Sellartius, and smoke them out.
Were we going to drive you off in time, then, after all?

BDELYCLEON
But by Zeus, you wouldn't have escaped them so easily
if they'd happened to gorge on the songs of Philocles.

CHORUS
Isn't this plain, then,
to the poor — that tyranny
has crept up on me in secret,
if you, O wretch of wretches and long-haired Amynias,
keep us off from the laws the city laid down,
with no excuse,
no plausible word,
ruling alone, all by yourself?

BDELYCLEON
Is there any way, without a fight and without that shrill bawling,
we could come to words with one another and to a settlement?

CHORUS
Words — with you, you people-hater, lover of monarchy,
ally of Brasidas, wearer of woollen fringes
and tassels, growing your beard unshorn?

BDELYCLEON
By Zeus, it'd really be better for me to give up my father entirely
than to do battle every day with such great troubles.

CHORUS
But you're not even at the parsley-and-rue stage yet —
for we'll throw that in among the three-quart phrases.
But you feel no pain now; you'll feel it when the prosecutor
pours out these same charges over you and calls you a conspirator.

BDELYCLEON
By the gods, won't you be rid of me at last?
Or am I doomed to flay and be flayed all day long?

CHORUS
Never — not while any part of me is left,
seeing you've set yourself thus at tyranny over us.

BDELYCLEON
How everything is tyranny and conspirators to you,
whether someone brings a charge great or small —
a word I hadn't even heard for fifty years!
But now it's far cheaper than salt-fish,
so its very name is bandied about in the market.
If a man buys gurnard but won't have sprats,
at once the seller of sprats nearby says:
"This man looks like he's marketing with tyranny in mind."
And if he asks for a leek as a relish for his anchovies,
the green-grocer-woman, squinting at him, says:
"Tell me — you ask for a leek? With tyranny in mind,
or do you think Athens grows you relishes for free?"

XANTHIAS
And me too — yesterday a whore, when I came in at midday
and bade her ride me jockey-style, got angry with me
and asked if I was setting up the tyranny of Hippias.

BDELYCLEON
Yes, for this sort of thing is sweet to their ears — that even now I,
because I want my father, freed from these
dawn-haunting, sycophant-ridden, lawsuit-wretched ways,
to live a noble life like Morychus — I'm charged
with doing this as a conspirator and thinking tyrannical thoughts.

PHILOCLEON
And rightly so, by Zeus — for I wouldn't take even birds' milk
in place of the life you're now robbing me of.
I take no joy in skate or eels, but I'd far sooner
eat a little stewed lawsuit served up in a casserole.

BDELYCLEON
By Zeus, yes — you've grown used to delighting in such things.
But if you'll bear with me in silence and learn what I say,
I think I'll teach you that you're all wrong in this.

PHILOCLEON
I'm doing wrong by judging?

BDELYCLEON
Yes — and you're laughed at,
without knowing it, by the very men you all but worship.
You're a slave without realizing it.

PHILOCLEON
Stop talking of slavery,
when I rule over all.

BDELYCLEON
Not you — you serve,
thinking you rule. Come, father, teach us:
what's this honour you have, reaping the fruits of Hellas?

PHILOCLEON
Gladly — and I'm willing to put it to these men as judges.

BDELYCLEON
And so am I.
Now release him, all of you.

PHILOCLEON
And give me a sword too.
For if I'm beaten in the argument by you, I'll fall on the sword.

BDELYCLEON
Tell me — what if — well — you won't abide by the verdict?

PHILOCLEON
May I never drink the wage of the Good Spirit unmixed.

CHORUS
Now the man from our own
wrestling-school must say something
new, so he'll show himself —

BDELYCLEON
Someone bring me out the writing-case here, quick as you can.
But how will he show himself, if you keep cheering him on like that?

CHORUS
Not in the young man's
manner there, in his speaking. For you see how
great is your contest,

if — which heaven forbid — this man
means to beat you.

BDELYCLEON
Well, whatever he says, I'll plainly write it down as notes.

PHILOCLEON
What do you say, then — if this fellow beats me in the argument?

CHORUS
No longer will the throng of old men
be of use, not a whit;
mocked in the streets we'll be
called "olive-branch-bearers," mere husks
of pleadings.

CHORUS
But O you who are about to argue for the whole kingdom
that is ours — now, take heart, and put every tongue to the test.

PHILOCLEON
Right then, straight from the starting-line I'll prove about our power
that it's inferior to no kingdom.
For what is happier or more to be envied now than a juror,
what creature more luxurious or more terrible — and an old man at that?
First, as I creep from bed, men keep watch for me at the courtroom rails,
great men, four cubits tall; and then, the moment I come up,
he thrusts into mine his delicate hand, that has stolen public funds;
and bowing low they beseech me, pouring out piteous voices:
"Pity me, father, I beg you — if you yourself ever pilfered anything
when holding office, or on campaign shopping for your messmates" —
a man who'd never have known I was alive but for my earlier acquittal of him.

BDELYCLEON
Let me have this as a note — about the suppliants.

PHILOCLEON
Then, going in, having been entreated and wiped clean of my anger,
once inside I do none of all the things I promise,
but I listen to them sending out every kind of voice for acquittal.
Come, let me see — what flattery is there a juror can't hear there?
Some lament their poverty and add
woes to what they have, till in his grief he makes them equal to mine;
others tell us tales, others some funny bit of Aesop;
others crack jokes, so I'll laugh and lay aside my wrath.
And if we're not won over by these, then at once he hauls up his little children
by the hand, his daughters and his sons; and I listen,
while they, ducking their heads together, bleat; and then the father, on their behalf,
trembling, beseeches me like a god to acquit him at the audit:
"If you delight in a lamb's voice, take pity on a boy's voice;
or if again you delight in piglets, be persuaded by a daughter's voice."
And then for him we slacken the peg of our anger a little.
Isn't that great power, and a mockery of wealth?

BDELYCLEON
This is the second thing of yours I write down — the mockery of wealth.
And remember the good things you say you have, you who claim to rule Hellas.

PHILOCLEON
When boys are examined for citizenship, it's ours to inspect their privates.
And if Oeagrus comes in as a defendant, he doesn't get off
until he's recited us the finest speech, picked out of his Niobe.
And if a flute-player wins his case, in return for that
he pipes us jurors out as we leave, with mouthpiece on.
And if a father dying leaves an heiress-daughter to someone,
we tell the will to go weep loud and long, head and all,
and the seal-shell so very grandly sitting on the marks,
and give her to whoever wins us over with his pleading.
And we do all this unaccountable — no other office is like it.

BDELYCLEON
Yes, this is the one grand thing of all you've said that I count you blessed in.
But about the heiress's will you do wrong, shelling it open.

PHILOCLEON
What's more, whenever the Council and the People are at a loss
to decide some great matter, they've voted by decree
to hand the wrongdoers over to the jurors.
And then Euathlus and that great shield-throwing Toady-onymus
say they'll never betray us, but will fight on behalf of the masses.
And in the Assembly no one's ever carried a motion, unless
he proposes to dismiss the courts the moment they've judged one case.
And Cleon himself, the great bawler, alone of all doesn't nibble at us,
but keeps us safe in his hand and beats off the flies.
But you've never done your own father any of these things.
No — Theorus, though he's a man no less than Euphemius,
takes the sponge from the basin and blacks our shoes.
Just look from what blessings you're shutting and shutting me out,
the things you said you'd prove were slavery and servitude.

BDELYCLEON
Have your fill of talking — for at any rate you'll stop at last and lay bare
an arse that comes off the worse for its bath despite all the holiness of your office.

PHILOCLEON
And what's sweetest of all of these, which I'd forgotten —
when I go home with my wage, and then, the moment I arrive, all of them at once
greet me for the sake of the silver, and first my daughter
washes me and anoints my feet and, bending down, kisses me,
and "daddy-ing" me, fishes the three-obol piece out with her tongue;
and my little wife, coaxing me, brings me a barley-cake,
and then sits beside me and presses me — "Eat this,
have a bite of this." In these things I delight, and I needn't
look to you and the steward, to see when he'll grudgingly serve up my lunch,
cursing and muttering. But if he doesn't knead it for me quick,
I have this here as a bulwark against ills, a defence against missiles.
And if you won't pour me wine to drink, I've brought in this jug
full of wine — the donkey here — and I tip it up and pour for myself, lying down;
while it, gaping open, brays a great soldierly fart at your wine-cup.
Isn't that great power I wield, no less than Zeus's,
when I hear the very things they say of Zeus?
At any rate, if we make an uproar,
every passer-by says,
"How the court thunders,
O King Zeus!"
And if I lighten, the rich ones cluck
and shit themselves at me,
the very grand ones too.
And you yourself fear me most of all;
by Demeter you fear me — but may I
perish if I fear you.

CHORUS
Never have we heard anyone
speak so clearly,
nor so cleverly.

PHILOCLEON
No — he thought he'd harvest a deserted vineyard so easily.
For he knew well that I'm strongest in this.

CHORUS
How he's gone over everything
and passed nothing by, so that I
grew taller listening,
and thought I myself was judging
in the Isles of the Blest,
delighting in his speech.

PHILOCLEON
How he's writhing already, and isn't himself.

BDELYCLEON
For sure, I'll make you look leather-faced today.

CHORUS
You must weave all sorts of wiles
for your acquittal.
For it's hard to soften my anger
for a young man
who doesn't speak on my side.

CHORUS
In view of this it's high time you sought a good, freshly-cut millstone,
unless you've something to say, able to grind down my wrath.

BDELYCLEON
It's hard, and the task of a clever mind, and greater than belongs to comic poets,
to cure an old disease bred deep in the city.
But, O father, our son of Cronus —

PHILOCLEON
Stop, and don't "father" me.
For unless you teach me quick how I'm a slave,
there's no way you won't die — even if I must keep off sacrificial meat.

BDELYCLEON
Listen now, dear daddy, and unknit your brow a little.
First, reckon roughly — not with pebbles but on your fingers —
the tribute that comes in to us from the cities, all together;
and apart from this the dues separately, and the many one-percents,
court-fees, mines, markets, harbours, rents, and confiscated goods.
The total of these comes to nearly two thousand talents for us.
Now set down from this the year's pay for the jurors,
six thousand of them — never more have settled in the land —
and it comes, I suppose, to a hundred and fifty talents for us.

PHILOCLEON
So the pay we got wasn't even a tenth of the revenue!

BDELYCLEON
By Zeus, no, it wasn't.

PHILOCLEON
And where does the rest of the money go, then?

BDELYCLEON
To these fellows of "I'll-never-betray-the-Athenian-rabble,"
"I'll-always-fight-for-the-masses." For you, father, choose them
to rule over you, taken in by these little phrases.
And then these men take bribes of fifty talents apiece
from the cities, threatening and frightening them like this:
"You'll pay the tribute, or I'll thunder and overturn your city."
And you're content to gnaw the scraps of your own office.
And the allies, when they see the rest of the common herd
sucking at an empty ballot-box and chewing nothing,
think you a Connus's-vote — a worthless thing — while to these men they bring gifts:
jars, wine, rugs, cheese, honey, sesame, cushions,
bowls, fine mantles, garlands, necklaces, drinking-cups, health-and-wealth;
while to you, of all you rule over, after toiling much on land and much at sea,
no one gives so much as a head of garlic for your boiled greens.

PHILOCLEON
By Zeus, no — I had to send to Eucharides for three cloves of garlic myself.
But you're wearing me out, not showing me this slavery itself.

BDELYCLEON
Isn't it great slavery, that all these men should be in office,
themselves and their flatterers drawing pay,
while you're content if someone gives you three obols —
obols you yourself earned with much toil, rowing
and fighting on land and besieging?
And besides this you go about under orders, which throttles me most,
when some catamite youngster, the son of Chaereas,
comes in, striding like this, swaying his body and all soft,
and tells you to come early and in good time to judge — since whoever of you
comes later than the signal won't get the three obols;
while he himself takes his advocate's drachma even if he comes late.
And sharing the spoils with one of his fellow-officials,
if some defendant offers anything, the two of them, putting their heads together,
make a deal; and like men working a two-handled saw, one pulls and the other gives way;
while you gape at the paymaster, and what's going on escapes you.

PHILOCLEON
Is this what they do to me? Oh no — what are you saying? How you stir my bottom up,
and draw my mind round more, and I don't know what on earth you're doing to me.

BDELYCLEON
Consider then how, when you might be rich, and all of you,
you've been somehow hedged round — I don't know how — by these eternal demagogues;
you who rule over more cities than any, from the Black Sea to Sardinia,
get no enjoyment of it but the pittance you carry — and that they drip into you
in wool, little by little, just to keep you alive, like oil.
For they want you to be poor; and I'll tell you why:
so you'll know your tamer, and then, whenever he hisses you on
against one of his enemies, you'll leap on them savagely.
For if they wanted to provide a living for the People, it would be easy.
There are a thousand cities that now pay us tribute;
if someone ordered each of them to feed twenty men,
two myriads of common folk would live in clover —
in hares of all kinds and garlands and beestings,
enjoying things worthy of their land and of the trophy at Marathon.
But as it is, you go like olive-pickers, trailing after the man with the pay.

PHILOCLEON
Oh, what's happening to me? A numbness is pouring down over my hand,
and I can't hold the sword, but I'm growing soft already.

BDELYCLEON
But whenever they themselves are afraid, they offer you Euboea,
and undertake to provide grain, fifty bushels a head;
yet they never gave it to you, except the other day five bushels —
and even that you got barley by the quart, with difficulty, after defending yourself on a charge of being an alien.
That's why I always shut you up, wanting
to feed you myself, and not have these men
mock you with their loud-mouthing.
And now I'm simply willing to provide
whatever you want,
except the paymaster's milk to drink.

CHORUS
He was a wise man, surely, who said:
"Don't judge until you've heard the tale from both sides."
For now you seem to me to win by far;
so I slacken my anger already and throw down my staffs.
But O fellow-reveller of our own age,

CHORUS

nor a man too stiff and stubborn.
Would that I had some guardian or kinsman
to give me such advice!
But to you now some god, present and manifest,
is helping in your affairs,
and is plainly doing you good;
and you, since he's here, receive it.

BDELYCLEON
And what's more, I'll feed him, providing
everything fitting for an old man: gruel
to lick up, a soft cloak, a fleecy wrap,
a whore to rub his prick
and his loins.
But that he's silent and doesn't grunt a word —
that I can't take.

CHORUS
He's come to his senses about the things

the things he wouldn't be persuaded of when you bade him.
But now perhaps he's persuaded by your words,
and is wise, after all, changing
his ways for the future,
and obeying you.

PHILOCLEON
Alas, alas for me!

BDELYCLEON
You there, why are you wailing?

PHILOCLEON
Don't promise me any of these things.
Those are what I love — there would I be,
where the herald says, "Who hasn't voted? Let him stand up."
And I'd stand at the ballot-funnels,
the last of those voting.
Hurry, my soul. Where's my soul?
Make way, O shade. By Heracles,
may I never now, among the jurors,
catch Cleon thieving.

BDELYCLEON
Come, father, by the gods, be persuaded by me.

PHILOCLEON
What shall I be persuaded of? Say what you want, except one thing.

BDELYCLEON
What thing? Let me see.

PHILOCLEON
Not to judge. That
Hades will decide before I'm persuaded of it.

BDELYCLEON
Well then, since you take such pleasure in doing this,
don't go there any more, but here,
stay at home and judge the household slaves.

PHILOCLEON
About what? You're talking nonsense.

BDELYCLEON
The very things that are done there:
that the maidservant opened the door secretly —
on her you'll vote a single penalty, just one.
And anyway you used to do this there every time.
And this part's reasonable too: if it's sunny
at dawn, you'll hold court in the sun;
and if it snows, sitting by the fire;
if it rains, you'll go in; and if you wake up at midday,
no presiding magistrate will shut you out at the rail.

PHILOCLEON
This pleases me.

BDELYCLEON
And besides, if anyone
makes a long speech, you won't have to wait hungry,
gnawing at yourself and at the defendant.

PHILOCLEON
But how will I be able to judge well, discerning,
as before, while still chewing on the matter?

BDELYCLEON
Far better. For there's a saying, this one,
that the jurors, when the witnesses lie,
barely make out the matter, chewing it over.

PHILOCLEON
You persuade me round. But you still haven't told me
where I'll get the pay.

BDELYCLEON
From me.

PHILOCLEON
Good —
since I'll get it for myself, not along with another.
For Lysistratus the mocker did me a most shameful turn.
Taking a drachma with me the other day,
he went and changed it among the fishmongers,
and then put three mullet-scales into my hand;
and I gulped them down, thinking I'd got obols;
and then, disgusted at the smell, I spat them out
and dragged him off to law.

BDELYCLEON
And what did he say to that?

PHILOCLEON
What? He said I had the gut of a cock —
"You'll soon be digesting silver," he said.

BDELYCLEON
See how much you'll gain by this too, then.

PHILOCLEON
No small thing. But do what you mean to.

BDELYCLEON
Wait now — I'll come bringing these things.

PHILOCLEON
See the thing — how the oracles come true!
For I'd heard that one day the Athenians
would try their cases at their own houses,
and every man would build in his porch
a tiny little courtroom of his own,
like a shrine of Hecate, everywhere before the doors.

BDELYCLEON
There, what more will you say? See, I bring everything
I said, and much more besides.
Here's a chamber-pot, if you need to piss,
hung up beside you, near, on the peg.

PHILOCLEON
A clever thing, and fitting for an old man —
you've found, in truth, a remedy for the strangury.

BDELYCLEON
And here's a fire too; and a lentil-soup stands ready
to sup, if you need anything.

PHILOCLEON
That's clever too;
for even if I'm feverish, I'll still get my pay.
Staying here, I'll sup my lentil-soup.
But why have you brought the bird out to me?

BDELYCLEON
So that, if you fall asleep while someone's pleading,
it can crow from above and wake you up.

PHILOCLEON
One thing more I long for; the rest pleases me.

BDELYCLEON
What's that?

PHILOCLEON
If only you could bring out the hero-shrine of Lykos.

BDELYCLEON
Here it is — and here's the lord himself.

PHILOCLEON
O master hero, how grim you are to look at!

BDELYCLEON
Just like Cleonymus appears to us.

PHILOCLEON
At any rate, hero though he is, he too has no arms.

BDELYCLEON
If you'd sat down quicker, I'd have called the case quicker.

PHILOCLEON
Call it now — I've been sitting a long while.

BDELYCLEON
Come now, what case shall I bring in first for him?
What mischief has someone in the house done?
Thratta, scorching the pot the other day —

PHILOCLEON
Hold on, you! How nearly you ruined me!
You mean to call the case without a railing,
the first of our holy things to appear?

BDELYCLEON
By Zeus, it's not here.

PHILOCLEON
Then I'll run and
fetch it myself this instant from inside.

BDELYCLEON

SOSIAS

SOSIAS
That's what I'll do.

PHILOCLEON
No — but so that,

There.

BDELYCLEON

Whew, whew!

PHILOCLEON
This one here, I think, will be shitting himself today.

BDELYCLEON
Won't you ever stop being harsh and peevish,
and that toward defendants — but bite down hard?
Get up, make your defence. Why are you silent? Speak.

PHILOCLEON
But it seems this one has nothing to say.

BDELYCLEON
No — I think he's suffered the very thing
Thucydides once suffered as a defendant:
he became suddenly stricken in the jaws.
Get out of the way. For I'll make the defence.
It's hard, gentlemen, to answer for a dog
who's been slandered — but I'll speak all the same.
For he's a good dog, and he chases off the wolves.

PHILOCLEON
A thief, rather, this one — and a conspirator.

BDELYCLEON
By Zeus, no — he's the best of the dogs there are now,
able to stand guard over many sheep.

PHILOCLEON
What's the use, if he eats up the cheese?

BDELYCLEON
What use? He fights in front for you and guards the door
and is best in all else. And if he did pilfer,
forgive him — for he doesn't know how to play the lyre.

PHILOCLEON
I'd wish he didn't even know his letters,
so he couldn't write up our account, the rogue.

BDELYCLEON
Hear my witnesses, good sir.
Get up, Cheese-grater, and speak out loud.
For you happened to be the housekeeper. Answer clearly:
did you, or did you not, grate down for the soldiers what you took?
She says she grated it.

PHILOCLEON
By Zeus, but she's lying.

BDELYCLEON
My good sir, pity the wretched.
For this Labes here eats up necks
and fish-bones, and never stays in one place.
The other's only good for guarding the house.
For staying put, he begs his share
of whatever anyone brings inside; and if he doesn't get it, he bites.

PHILOCLEON
Ugh. What's this trouble that's softening me?
Some evil is creeping round me, and I'm being won over.

BDELYCLEON
Come, I beg you — pity him, father,
and don't destroy him. Where are the children?
Come up, you wretched, whining things,

PHILOCLEON
Get down, get down, get down, get down!

BDELYCLEON
I'll get down.
And yet this "get down" has deceived
a great many. But still, I'll get down.

PHILOCLEON
Go to the ravens! What a no-good thing this supping is!
For I just now wept, against my own judgment —
for no reason at all, except that I was full of lentil-soup.

BDELYCLEON
So isn't he acquitted, then?

PHILOCLEON
It's hard to know.

BDELYCLEON
Come, dear daddy, turn to the better course.
Take this voting-pebble and to the second urn,
with eyes shut, dash past it, father, and acquit.

PHILOCLEON
No, indeed — for I don't know how to play the lyre.

BDELYCLEON
Here now, let me lead you round this way the quickest.

PHILOCLEON
Is this the first one?

BDELYCLEON
It is.

PHILOCLEON
Then in it goes, here.

BDELYCLEON
He's been deceived and has acquitted against his will.
Come, let me empty them out.

PHILOCLEON
How have we done in the contest?

BDELYCLEON
It looks like it'll show. You've got off, Labes!
Father, father, what's the matter? Oh no — where's water?
Lift yourself up.

PHILOCLEON
Just tell me this:
did he really get off?

BDELYCLEON
By Zeus, yes.

PHILOCLEON
Then I'm nothing.

BDELYCLEON
Don't take it to heart, good sir — but get up.

PHILOCLEON
How then shall I bear this on my conscience,
having acquitted a defendant? What ever will become of me?
But, O greatly honoured gods, forgive me;
for I did it unwillingly, and not from my own nature.

BDELYCLEON
And don't be vexed at all. For I, father,
will keep you well, taking you everywhere with me,
to dinner, to drinking-parties, to the festival,
so you'll spend the rest of your time pleasantly;
and Hyperbolus won't be gaping with deceit at you.
But let's go in.

PHILOCLEON
This now, then, if you think best.

CHORUS
Go your ways rejoicing, wherever you wish.
But you, meanwhile, O countless myriads,
take care that the good things about to be spoken
don't fall feebly to the ground.
For that is the part of dull spectators
to suffer, and not befitting you.

CHORUS
Now again, people, pay attention, if you love anything that's clean.
For the poet now wishes to find fault with the audience.
For he says he's been wronged first, after doing them much good —
partly not openly, but secretly helping other poets,
imitating the prophetic art and cunning of Eurycles,
slipping into others' bellies to pour out much comic stuff;
and after that, openly in his own right, taking the risk,
guiding the mouths not of others' Muses but of his own.
And raised high, and honoured as no one ever was among you,
he says he didn't, puffed up, fail to finish, nor swell his pride,
nor go reveling round the wrestling-schools to seduce; nor, if some lover
who hated to see his boyfriend lampooned came hurrying to him,
did he ever consent to anyone, he says, having a fair-minded resolve,
so that he might not make the Muses he uses into procuresses.
Nor, when he first began to produce, did he attack mere men,
but with a Heracles-like wrath he set on the greatest,
boldly grappling from the very start with the saw-toothed one himself,
from whose eyes flashed the most terrible rays of the Bitch-star,
and round his head a hundred heads of accursed flatterers licked,
and he had the voice of a torrent giving birth to destruction,
the smell of a seal, the unwashed balls of a Lamia, a camel's arse.
Seeing such a monster, he says he wasn't afraid and didn't take a bribe,
but on your behalf he still fights even now; and he says that after him
last year he set upon the agues and the fevers,
which throttled your fathers by night and choked your grandfathers,
and lying down on the beds of the quiet, unmeddlesome among you
glued together affidavits and summonses and depositions,
so that many, in terror, leapt up and ran to the polemarch.
Though you found such a guardian-spirit and purifier of this land,
last year you betrayed him when he sowed the freshest new ideas,
which, through your failing to understand them clearly, you made fruitless.
And yet, pouring many libations over many offerings, he swears by Dionysus
that no one ever heard better comic verses than these.
So this is a disgrace to you, who didn't grasp it on the spot;
but the poet is reckoned none the worse among the wise,
if, outstripping his rivals, he wore out his fancy.

CHORUS
But henceforth, you blessed people,
love and cherish more
the poets who seek
to say and discover something new,
and keep their conceptions safe,
and store them up in your chests
along with the apples. And if you do this,
all year long your clothes
will smell of cleverness.

CHORUS
O we who once were brave in the dances,
brave in battles,
and in this one thing alone, the bravest of men;
that was once, before these things; but now it's
gone, and these hairs of ours,
whiter than a swan's, bloom upon us.
But even from these remnants
we must get youthful strength;
for I think my own
old age is better than the curls
of many young men, and their figure
and their wide-arsed ways.

CHORUS
If any of you spectators, seeing my appearance,
then wonders at the sight of me wasp-waisted in the middle —
what the meaning of our sting is —
I'll easily explain, even to one who was unmusical before.
We are the men who have this rump-appendage,
the only true-born native Attic men,
a most manly race, and we did this city the most service
in battles, when the barbarian came,
smoking the whole city with fire and burning it,
eager to take by force our wasps' nests.
For at once, running out with spear, with shield,
we fought them, having drunk the wine of sharp courage,
man standing by man, gnawing our lips in rage;
and for the arrows you couldn't see the sky.
But still, with the gods' help, we pushed them off toward evening.
For an owl flew over our army before we fought;
and then we followed, harpooning them tuna-style into their baggy trousers,
and they fled, stung in the jaws and the eyebrows;
so that among the barbarians everywhere, even now,
nothing's called more manly than an Attic wasp.

CHORUS
Was I not terrible then, fearing nothing at all,
and did I not overthrow
the enemy, sailing there with the triremes?
For it was no concern of ours then
to make a fine speech,
nor to lay a sycophant's information against anyone,
but only who'd be the best
oarsman. And so, having taken many
cities of the Medes,
we're most to blame for the tribute's being
brought here — which the younger men steal.

CHORUS
Looking at us in many ways, you'll find us in all
most like wasps in our ways and in our manner of life.
For first, there's no creature, once provoked,
more sharp-tempered than we, nor more peevish;
and then in all else we contrive things like wasps.
For gathering in swarms, just like wasps' nests,
some of us judge where the archon is, some by the Eleven,
some in the Odeon, some packed close against the walls,
nodding toward the ground, scarcely
stirring, like the grubs in their cells.
And as for the rest of our way of life, we're most resourceful;
for we sting every man and provide ourselves a living.
But there are drones sitting among us,
without a sting, who, staying put, eat up
the toil of our tribute, not labouring at all.
And this is most painful to us — if some man who's never served
swallows down our pay, having taken up neither
oar nor spear nor blister for this land.
But it seems to me that henceforth, in short, every citizen
who hasn't the sting shouldn't draw the three obols.

BDELYCLEON
"Wealth and might
to Cleitagora and me,
together with the Thessalians—"

PHILOCLEON
You've boasted a lot, and so have I.

BDELYCLEON
This much you understand well enough.
But let's see how we're to go to dinner at Philoctemon's.
Boy, boy, Chrysus, pack the dinner for us,
so that for once after a long while we may get drunk.

PHILOCLEON
No, no.
Drinking's a bad thing. For from wine come
door-smashing and brawling and pelting with stones,
and then paying out money out of your hangover.

BDELYCLEON
Not if you're in the company of fine, decent men.
For either they begged off the injured party for you,
or you yourself told some witty story,
an Aesop-joke or a Sybarite one,
which you learned at the party; and then you turned
the matter to a laugh, so the man let you go and went off.

PHILOCLEON
Then I must learn lots of these stories,
if I'm to pay no penalty when I do some mischief.

BDELYCLEON
Come now, let's go. Let nothing hold us back.

CHORUS
Many a time I've thought myself
naturally clever, and never dull;
but Amynias, son of Sellus, of the Top-Knot clan,
this one whom I once saw dining
on apple and pomegranate
with Leogoras —
for he's as hungry as Antiphon;
but he went off as ambassador to Pharsalus, and there,
alone among the lonely
Penestae of the
Thessalians, was himself a serf inferior to none.

CHORUS
O blessed Automenes, how we count you blessed,
because you've begotten the most skilled of children:
first, a man dear to all and most clever,
the finest lyre-singer, whom grace attended;
and another, an actor, marvellously clever;
and then Ariphrades, by far the most ingenious,
who once swore that, having learned it from no one,
but from his clever nature, self-taught,
he learned to ply his tongue in the brothels every time he goes in.

CHORUS

when Cleon harried me, pressing on,
and galled me with vile abuse; and then, when I was being flayed,
the outsiders laughed to see me bawling loud,
caring nothing for me, but only wanting to know
whether, hard-pressed, I'd let fall some little joke.
Seeing this, I played the ape a little;
and then now the vine-prop has deceived the vine.

Up and out of the way!
Someone among those following
behind me is going to howl;
for, you wretches, if you don't clear off,
with this torch here
I'll make you into roasted kippers.

A FELLOW-GUEST
For sure, you'll pay us all the penalty for this
tomorrow, young blood though you are.
For we'll come in a crowd to summon you.

PHILOCLEON
Hey-ho, ha-ha — summon, will you!
How old-fashioned of you! Don't you know
that I can't bear even to hear
of lawsuits? Ugh, ugh!
This is what pleases me — away with the voting-urns!
Won't he be off? Where's our
juryman? Out of the way.
Come up here, my little golden cockchafer,
take hold of this rope with your hand.
Hold on; but mind, the rope's rotten —
still, it doesn't mind being rubbed.
You see how cleverly I filched you away
when you were just about to play the Lesbian for the guests;
so pay this prick of mine a favour for it.
But you won't pay it, or do me the service, I know;
you'll cheat me and gape a great laugh at it,
for you've done it to many others already.
But if you'll not be a bad woman now,
when my son dies I'll buy you free
and keep you as my mistress, little piglet.
As it is, I'm not master of my own money,
for I'm young and watched very closely;
my little sonny keeps an eye on me, and he's peevish
and a cumin-splitting, cress-scraping skinflint besides.
So he's afraid for me, that I'll go to ruin;
for he has no father but me.
And here he is himself, running, it seems, at you and me.
But quick as you can, stand still, taking these torches,
so I can mock him roundly,
as he did me before the Mysteries.

BDELYCLEON
Hey you, you there, you smoke-dried old lecher and pig-squeezer,
you seem to long and lust for a coffin ripe and ready.
By Apollo, you won't get away with doing this.

PHILOCLEON
How you'd love to eat a lawsuit dressed in vinegar!

BDELYCLEON
Isn't it dreadful that you mock me, having filched
the flute-girl from the fellow-guests?

PHILOCLEON
What flute-girl?
What's this nonsense, as if you'd fallen off a tomb?

BDELYCLEON
By Zeus, this is your Dardanis, somewhere about.

PHILOCLEON
No — it's a torch burning in the market for the gods.

BDELYCLEON
This a torch?

PHILOCLEON
A torch indeed. Don't you see it's branded?

BDELYCLEON
And what's this black part of it in the middle?

PHILOCLEON
That's the pitch coming out as it burns.

BDELYCLEON
And isn't this behind here an arse?

PHILOCLEON
No — that's a knot of the torch sticking out.

BDELYCLEON
What do you mean, you? What sort of knot? Come here, you!

PHILOCLEON
Ah, ah — what are you going to do?

BDELYCLEON
Take her and lead her off,
taking her from you, and reckoning you rotten
and able to do nothing.

PHILOCLEON
Listen to me now.
At Olympia, when I was a spectator there,
Ephoudion fought Ascondas finely,
old man though he was; and then, with a blow of his fist,
the older man knocked down the younger.
In view of this, watch you don't get black eyes.

BDELYCLEON
By Zeus, you've learned your Olympia by heart.

A BREAD-WOMAN
Come, stand by me, I beg you, by the gods.
For this is the man who ruined me,
beating me with the torch, and knocked out from here
ten obols' worth of loaves and four extra ones besides.

BDELYCLEON
See what you've done? Now there'll be more troubles and lawsuits
to deal with because of your wine.

PHILOCLEON
Not at all, since
some clever stories will settle them;
so I know I'll be reconciled with this one.

BREAD-WOMAN
By the Two Goddesses, you won't get away with it —
ruining the wares of Myrtia, daughter of Ancylion
and Sostrate — like this.

PHILOCLEON
Listen, woman; I want to tell you
a charming tale.

BREAD-WOMAN
By Zeus, not to me, my good man.

PHILOCLEON
Aesop, walking home from dinner one evening,
had a bold, drunken bitch barking at him.
And then he said, "O bitch, bitch,
if, by Zeus, instead of that wicked tongue
you'd buy yourself some wheat somewhere, I'd think you had sense."

BREAD-WOMAN
And you mock me? I summon you, whoever you are,
before the market-inspectors, for damage to my wares,
with Chaerephon here as my witness.

PHILOCLEON
By Zeus, no — listen, if you'll think I say something.
Lasus and Simonides once competed against each other;
then Lasus said, "It's little I care."

BREAD-WOMAN
Really, you?

PHILOCLEON
And you, Chaerephon — you seem fit
to be witness for a woman, sallow-faced,
an Ino hanging at the feet of Euripides.

BDELYCLEON
Here comes another one, it seems,
to summon you; he's got a witness, anyway.

A PLAINTIFF
Oh, wretched me! I summon you, old man,

BDELYCLEON
Assault? No, no, don't summon him, by the gods;
for I'll pay you a penalty on his behalf,
whatever you fix, and I'll be grateful besides.

PHILOCLEON
I myself will gladly be reconciled with him;
for I admit I struck and pelted him.
But come here. Will you leave it to me,
how much silver I should pay for the matter
to be your friend henceforth, or will you fix it?

PLAINTIFF
You say. For I want no lawsuits and no troubles.

PHILOCLEON
A man of Sybaris fell out of his chariot,
and somehow cracked his head very badly indeed;
for he happened to be no skilled hand at horsemanship.
And then a friend of his stood by and said to him:
"Let each man do the craft he knows."
So you too — run off to Pittalus's surgery.

BDELYCLEON
This too is just like the rest of your ways.

PLAINTIFF
Well, you at least remember what he answered.

PHILOCLEON
Listen, don't run off. In Sybaris a woman once
smashed a jar.

PLAINTIFF
I call you to witness this.

PHILOCLEON
So the jar, having a witness, called one to attest;
then the Sybarite woman said: "If, by the Maiden,
you'd let this witnessing go and quick
buy yourself a bandage, you'd have more sense."

PLAINTIFF
Go on insulting, until the magistrate calls the case.

BDELYCLEON
By Demeter, you won't stay here any longer,
but I'll pick you up and carry you off —

PHILOCLEON
What are you doing?

BDELYCLEON
What am I doing?
I'm carrying you inside from here; or else soon
there'll be too few summoners for those you summon.

PHILOCLEON
The Delphians once, against Aesop —

BDELYCLEON

PHILOCLEON

BDELYCLEON

CHORUS

how he's changed
from dry ways and a dry life;
and now, having learned otherwise,
he'll surely shift greatly
toward the luxurious and the soft.
But perhaps he won't want to;
for it's hard to stand off
from a nature one always has.

keeping company with others' minds,
they changed their ways.

CHORUS
With much praise from me
and from the right-thinking,
having won it, the son of Philocleon

so agreeable, nor was I

PHILOCLEON

XANTHIAS

PHILOCLEON

XANTHIAS

PHILOCLEON

XANTHIAS

PHILOCLEON

XANTHIAS

PHILOCLEON

PHILOCLEON
