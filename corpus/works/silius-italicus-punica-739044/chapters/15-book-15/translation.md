15. But a new care was wearing at the senate of Romulus,
who should take up against the proud enemy the trembling peoples
and the Iberian war, their resources worn down. The twin Scipios
lie low before the haughty foe, brothers, the warlike hearts of Mars.
Hence a fear, lest now the Tartessian land should yield
to Tyrian laws and dread the wars drawn nearer.
The anxious throng of fathers, mournful, looks round in their shaken
command for remedies and prays the gods
for a leader who would dare to succeed to the mangled camp.

The throng deters the young man, who longs to make atonement
to the spirits of his father and his uncle, terrified by the grim grief
and reckoning his years, his kindred blood:
if he should seek the ill-fated nation, he must fight it out
amid the tombs of his own, against him who broke the arms of the two,
who broke the counsels of the leaders, and who blazes with a better War-god:
nor is it easy to undertake vast wars with tender arms
and to demand the command in a raw and untried age.

These cares the young man, sitting beneath the green shade
of a laurel at the edge of the buildings, was turning over in his breast,
when suddenly there stand by him, gliding down on the right and the left
through the air, an image not a little greater than mortal —
on this side Virtue, on that, Pleasure hostile to Virtue.
The one breathed from her head an Achaemenian fragrance,
her ambrosial locks spread out, and shining in a robe
where she had washed Tyrian purple with tawny gold.
Beauty sought by the needle was on her brow, and her wanton
eyes cast frequent flames with their shifting motion.
The other's bearing was unlike: her brow rough and her hair
never changed by being composed; a standing countenance, and in face
and gait nearer to a man, and of glad modesty,
tall upon her shoulders she shone in the thread of a snow-white robe.

Then Pleasure, trusting in her promises, takes up first:
"What madness is this, boy, to consume unworthily in war
the flower of your age? Have Cannae and the Maeonian lake,
heavier than the Stygian marsh, and the Padus passed from your mind?
To what end at last will you provoke the fates by warring?
Do you even prepare to attempt the Atlantic realms
and the Sidonian homes? I warn you, cease to contend
with perils and to fling your head against the arms-resounding storm.
Unless you flee these ways, savage Virtue will bid you
flit through the midst of battle-lines and the midst of fires.
She it was who thrust your father and your uncle, who, prodigal, thrust Paulus,
who thrust the Decii, down to the Stygian waves of Erebus,
while she holds out to the ashes a title and to the tombs names to be remembered,
to a shade that will not feel what it achieved.
But if you accompany me, boy, the age of your allotted time
shall now run down for you on no harsh path.
Never shall the trumpet break your trembling slumbers,
you shall not try the Arctic ice, nor the ardour of raging
Cancer, nor tables often laid out on bloody
turf. Harsh thirst shall be absent, and the swallowing
of dust beneath the helmet, and the lesser share of toils.
But white days and serene hours shall run for you,
and it shall be granted to hope for old age amid soft living.
How many things has the god himself begotten for glad uses,
and given to man with full hand the goods of joy!
And the same god, a model of a gentle life to mortals,
holds undisturbed ease with a placid mind.
I am she who joined Venus to Anchises by the waters
of Simois, whence the founder of your race was born to you.
I am she who often turned the father of the gods above,
now into the shape of a bird, now into the horns of a fierce bull.
Turn your ears this way. The age of mortals runs on,
nor is it granted to be born twice: the hour flees, and the Tartarean
torrent snatches it away, and refuses to bear with itself beneath the shades
whatever has pleased the mind. Who, at his last light,
has not groaned, too late, that he sent away my hours?"

After she fell silent and an end was set to her words,
then Virtue: "Into what snares," she said, "do you lure the young man
in his flowering years, into the darknesses of life,
to whom reason and the heavenly seeds of a great mind
have been granted by the gift of the gods? As far as the high
heaven-dwellers excel mortals, by so much do those excel
all living creatures. For Nature herself bestowed
these lesser gods upon the lands; but by a fixed bond
she condemned the degenerate souls to the Avernian shadows.
But to those who keep the birthright of their ethereal seed,
the gate of heaven lies open. Why should I recall the son of Amphitryon,
who tamed all things? Why him for whom, when Liber after the Seres and the Indians
bore back his standards from the captive East,
the Caucasian tigers drew the chariot through the towns?
Why should I recall the Ledaean brothers, sighed for by sailors
in great peril, and your own Quirinus?
Do you not see how the god raised men's lofty faces
to the stars and shaped their countenances sublime,
when he had laid the cattle and the race of birds and the shapes of beasts
everywhere prostrate upon the sluggish and unsightly belly?
The race of men, born for glory, is happy in glory,
if it take the gifts of the gods. Come, look here a little
(nor will I seek far back): Rome lately unequal
to threatening Fidenae and content to grow by an asylum,
by what right hands she has raised herself: look too, far and wide,
what cities once flourishing in luxury Pleasure has overthrown.
For neither the anger of the gods so much, nor weapons, nor enemies
harm so much, Pleasure, as you alone, gliding into men's souls.
Foul Drunkenness is your companion, yours Luxury, and ever
flitting about you on black wings, Infamy.
With me are Honour and Praises and Glory with glad face
and Distinction and Victory of one colour with snow-white wings.
Triumph, girt with laurel, leads me to the stars.
My house is chaste, and my Penates stand on a lofty hill:
a steep path leads up the rocky slope.
Harsh at the beginning (for it is not my way to deceive),
labour attends the way. He who would enter must strive,
nor must he reckon as goods those things which faithless Chance
has power to give and the same to snatch away. Soon, lofty from on high,
you shall see beneath you the race of men. All things, on the contrary,
remain to be endured, beyond what coaxing Pleasure promises.
Flung on hard straw beneath the stars you shall suffer
sleepless nights and shall master cold and hunger.
You too, a cultivator of justice, whatever you take in hand,
shall judge the gods to stand as witnesses of your deeds.
Then, as often as the perils of your country and of affairs demand it,
you shall bear arms first, first you shall raise yourself onto the hostile
walls, nor be conquered in mind by steel or by gold.
Hence I shall give you not garments tainted with Tyrian
purple, nor the gift, unworthy of a man, of fragrant balsam,
but I shall give you this: to overcome by your hand the one
who now wearies your empire with savage war,
and to lay the proud laurel in the lap of Jupiter, the Carthaginians cut down."

When Virtue had sung these things from her hallowed breast,
she turned the young man, glad at the examples and approving with his face
what he had heard. But indignant Pleasure
did not restrain her words. "I delay you no longer," she cried.
"They will come, they will come one day, my times,
when Rome, made docile in a great contest, shall serve
my commands, and honour shall be given to me alone."
So, shaking her head, she lifted herself into the black clouds.

But the young man, full of the warnings, plans mighty things
in his heart and grows warm with love of the Virtue he was bidden to.
He seeks the lofty rostra, when none wills the fierce wars,
and demands the heavy duties of doubtful Mars.
The minds of all are stirred: some believe the eyes of the father,
some the grim countenance of the uncle, grow young again.
But although they are roused, yet silently a sickly terror
of peril creeps over their breasts, and, fearing, they weigh
the mass of the war, and anxious favour numbers his years.

And while the crowd reviews these things with confused murmur,
behold, athwart the sky, a serpent, gleaming with scales rough
with gold among the clouds and seen amid the iron,
was beheld to radiate through the air with a burning furrow,
and, where the region tends toward the shore of heaven-bearing Atlas,
to glide on with the resounding pole. Twice and thrice the father
added a flashing thunderbolt to the augury, and far and wide
the wandering thunders crashed through the suddenly stirred world.
Then indeed they bid him take up arms and on their knees salute,
bowed down, the augury: that he should go where it was clear
the gods led, and where with his father's sign he pointed out the beginning.

Eagerly the companions of his fortunes and the ministers of war
gather themselves and beg to share the keen
labours, and it is in place of praises to be under the same arms.
Then a new fleet goes down into the blue sea.
Ausonia goes as companion and crosses over into the Iberian lands:
as, when Corus has flung savage battles upon the straits,
he hurls himself high over the Isthmus with a curved wave
and, rushing with foaming flood over the groaning rocks,
mingles the Ionian sea with the Aegean. Lofty for arms
Scipio darts forth and, standing on the prow's front, speaks:
"God mighty with the trident, whose deep seas we begin
to traverse, if I prepare just things, grant my fleet
to run down them, father, and scorn not to aid our labours.
Through the sea I bear a holy war." Then a light breeze,
breathing favourable, drives on the sails with its blast.
And now the nimble ships, where the Tyrrhenian blue sounds,
passed by the Ausonian side, and with swift prows
cross the Ligurian shores. Hence from the deep flood
they see, far off, a land bursting into the stars,
the airy Alps. Walls meet them, founded by the Greeks,
of Massilia: this city, girt with proud peoples,
when the barbarous neighbour terrifies with savage rite,
the Phocaean keeps the custom and culture and dress
of her ancient fatherland, a guest among arms-bearing nations.
Hence the Ausonian leader picks his way through the winding
bends of the sea curved by the flood. A hill appears
lofty with its wooded summit, and the forests of the Pyrenees
flee into the clouds; then Emporiae, and through their ancient
risings the crowd of the Greeks, then Tarraco, host to Bacchus.
They settle in the harbour. Safe in the closed flood
the ships stand, and the toil and the terror of the deep are laid aside.

Night, most peaceful, had given slumbers like to death:
the image of his father seemed to stand before the man's face
and, troubled at the sight, to warn him with this voice:
"Son, once the salvation of your begetter, son, your parent's
glory even after death, the land, dire creatress of wars,
must be laid waste by you, and the proud leaders of Libya
mastered by slaughter and cautious valour,
who now spread their columns in three camps.
If it should please you to join hand to hand, and the lines, summoned
from here and there, should meet, who would have strength to bear the men
rushing on with their threefold mass? Stand off from the doubtful
labour, but, not sluggish, take up the better course.
A city is inhabited, founded long ago by an ancient Teucer,
by name Carthage: a Tyrian dweller holds the walls.
As it is the chief of Libya's own, so memorable in the Iberian lands
is this head. None will vie with it in the wealth of gold,
none in harbour or lofty site, none in the dowries of a rich
field or in nimble vigour for forging weapons.
Invade this city, son, while its leaders are turned away:
no battle-line will have prepared so much of fame or of booty."

Such things his father showed, and warned him more closely,
when sleep and the dissolving image left the young man.
He rises and addresses the powers that dwell in the infernal
groves, and, a suppliant, his father's spirits by name:
"Be my leaders in war, and lead me to the city shown me;
I, your avenger, gleaming in Sarranian purple,
will send funeral-offerings, conspicuous, the Iberians routed,
and will add to your tombs sacred games in contest."

He goes before and hastens the ways and snatches the column
along, swift in its nimble course, and wears out the plains.
So, when the chariot-horse has leapt headlong from the Pisaean barrier,
not only before the others, but indeed — wondrous to tell —
before its own self the victor goes, and through the air
no eyes hold out to keep pace with the flying car.
And now the seventh light of Hyperion, rising with its lamp,
was gradually raising the citadels nearer to those drawing on
toward the city, and the roofs grew with the nearing step,
and Laelius, borne by sea, kept the hour
which the leader had given for the fleet to subdue the walls,
and was girdling the buildings from behind with his keels poured against them.

Carthage, aided by the lavish favour of nature,
raises its lofty walls high, flowed around by the sea.
A modest island closes the narrowed jaws of the sea,
where Titan sprinkles the lands at its eastern rising.
But, where it looks toward the late ridges of falling Phoebus,
it drives its waters into a sluggish plain of standing pools,
which the incoming and ebbing tide swells and draws back in turn.
But on the front it sits, lofty toward the cold Bear,
a city set on a ridge, and runs down sloping to the sea,
and defends its safe walls with the eternal wave.

Bold, as if marching on a level plain to bear
victorious standards, the soldier strove to scale the ridge.
The nature of the ground fought against them, and with small effort
from above the warriors, rolled here and there down the slopes,
unsteady, poured out their lives, their bodies maimed.
But when the rolling wave of the sea retreated
and the flood fled into the open seas in swift gliding,
and where lately the lofty ships had furrowed the blue,
there Nereus gave them to cross over unharmed on foot,
straining in silence, undismayed to approach this way,
the Dardanian leader draws a sudden band over the sea
and, on foot, flies through the waves to the walls. Then the swift men
hasten from behind, where Aris, trusting in the waves,
had left the city ungarrisoned, without soldiery.
Then prostrate on the ground — pitiable — the Carthaginian
gave his vanquished neck to chains and surrendered his unarmed people.
This city the rising Titan saw, as he rose, walled
about with camps, and beheld it taken, the same,
sooner than he dipped his axle in the Hesperian flood.

Dawn, entering the lands, had driven out the shadows:
at the outset they set up altars. A bull, a lofty victim,
falls to Neptune and equally to the Thunderer.
Then merits are matched with gifts, and valour takes
the rewards won by blood: this man shines on his breast with trappings,
this one girds his warlike neck with a golden collar,
that one gleams, lofty in the honour of the mural crown.
Laelius before all, whose right hand and house were famous,
is given thirty oxen and the lofty title of the sea-fight
and the recent arms of the Carthaginian
commander. Then to the men a spear, then to each the standards
of Mars, as he deserved, and the first-fruits of the booty are given.

After the praises of men and gods were completed,
the captive wealth is viewed and the booty set in order:
this gold for the senators, these talents for war and Mars,
this for the gifts of kings, this above all for the temples of the gods,
the rest for the right hands of the warriors and their fair toil.
Moreover the ruler of an Iberian people is summoned,
whose betrothed and whose love, fixed in his bones, was for that betrothed.
This token of her beauty Scipio granted, glad and exulting,
a gift to the rejoicing maiden, untouched.
Then, free of cares, on the neighbouring shore they set up tables
and hold banquets with festal sport.
Laelius speaks out: "All hail, O leader to be revered,
hail, soul of chastity. Let the glory and praise of the great
heroes yield to you, and the valour celebrated in song.
The Mycenaean ruler who drew a thousand prows onto the seas,
and he who mingled Thessalian arms with the Inachian,
violated their allied pact for a woman's love,
and then no tents stood on the Phrygian field
not full of captive couches: for you alone a barbarian
maiden has been kept more holily than the Iliac priestess of Phoebus."
Such things and the like they wove in alternate talk,
until Night, her body wrapped in a black mantle,
brought in her dark horses and counselled rest.

Meanwhile, with Emathian tumult, the Aetolian land
seethed, struck by the sudden keels of the Macedonians.
Next to this the Acarnanian was joining right hands with the enemy.
The cause of the new upheaval was the forces allied by pact
with the Carthaginians and king Philip for the Ausonian war.
He, distinguished in race and from the origin of an ancient kingdom,
swelled with the sceptres of the Aeacidae and his ancestor Achilles.
He both terrified Oricum with nocturnal arms,
and where through Illyricum the Taulantian dweller
inhabits little walls of no name,
he advanced, turbulent, with weapons. He, borne over the sea,
now harassing the lands of the Phaeacians and Thesprotia,
ranged over Epirus, his daring vain with a useless helmet,
now displayed his standards on the Anactorian shore
and drenched the Ambracian gulfs and the Olpaean shores
with swift war. He drove with oars the seething shallows
of Leucate and swiftly saw the Actian temples of Phoebus.
Nor did he leave the harbours of Ithaca, the realms of Laertes, and Same
unapproached, and the rocks of the Cephallenians sounding with hoary
flood, and Neritos with its rocky fields.
He too, rejoicing greatly to approach the seat of Pelops and the Achaean
walls, was drawing near to Calydon, grim to Diana,
and the homes of Oeneus, the Curetic dwellings,
promising against Hesperia his own battles to the Greeks.
Then Ephyre was ranged over, and Patrae and royal Pleuron
and twin-peaked Parnassus and the rocks that speak with Phoebus.
And often, the war recalling him to his fathers' Penates,
when now Sarmatian Orestes infested his realms,
now the harsh force of the Dolopians had overflowed into his fields,
yet not easy to desist from his vain undertaking
he carried the shadow of war round the Greek shores:
until, stripped now by sea, now by land, he gave up
the hope set in the Tyrians, and, a suppliant, ratified the Dardan
treaties, nor shrank from accepting a law for his kingdom.

Then too the fortune of Tarentum, sprung from Tyndareus, increased
the Latin resources and praise alike. For at last the treacherous
city was subdued by old Fabius, the last title in arms
of the cautious leader. Then too his cleverness won a safe
honour, the walls taken without blood.
For when it was found out that he who ruled the Punic standards
was being consumed by a woman's flame, and a secret trick
thereafter pleased his quiet valour, the brother of the beloved
woman (for he was in the Rutulian camp) is compelled
to go to his sister and to conquer the woman's heart with great
promises, if she should grant to pass the unbarred gates
to the Libyan commander. And, gaining his wish,
Fabius, the Carthaginian outwitted, penetrated by night
the walls surrounded by weapons and ungarrisoned.

But who then would hesitate to say that Phoebus had turned away
his horses from the city of Romulus, who at the same time
should hear that Marcellus had met death in arms?
That mass of a man, and his breast inhabited by hot Gradivus
and never made to tremble by any perils,
alas, to make Hannibal so famous by his bright fall,
sank down. The terror of Carthage lies on the plains,
perhaps about to snatch from Scipio the name of the war
brought to its end, had some god added a little to his age.

A hill separated the Agenorean rampart from the Ausonian
mound (Mars had settled in the fields of Daunus).
Crispinus, companion of his cares and partner of the highest
honour, was stirring up shared wars with Marcellus.
To him Marcellus: "My mind yearns to range over the neighbouring
woods and to set men on the middle of the mountain,
lest the Libyan first seize the height by hidden ventures.
If it is your wish, Crispinus, I would have you a partner
of the labour. Counsels are never lacking to two."
When these things were settled, they strive for some time now to lift
themselves onto their eager horses. Marcellus, when he saw
his son fitting on his arms and glad at the tumult,
said: "You surpass our vigour by your wondrous ardour.
Let the premature labour be fortunate. As I saw you in the Sicanian
city, when your age did not yet allow you war,
handling the battle by my countenance!
Here, glory, here, our own, join yourself to your father's side
and learn from me as master to attempt the new War-god."
Then, embracing the boy's neck, he prays thus, a few words:
"Highest of the gods, grant that, from the governor of the Libyans now,
on these, on these shoulders, I may bear you the spoils of honour." No more,
when Jupiter poured bloody dews from the serene
sky and sprinkled the arms, unpropitious, with black drops.
Scarce had they finished speaking and entered the narrow
jaws of the death-bearing hill, when a swift throng,
the Nomads, attack with javelins, and are borne like a storm-cloud
of heaven, the blind covert pouring forth
armed knots into the battle. After his beleaguered valour
sees that nothing remains which he should owe further
now to the gods above, he yearns to bear with him beneath the shades
a great name of death. Now with hurled spears from afar
he rises the higher, now he rages hand to hand with the sword.
And perhaps he would have swum out of the savage straits of swift peril,
had not a weapon come into the limbs of his son before him.
Then the father's hands trembled, and, loosened with grief,
the ill-fated arms slipped from his stiffened palms.
A spear, meeting him, passes through his bared breast,
and, falling, with his pressed chin he marked the grasses.

But when the Tyrian leader amid the savage battles
saw the weapon fixed beneath the breast set against him,
he cried out hugely: "Cease now, Carthage, to fear
the Latin laws: the deadly name lies low,
the pillar of the Ausonian realm. But let a right hand
so like our own be sent not obscure to the shades.
Great-souled valour is free of envy." At once a tomb's
high altar is built and raised to the sky.
They carry together vast oaks from the woods: you would believe
the Sidonian leader had fallen. Then incense and feasts
and the fasces and the man's shield, the last procession, are borne.
He himself, setting fire beneath, "Praise eternal is won," he said.
"We have taken Marcellus from Latium. Perhaps the Italian
race may at last wish to lay down its arms. Go, perform
the obsequies of a proud soul, and grant his ashes the duty
of the last office: never will I deny you this, Rome."
Like and the same was the fortune of the labours of the other
consul: a horse bore him back lifeless to the standards.

Such things in Ausonia. But not such was for the Iberians
the outcome of arms on the plains. Swift victory, all
the Carthaginians snatched away on a sudden, far and wide
had terrified the nations. For the leaders one hope of safety,
if they should join their allied forces. That the young man, begun
under mighty auspices, as if he bore his country's thunderbolts
in arms, had snatched in less than a whole day a city walled
on a lofty mountain-top and on crags, heaped with the slaughter
of men, where that martial Hannibal in the land
of Spain, a year consumed, had overthrown Saguntum,
to be matched neither in manhood nor in the richness of its wealth.

Nearest, his mound set against the rocky woods,
Hasdrubal stretched out his camp, breathing his brother's mighty deeds.
Here the strength of the Cantabrian, mingled with the rebel Africans,
here the Asturian swifter than the swift Moor;
and so great was the majesty of the commander in the Iberian land
as great was Hannibal's terror on the Laurentine shore.
By chance the day had brought back to the Tyrians the ancient solemn
honour, on which they first, having begun the foundations
of high Carthage, founded the new city with huts.
And glad, recalling the origins of his people, the leader
held festal joys with garlanded standards,
making peace with the gods. A fraternal cloak shone,
let down from his shoulders, a gift, which as a pledge of close
alliance the Trinacrian king had sent among the presents
to the Libyan, a token worn by the Aeolian tyrants.
A golden pinion was snatching a boy to the upper air
through the clouds, an eagle poised in interwoven flight.
A vast cave nearby, which the needle counterfeited in purple,
the home of the Cyclopes. Here, reclining, Polyphemus
swallows with his death-bearing jaws bodies dripping with gore.
Around lie bones broken and shaken out by his bites.
He himself, his hand stretched out, demands the cups of Laertes
and mingles with the wine the gore belched from his mouth.

Conspicuous in the art of the Sicilian woof, the Tyrian
was praying for the peace of the gods above at the grassy altars:
behold, amid their midst, a messenger, borne on a galloping
four-footed horse, reported that hostile arms were drawing near.
Their minds were troubled, and the unfinished honour of the gods
gave way. The rites broken, they leave the altars:
they are shut in by the rampart, and when dewy Aurora sent
thin light to the pole, they snatch up the contests of Mars.
Bold Sabbura received the whistling cornel-spear of the
son of Scipio, and the twin lines were stirred as by an omen.
The Latin leader cries out: "The first victim for you,
hallowed spirits, lies on the field. Come now, soldier,
to battle and slaughter, as you were wont to go with your leaders
breathing, so rush." And while he speaks these things,
they fall on. Laenas slays Myconus, and Latinus Cirta,
and Maro Thysdrus, and Catilina cuts down Nealces, defiler
of his sister's marriage-chamber. Kartalo, ruler of the Libyan
sands, falls in the way of keen Nasidius.
You too the terrified land of the Pyrenees saw,
mingled with the Carthaginians and raging scarce to be believed,
great glory of Dardania, Laelius, to whom happy
Nature gave all things, with no god refusing.
He, heard in the forum, when he had loosed his sweet mouth,
matched the Neleian honey of Pylian old age.
He, when the suspended fathers and the senate-house demanded
his voice, drew the hearts of the senate as with a song.
The same man, when a sudden grim murmur of the trumpet had grazed
his ear on the field, rushed with such fervour
into battle and the lines that you would think he was born for war
alone: nothing of life pleased him to be done without praise.
Then too he struck down Gala, handling battles in stealthy
light. His mother had once withdrawn him from the rites
of Carthage by a substituted birth,
but no joys stand long for the deceived gods.
Then he sent Alabis and Murrus and Draces down to the shades,
Draces begging his last, with a womanish cry.
His neck, amid words and prayers, he severs
with the sword: the murmurs lasted on in the cut-off neck.

But the Libyan leader had no equal ardour in arms.
He makes for the coverts of a leafy hill and the trackless
rocks, nor did the slaughter or the final losses move him
of his column. An exile, he gazed at Italy and the Alps,
the great rewards of flight. A watchword gives the silent signal:
that the battle be carried off into the hills and the woods,
the men scattered, and that whoever shall have escaped
should seek the topmost citadel and summit of the Pyrenees. Then first,
the honour of his arms stripped off and concealed by an Iberian shield,
he goes off into the mountains and willingly leaves his straggling
columns. The Latin soldier sends his victorious standards
into the deserted camp. Not by any city taken
was more booty won, and he held back the angers, delayed
from slaughter, as the Libyan leader had foreseen:
as a beaver, caught in the waves of a river's flood,
the part torn off from its groin and the cause of its peril,
swims out, escaping, while the enemy is intent on the booty.
After the tireless Carthaginian is hidden in the secret shadows,
trusting in the rocky wood, greater wars
are sought again in turn, and the enemy more surely overcome.
On a mound of the Pyrenees they fix a shield with an inscription:
THE SPOIL OF HASDRUBAL, SCIPIO THE VICTOR, TO GRADIVUS.

Meanwhile, the terror laid aside, beyond the steeps of the mountain,
in the Bebrycian court, the Carthaginian armed the peoples,
lavish to buy right hands and ready
to squander on war what war had won. Sent ahead,
the weights of silver and gold, won in metal-bearing
lands by long peril, increased the fierce spirits.
Hence the venal souls filled the new camps with no slow
soldiery, those who rejoice in the flood of the Rhone,
through whose fields the Arar creeps most sluggish of wave.
And now, the winter spent, the year had begun to grow mild:
thence, entering his swift way through the Celtic fields,
he marvels at the tamed Alps and the steeps of the mountain
made passable, and seeks the footprints of the Herculean tread
and compares his brother's ways with the divine ventures.
But when he came to the summit and settled in the camp
of Hannibal, "What walls," he said, "what walls, I pray,
does Rome raise higher, which after these, conquered by my
brother, should stand unharmed? Let the glory of my right hand,
I pray, be so happy, and let no sinister god begrudge
that I have gone all the way to the stars." Then eager, from his lofty
column, where the mound, sloping down, shows
a fortified way from on high, he flies down with hastened arms.
Not with such fear did the beginnings of the war resound:
now they boast of a twin Hannibal, now of two camps
joining from here and there, and that the leaders, fed by prosperous
wars on Italian blood, are joining their War-god
and doubling their lines: that the enemy would come to the walls
in headlong course and would see the darts lately hurled
by Elissaean arms clinging in the gate.
At these things the Oenotrian Land, gnashing, thus to herself:
"By so great a frenzy, alas, you gods, am I scorned and despised
by the Sidonian race, I who once granted Saturn,
fearing his son's sceptre, to settle on my shores
and to reign? This is now the tenth summer that turns
since we are trampled. A young man, to whom there remain only
wars against the gods above, snatched from the farthest shore,
brought arms against me, and, the Alps violated, ablaze,
came down into our fields. How many bodies of the slain
have I covered, so often disfigured by my outstretched foster-children!
No tree of mine flowers with happy berries:
the unripe corn is cut down by the swift sword:
the roofs of the villas, fallen, are borne
into my lap and foul my realms with their ruins.
Shall I endure even this one, who now has brought himself to my vast
shores, seeking to burn out the wretched remnants of war?
Then let the wandering African cleave me with the plough
and let the Libyan commend his seed to Ausonian furrows,
unless I bury in one tomb all the columns that exult
on my broad plains." While she turns over such things,
and black Night closes the chambers of gods and men,
she rushes headlong to the camp of her Amyclaean grandson.
He then was keeping, on the turf of a neighbouring rampart,
the Carthaginian who held his arms within the Lucanian bounds.
Here the image of the Latin land approaches the young man:
"Glory of the besieged and, Marcellus snatched away, greatest
hope of Rome, Nero, break and drive off your slumber.
Something great you must dare, if you will trust your country's fates,
which, even with the enemy driven from the walls,
shall make victors tremble that it was done. In gleaming arms
the Carthaginian has flooded the plains, where the Sena keeps
through the ages the name left by the Gallic peoples.
Unless you swiftly snatch your wing-footed squadrons to the contest,
you shall help too late, after Rome's destruction.
Rise, come, bear your steps. The broad fields in the region of the Metaurus
I have doomed to the tombs and bones of the Carthaginians."
With these words it goes, and departing seemed to drag him
trembling and to drive the squadrons through the broken gates.
Troubled, with kindled heart, he breaks his slumber
and, a suppliant, stretching his twin palms to the stars,
prays to the Earth and the Night and the stars sprinkled in the sky
and to Phoebe, guide of his way under the silent light.
Then he chooses right hands worthy of so great an attempt,
and where Larinas, neighbour of the upper sea, lies,
and where the Marrucine race, hardy in war, and the Frentanian
untaught to lay aside faith to his allies in arms,
then, where the Praetutian youth tames the vine-bearing
fields, glad of the labour, both than wing and than thunderbolt and than the wintry
waves and than the Achaemenian bow swifter
he flies forth. Each is his own exhorter: "Come, press on, the safety
of Ausonia the doubtful gods, and whether Rome stand or fall,
have set in your feet," they cry, and rush.
The keen leader has the manner of exhorting, to go before:
him they magnify, striving to match his course by following,
and untiring they are borne by night and day.

But Rome, hearing only that the evils of the adverse
war were swelling, trembled with fear, and complained that Nero
had hoped too much, and that with one wound her
remaining life could be taken away. That neither arms nor gold
nor manhood nor blood to shed remained.
Was he then to attack Hasdrubal, who for battle could not be enough
for Hannibal alone? And again, when he should learn that the arms
had been turned aside from his own rampart, the Carthaginian
would cling to the gates. That one had come who would vie
with his proud brother, to whom would yield the greatest glory
of the city's destruction. The order of the fathers raves, frantic,
in the depth of its heart, and meanwhile meditates, with great love
of preserving its honour, by what end it might snatch itself
from threatening servitude and escape the unjust gods.
Amid these groans, in the dark of the shadowy night,
Nero comes up to the camp which, joined to fierce
Hasdrubal, Livius held with a guarded rampart.
He was once a warrior and skilled to kindle war,
a famous fighter who had flourished in his early years,
soon, wounded by the false charge of an unjust crowd,
had hidden his sad years in the secrets of the countryside.
But after a heavier burden and terror, with the peril
nearer, demanded the man, recalled to arms,
so many leaders slain, he had given his anger to his country.

But the tricks of the fresh arms did not escape Hasdrubal,
though Night had veiled the cunning with darkness.
Traces of dust seen on the shields stirred his suspicion,
and, the sign of a hurried arrival, both the horse and the men
drawn up in body. Besides, the trumpet, twice giving a clear
signal, joined to a twin general, betrayed the camp
to the king. But, if his brother's life survived,
how at last was it lawful to join the allied forces
to the consuls? But indeed only, until the truth should be laid bare,
there remained the stratagem of delaying and of dragging out the war.

Nor does he put off the counsels of flight in sluggish dread.
Night, mother of sleep, had purged mortal breasts of cares,
and the darkness fostered dread silences:
he creeps out, bearing his steps in suspense from the camp,
and bids the columns slip away, silent, with quiet tread.
Having found a moonless night through the silent fields,
they hasten and avoid sounds. But the Earth, roused, cannot
be deceived under so great a movement: she entangles the driven
ways with blind error and, the shadows favouring,
wheels round in narrow space those led along their own footprints.
For where the river, with winding bends, slants
its curved banks, and with a backward gliding over the rough ground
returns into itself, here, the leading toil in vain,
they enclose a tiny circle with frustrated steps,
and in the error of the way the gift of the darkness was taken away.

Light presses on and discloses the flight. A keen
whirlwind of horsemen rushes from the open gates, and an iron
tempest covers the plains far and wide. Not yet are arms and hands
mingled: already the darts, sent ahead, drink blood.
Hence the Dictaean feathers, bidden to halt the fleeing
Carthaginian, fly, hence the lance with its black whirl
bears death to whatever man the stroke shall seize.
They lay aside the care of departure and, trembling, draw up
their forced lines and turn their hopes to battle.

Himself amid the midst (for he saw the hardness of affairs),
the Sidonian leader, lofty on the high back
of his galloping horse, stretching both voice and hands:
"By the glories sought for you under the farthest pole,
by the praises of my brother I beg, let us prove that the brother
of Hannibal has come. For Latium Fortune labours
to give proofs by adversities and to show how great a man,
tamer of the Iberian land, has turned against the Rutulians,
a soldier wont to make war at the Herculean columns.
Perhaps my brother too may come into these very battles.
Worthy of a man, worthy spectacles, I beseech, with a soil
full of bodies, hasten. Whatever leader could be feared
in war lies before my brother. Now the one hope,
from his punishment and his hiding-place, Livius, broken in years,
is offered to you, a head condemned. Go, come, I pray,
lay low the leader, with whom for my brother to clash
were a shame, and grant an end to his base old age."
But on the other side Nero: "Why do you delay to close the labours
of a mighty war? By your feet, soldier, a great glory
is won: now heap it up, the steep thing begun, by your right hand.
Alas, rashly you left the camp, the strength led away,
unless victory absolves the deed. Take the lead in praise:
the enemy will be remembered to have fallen at your coming."

In another part, conspicuous, his grey hair bared of the helmet,
Livius: "Hither, young men, hither watch me rushing
into battle, and as far as my sword shall have opened a way,
so far enter, and at last shut off with steel
the Alps now too open to the ranging Carthaginians.
For unless we lay low the columns with swift War
and a sudden thunderbolt of Carthage, Hannibal, be at hand,
what god would snatch any of us from the infernal shades?"
Then, the helmet taken on his head, he ratifies his grim
words with the steel, and, covered, mingles fierce battles in his old age.
Him, through the wedges and through the thickest bodies
of the field, dealing as many to death as he hurled darts,
the troubled Macae fled, the fierce Autololes fled
and the youth of the Rhone with unshorn hair.

Nabis, coming from the prophetic sands of Ammon,
mingled wicked battles secure of his fate,
as if a god protected him, and, swollen, with vain mouth
had promised to fix Italian spoils in his fathers' temples.
A blue robe blazed with the Garamantian gem,
as when the scattered lights of the stars glitter in the sky,
and he had kindled his helmet with gems and his shield with gold.
From his horned helmet a hanging fillet bore before it
sacred terrors and the honour of the gods.
The man had a bow and a quiver, and darts steeped
in horned vipers, and armed with poison he waged his wars.
Moreover, set on his horse's back in his wonted manner,
the weight of his Sarmatian pike, supported on his knee,
he drove level against the enemies opposite.
Then too, transfixing through the limbs, through the arms,
Sabellus with a weapon, before the consul's eyes, with a great shout
he was carrying him off exulting, and exulting sang of Ammon.
The elder bore not this rage and such great swellings
in his barbarian heart, and hurled his weapon, and at once
the victor took from the victor both the booty and the life together.

Hasdrubal leaps up, sad at the heard shout of the ruin,
and, as the Arab began to snatch the spoil of gem-bearing
dress from the slain and the trappings stiff with gold,
balances a javelin from behind to the bone.
The wretch, the coverings seized with his two palms,
was hastily plucking at them and had bared his trembling limbs:
he fell, and gave back the sacred robes and the golden threads
to the lifeless man, sinking onto the foe he had despoiled.
But Canthus slays Rutilus — Canthus, possessor of the sand
where the Philaeni placed their unconquered, famous name —
Rutilus, rich in sheep, for whom a thousand woolly ewes
bleat in the deep stalls. He himself, exercising his ease
with soft care, now in a cold river was breaking
the excessive heats for his flock, now glad in the grass
was shearing the gleaming fleeces of snowy wool,
or, when the flock returned from pasture to its shelter,
watched in the sheepfolds the lambs that knew their mothers.
He fell, betrayed, his shield's bronze transfixed,
and groaned too late that he had gone forth from his father's stalls.

The more keenly for this the Italian youth charges and presses:
as a torrent, as a tempest, as the flame of the flashing
thunderbolt, as the sea flees Boreas, as the hollow
clouds run, when Eurus has mingled sky with sea.
Tall stood the cohorts, the standards of the Celts,
the front line. These the sudden force loosens with its charge
and fierce wedge, and the men, weary with the error of the ways,
unequal to the ground and panting from the long toil,
their nation's native fear turns away. To add to their backs
spears the Ausonian, and to press on with the pursuing weapon
and not to grant flight. Thyrmis falls by one wound,
Rhodanus by more than one, and the lance casts down Morinus,
overthrown by an arrow and now at the very point of falling.
Keen Livius on his horse, having given all the reins,
presses the retreating and drives his swift-hoofed steed
upon the departing squadrons. Then the fleeing necks of turned-away
Mosa he scatters with his sword. The head, slipped from on high
with the helmet, struck the ground with its weight: but the trunk,
sitting on, the troubled horse snatched into the battle.
Here Cato (for he too was brandishing weapons in the midst of the tumult)
said: "If, when we lost the first Alps in war,
this man had been set against the Tyrian youth! Alas, how great
a right hand has stood idle for Latium, and how many deaths
the grim votes of a corrupt field gave to the Carthaginians!"

And now the lines were giving way, and over all the Gauls'
fear had put on fear, and the Sidonian Fortune
was falling: to the Rutulians Victory had turned her wings.
Lofty, as if with his early youth flowering again,
the consul went exulting, greater and greater to behold.
Behold, drawing with him a squadron hoary with dust,
the Agenorean leader comes up, and whirling weapons
from his arms, resounds: "Check your flight. To what enemy do we yield?
Is it not a shame? An old man, his years withered, drives
the lines turned about. Now, I pray, now does my right hand in arms
degenerate, and is it ashamed of me? Mine was Belus, the source
of my ancestors, mine Sidonian Dido a kindred
name, and before all to be counted in war is Hamilcar
my father. Mine, to whom yield mountains and lakes
and plains and rivers, is my brother. Great Carthage thinks me
second to Hannibal, and on the Baetis shores
the nations that suffered my battles match me with my brother."
While he recounts such things, borne into the midst of the enemy,
when the new arms of the spotted consul gleamed,
he hurls his spear with too hasty effort. It on the edges
of the brazen shield and the coverings of the corslet, at the top
of the shoulder, fell, not happy, and the body, scantly aimed at,
it grazed, dyed with not much blood,
but promised vain joys to the wishing Carthaginian.

The Rutulians were troubled, and their breasts confused at the terrifying
sight. Then the consul, chiding the attempt:
"A body grazed amid feminine horns you would believe
struck by nails or by boyish palms.
Go, men, teach how great wounds Roman
hands are wont to inflict." Then indeed a vast force
of weapons pours out, and the sun is overcome by thick shadow.
And now over the outstretched plains the bodies of men, fallen
in alternate slaughter, lie, and the corpses, sunk into the wave,
joined the banks with a growing heap:
as, when Dictynna plies the shady glades with hunting
and gives spectacles to her glad mother,
shaking the woods of Pindus or ranging Maenala
(all the throng of the Naiads, accompanied with full quivers,
rushes, and the arrow-bearing bow-cases hiss),
then the beasts, strewn over the rocks and over their very lairs,
through the valleys and rivers and the caverns green with moss,
lie in much slaughter: she exults on the mountain's summit,
the daughter of Latona, ranging with her eyes over the welcome prey.

Having heard before others of the elder's wound, savage
Nero breaks his way through the midst, and, seeing the men's
battle equal, "What then, what is left after this
to the fates of Italy? If you do not conquer this enemy,
will you conquer Hannibal?" he says. He rushes the swifter, frantic,
into the midst, and when he saw the Tyrian leader roaring among the foremost
lines, as a beast of the raging sea,
tossed long along the barren deep toward its pastures,
when from afar, ill-wounded, it sees a fish in the wave,
seethes, and, ranging, swallows the swimming prey beneath the flood,
the sea mingled with fishes, far and wide:
no delay for weapon, none for words. "No longer," he said,
"shall you slip from me. Here the trackless groves of the Pyrenees
shall not deceive, nor shall you mock with vain promises,
as once, caught in the treacherous Iberian land,
you escaped our right hand by a lying treaty."
This Nero, and he hurls his javelin, nor was the stroke vain.
For balanced, the point settled in the far side.
He attacks over these things, undaunted, with drawn sword,
and pressing with his shield-boss the trembling limbs of the fallen,
"If you would by chance have any commands carried back
to your brother under this last mischance, we will bear them," he said.
The Sidonian in answer: "By no death am I terrified.
Use your War-god, while for our spirits there is at hand
straightway an avenger. If you prepare to bear my last
words to my brother, I bid that, victorious, he burn the
Capitol and mingle Jupiter's bones with the ash
and our ashes." As he longs to add more
and seethes with rage, he transfixes him in death with the sword
and, victor, snatches the faithless head. The columns, their leader
laid low, are strewn, trusting no longer in War.

And now black night took away the day and the sun's ways,
when they restore their strength with sparing food and sleep,
and, the day not yet returning, the victorious standards,
where they had come, they carry back to the camps closed in fear.
Then Nero, bearing high on a tall pike
the face of the slain leader: "We have repaid Cannae," he said,
"Hannibal, and the Trebia and the shores of Trasimene with you,
by your brother's head. Go, now double your treacherous wars
and summon your twin lines. These rewards remain
for those who should desire your arms, the Alps crossed."
The Carthaginian held back his tears and steadfastly lessened
by bearing his evils, and, his mouth closed, murmured to himself
that he would send to his brother, in due time, worthy funeral-offerings.
Then, his camp moved far off, dissembling under the guise
of rest, he shut off the doubtful contests of Mars.
