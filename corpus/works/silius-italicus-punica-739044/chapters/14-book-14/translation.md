14. Turn now your songs, you powers of Helicon,
to the sea of Ortygia and the cities of the Sicilian shore.
Here is the labour of your gift: now to approach the Daunian realms
of Aeneas' sons, now the Sicanian harbours,
or to range over the homes of the Macedonians and the Achaean fields,
or to dip wandering footsteps in the Sardinian wave,
or the huts once ruled by the Tyrian people,
or to visit the last day and the boundaries of the lands.
So Mars demands, driven and scattered over the shores.
Come then, let us follow where the trumpets, where the wars lead.

A great part of Ausonia, the land of Trinacria lies,
since once, the South Wind storming and the waves laying waste,
it received the straits driven on by the blue trident.
For long ago, the sea hurled by the blind force of a whirlwind
through the hidden depths, cleft apart the entrails of the torn land
and, breaking through the fields with mid-ocean,
carried over the cities along with their peoples, both wrenched away.
From that time swift Nereus, keeping the divided channels,
utterly forbids the sundered shores to be joined by his savage tide.
But the space which severs the partnership of the land,
the report goes (so close does the strait run between)
lets the barking pass across, and the morning songs of birds.
Much is the virtue of the soil: now to repay the ploughs their interest,
now to shade the hills with olive, to give a name to Bacchus,
to have bred the swift war-horse fit to bear the trumpets,
and to add Cecropian honeycombs to the nectar of Hybla.
Here too you will revere the Paeonian springs with their secret sulphur,
here the mouths of prophets worthy of Phoebus and the Muses,
the excellent ones, who make the sacred woods ring with song
and sound their Helicon with the Syracusan Muse.
A people quick of tongue, yet the same, when it stirred wars,
wont to mark out its harbours with trophies of the sea.

After the dread sceptre of Antiphates and the Cyclopian realms,
the Sicani first turned the new fields with the ploughshare —
Pyrene sent the peoples, who imposed on the empty land
a name borrowed from their fathers' river.
Soon a band of Ligurians, with a Sicilian leader, renewed
the changed names in realms won by war.
Nor was the Cretan a dweller to be ashamed of: Minos had led
men driven from a hundred walls to ill-fated battles,
seeking to punish Daedalus. When by an unspeakable trick
that judge had passed to the everlasting shades
through the snares of the daughters of Cocalus, the Minoan throng,
weary of the zeal for warring, settled on the Sicilian shores.
Trojan Acestes and Trojan Helymus mingled the Phrygian
stock, who, walls built and a people following them,
gave from themselves names that should last to the long walls.
Nor do the walls of Zancle bear an obscure renown,
which the hand of Saturn's daughter granted, her weapon laid aside.
But no glory is fairer on the Hennaean shores
than she who founded her name from the Sisyphean Isthmus
and shines far before others in her Ephyrean foster-children.
Here Arethusa receives in her fish-filled fount her own
Alpheus, who bears the tokens of the sacred crown.

But unfair Mulciber loves not the Trinacrian caverns.
For Lipare, consumed beneath by vast furnaces,
vomits sulphurous smoke from its eaten-out summit.
But Aetna belches out the groans of fires shut within,
its crags trembling, and, imitating the fury of the sea,
thunders restless through blind crashings of its din
by night and day alike. As from the dark fount of Phlegethon
a torrent of flames overflows, and with a pitchy storm
it rolls half-burnt rocks from its molten caverns.
But although it boils within with a great whirl
of flames and the fire, ever springing up beneath, flows on,
on its highest ridge it holds — wondrous to tell —
white ice neighbouring the flames, and with eternal frost
the burning crags bristle: winter stands on the summit
of the lofty hill, and black hot ash covers the snow.

Why should I tell of the lands ruled in Aeolus' name
and the homes of the winds and the bars set upon the storms?
Here, turned deep within to the Pelopean realms of Pachynus,
the rocks, beaten, answer back to the Ionian deep.
Here, set over against Libya and the raging North-Westers,
noble Lilybaeum sees its sloping claws.
But where the third brow of the land's opposite side
slopes toward Italy with its ridge thrust out to the shores,
lofty Pelorus raises itself with its sandy mass.

Over these lands, mild through a long age, Hieron had ruled
as a gentle master, strong to handle the people with serene
command and to harry no hearts with fear
of their parents, and, not easy to violate the faith
pledged at the altars, he had kept the bonds of alliance chaste
with the Ausonians through many years.
But when the fates released the man in his frail old age,
the deadly sceptre passed to his young grandson,
and the peaceful palace received unruly ways.
For the king, not yet endowed with twice eight years,
grew dizzy on the high throne and could not bear the weights
of kingship nor too much trust in unstable affairs.
And soon, in brief, with arms protecting his offences, no
right was known and no wrong unknown. Cheapest to the king
was the care for shame. To his headlong frenzy his mother's
descent from Pyrrhus gave spurs, and the proud
race of his ancestor, grandson of Aeacus, and Achilles eternal in song.
Therefore a sudden ardour for favouring the Carthaginians' designs,
and no delay for the crime: now he joins new treaties, the pact being
that the Sidonian conqueror should yield the Sicilian shores.
But the Penalties stood by, and the Fury denied a tomb,
in the very land where he had lately pledged not to see his ally.
For no longer easy to suffer his savage pride
and the young man burning in bloody luxury and mingling foul things
with dire — those whom anger and fear inflamed —
sworn men, cut him down. Nor now was there measure to the swords: they add
the slaughter of women, and lay low with the steel the seized
bodies of innocent sisters. New liberty rages in arms
and tosses off the yoke: part want the Punic camp,
part the Italians and what is known, nor is the throng of the frenzied
without those who would rather ally with neither pact.

With such an upheaval in Trinacria and the Sicanian affairs
in turmoil at the king's destruction, lofty in honour
(for the third purple had renewed his Latin fasces)
Marcellus brought his fleet to the shores of Zancle.
And when all was laid open to the man — the slaughter of the tyrant,
the doubtful minds of men, what places and how great the arms
of Carthage hold, what people remains friendly
to the Trojan-born, what swellings of pride Arethusa
conceives, and that she persists in not opening her gates —
he leans on the war and swiftly, through the nearest parts,
pours out with kindled breast the whole plague of arms.
Not otherwise Boreas, when headlong from Rhodope's peak
he has flung himself and with his tenth surge driven
the sea onto the lands, follows with a roar the mass
of the cast-out sea and rages with shrieking wings.
The first battles laid waste the Leontine fields,
the land once ruled by dread Laestrygon.
The leader pressed on, to whom to conquer the Greek
squadrons slowly was the same as to be conquered. He rushes over the whole field
(you would think a womanish crowd ran together against men)
and fattens with blood the fields pleasing to Ceres.
They are strewn everywhere, and swift Mars snatched away
the chance of escaping death on foot. For as flight promised
each man safety, the leader, forestalling, seizes him with the sword.
"Go, reap the unwarlike flock and cut them down with steel,"
he shouts, urging on the lingering bands with his shield-boss.
"With sluggish zeal for wrestling, taught to suffer soft contest
in the shade and rejoicing to gleam with oil,
the cowardly youth stands by, a middling glory for the conquerors.
This praise alone is granted: that you conquer with the enemy in sight."
At the leader's word the whole army charges in,
and they know with themselves only the contest that remains —
who shall surpass with his right hand and excel in rich spoils.

Not more does the wave of the Euboean Euripus rage,
dashed against the crags of Caphereus, nor does Propontis
cast out the sounding sea more violently through its narrow mouth,
nor does the strait that beats the Herculean columns at the sun's
far edge seethe and rush with greater tumult.
Yet a mild glory of the right hand shone forth amid such great
battles by fame. A Tuscan soldier (Asilus
was his name), once captured at the Trasimene streams,
having tried the easy servitude and the gentle commands
of his master Berya, had returned to his native shores
by the goodwill of his favouring lord, and, taking up arms again,
was then expiating his old misfortunes in the Sicilian war.
And he, while he mingled in the savage battles in the midst,
borne against Berya — to whom, sent to the royal pact
by the peoples of the Carthaginians and waging the allied wars,
a helmet of bronze guard had closed the face —
attacks the young man with the steel, and as he bears in fear
his unsteady steps backward, lays him low on the sand.
But the wretch, hearing the victor's voice and his life
trembling and lingering, as if leading it back from the Stygian abode,
breaks the bonds of the helmet ill-trusted from the chin
and was joining prayers and preparing to add words.
But suddenly terrified at the sight and the known face,
the Tuscan recalled his sword by hand, and of his own accord
poured forth such words with a groan, tears welling up:
"Do not, I beg, pray as a suppliant and in doubt for the light:
it is right for me to spare an enemy. By far the best
in soldiering is he for whom it is last and first to keep
faith amid wars: you grant me first to escape death
and save me, not yet saved by the enemy.
I, who have seen grim things, would not call myself worthy
to be turned again to worse things,
if through the midst of fires and through the midst of swords
my right hand should not give you a way." So he speaks, and of his own accord
raises him up, and with the gift of life makes life equal.

But, having now won the first contest begun on the Sicilian
shore, Marcellus, advancing with a quiet column,
turned his victorious standards to the Ephyrean walls.
Thence he surrounded the Syracusan citadels with his camp.
But his love of the sword grew faint. He longed by warning
to calm the men's blind hearts and to root out their angers.
Nor (should they perhaps refuse him, and should they believe
his mildness to be fear) is the abandoned siege kept up with slack
barriers: rather, on the contrary, more intent himself
he watches with wary arms, fearless of face,
and contrives unforeseen perils with secret care.
Not otherwise on the pools of Eridanus or the bank of Cayster
the white swan swims and gives its motionless body to the down-flowing
river and rows the silent waters with its feet.

Meanwhile, while the wavering counsel of the besieged falters,
peoples were summoned and cities bore their allied arms:
Messana, leaning on the strait and least
torn off, divided from Italy, and memorable for its Oscan birth;
then Catane, too near to burning Typhoeus
and once most renowned for having bred the pious brothers,
and you, Camarina, whom the fates forbade to be moved;
then she who calls Hymettus to contest with her nectared
combs, bold Hybla, and Selinus famous for its orchards,
and Mylae, once a just harbour, now with a bare shore,
an unfaithful refuge for those fleeing the seas;
and high Eryx too, and from its lofty summit
Centuripae, and Entella green with abundant Lyaeus,
Entella, a name dear to Hectorean Acestes.
Not Thapsus, not Acrae on its icy mounds
were lacking. The Agyrine band, and Tyndaris raising herself
with her twin Laconians, flows in. High Acragas
snatches up a squadron of a thousand horses and fires the air with neighings,
rolling a dusty cloud into the void.
Grosphus was the leader, whose chased shield bore
a fierce bull, the memorial of an ancient punishment.
That bull, when it scorched bodies set beneath in flames,
changed groans into bellowings, and one might believe that real
herds, driven from the stalls, poured out their cries,
not unpunished indeed: for the inventor of the dire art
himself, dying, bellowed mournfully in his own bull.
Gela came, drawing its name from the river; Halaesa came,
and those who tame the perjured hearts at the Palici
with present punishment, and Trojan Acesta came,
and Acis, who through the Aetnaean borders seeks the seas
and bathes with sweet water the welcome Nereid.
He once was a rival to your passion, Polyphemus,
when, fleeing the rustic anger of your violent breast,
he melted away into slender waters and escaped both the enemy
and mingled with you, Galatea, his victorious wave.
And those too who drink the sounding Hypsa and Alabis
and the Achates, clear in its gleaming flood,
who tend, wandering Chrysas, your fountains, and the Hipparis
of a poor channel, and the Pantagias easy to be passed in its scant flood,
and the yellow shallows of swift Symaethus.
The shores of Thermae, endowed with ancient song,
armed their men, where Himera plunges into the Aeolian
sea. For it splits itself into divided shores,
and seeks the west no less swiftly than it seeks the east.
Nebrodes nourishes the partings of the twin fount,
than which no mountain in Sicily rises richer in shade.
Lofty Henna gave the sacred right hands of the gods to the groves.
Here a cavern, loosening the vast gaping of the earth,
opens a blind path to the spirits by a dark track,
where the new Hymenaeus came to unknown shores,
where once, goaded by this desire, the Stygian ruler
dared to approach the day and, leaving sad Acheron,
drove his chariot through the void into forbidden lands.
Then, the Hennaean maiden snatched, headlong he turned
his eyes, dazed at the sky and fearing the light,
back to Styx, his horses, and hid his prey in the shadows.
Petraea sought Roman leaders, Callipolis sought
Roman treaties, and Engyon of the stony field,
Hadranum and Ergetium together, and Melite proud
in its weapons of wool, and Calacte, its shore rich in fish,
and Cephaloedias, whose shore on the stormy deep
bristles in its blue plains with feeding sea-monsters,
and those who from the Tauromenian seat behold Charybdis
swallowing down ships seized in the draught of its whirlpool
and hurling them again from the bottom to the stars.
This band cherished Latium and the Laurentine standards.
The rest of the Sicanian people stood by Elissa's vows.
Agathyrna gave a thousand, and Trogilus blown by the South Winds,
a thousand the Phacelina seat of Thoantean Diana.
Panhormos came, fruitful in threefold number,
whether you hunt wild beasts in the woods or sweep the sea
with nets or wish to draw down a bird from the sky.
Not idle Herbesus, not Naulocha sluggish in peril
sat still, not Morgentia in its leafy fields
held back from faithless war. Amastra came accompanied
by the men of Menae, and Tisse of small name,
and Netum and Mutyce and the youth of flowing Achaetes.
Drepane, and Helorus loud with its wave,
and Triocala, soon laid waste by the servile war, aided
the Sidonians; fierce Arbela and lofty Ietas
and Tabas teachable in war, and little Cossyra
and Megara, no greater, joined in concordant daring,
and Gaulus, conspicuous on the level sea,
when the halcyon sounds with its song and the unmoving wave
bears the floating nests on the lulled billows.
The renowned city of Syracuse itself had filled its broad
walls with gathered soldiery and varied arms.
The leaders, with vain-talking mouths, were further
maddening the people, easily driven and glad of tumult:
that their walls had never been entered by an enemy, and their four citadels,
and how great a shadow over the trophies of Salamis and the East
their city, impassable by its harbour's design, had cast,
a thing seen by their forefathers: three hundred triremes before their eyes
in one shipwreck, and Athens, propped on the quivered king,
sunk with impunity in the deep by the disaster.
The brothers born at Carthage inflamed the people,
of a Punic mother, but whom, driven from the Syracusan
city under accusation, their Sicilian father
had led out to the Libyan shores, and from the twin stock of their parents
they mingled Tyrian cunning with Sicanian fickleness.

Seeing these things, the leader, after the sedition seemed
past cure, and of its own accord the war was rising from the enemy,
calling the gods of the Sicilians to witness, and the rivers and lakes
and your fountains, Arethusa, that he was called to war
unwilling (that the enemy was putting on arms which it had long
not taken up of its own will), with a vast whirlwind of weapons
he attacks the walls and thunders with arms upon the city.
Equal anger snatches all alike, and they contend and rush.
A tower rising to the stars with manifold storeys
went up, which the Greek had made to grow to ten levels
and had consumed many of the forests' shadows.
From this they strove to roll down pine armed with fire and rocks
and to pour out the plague of hot pitch.
Cimber, hurling from afar a burning torch,
casts it and fixes the deadly weapon in its side.
Vulcan is fed, aided by the whirl of the wind,
and dragging the swelling ruin through the tower's vitals
and through the high mass and the floors so often renewed
mounts exulting, and swiftly devours the timbers crackling
in the flames, and, a vast vapour overflowing at once
to the sky, the victor licks the nodding summit.
They are filled with smoke and a fog of black gloom,
nor is it granted to any to escape, as if seized by the swift stroke
of a thunderbolt, the ruins fell away into ashes.

Equal fortune on the sea against the wretched keels.
For when they bore themselves nearer to the buildings and the city,
where the harbour brings its peaceful waters to the walls,
an unforeseen plague terrified them with a new device.
A beam, skilfully rounded and with its knots smoothed all about,
like a ship's mast, bore, fixed in front,
the weapons of a hooked hand: this, from the lofty rampart of the wall,
seized the warriors up into the air with the claws
of the curved iron, and, drawn back, carried them into the middle of the city.
Nor that force men alone, but often the warlike beams
snatched up a trireme, when from above they struck
the steel driven down upon the stern and its tenacious bites.
And these, as soon as, the iron fixed in the neighbouring timbers,
they had lifted the ship on high — a pitiable sight —
suddenly again, the chains skilfully loosened,
gave it back headlong to the deep with such great mass
that the waves swallowed the whole ship with its soldiers.
Above these snares the wall, skilfully hollowed,
gave narrow openings, through which it was safe
to pour weapons in secret upon those who hurled, the rampart set against them.
Nor was the labour without trickery, lest by the same narrow path
the darts, hurled back again from the same enemy, should return.
Greek cunning and craft more powerful than arms
kept off Marcellus and such great threats by land and sea,
and a vast war stood at the walls.

There was a man, an immortal glory to the Isthmian colonists,
in genius easily before all the foster-children of the earth,
bare of wealth, but to whom heaven and the lands lay open.
He had known how the new Titan at his rising should reveal rains,
sad with darkened rays; whether the unstable earth clings fast
or hangs poised; why by a fixed law Tethys, poured around,
binds this globe with her waters; and at once the labours of sea and moon,
and by what law father Ocean pours out his tides.
That he had numbered the sands of the capacious world
is no idle belief. They tell too that he drew ships
and piled-up rocks against the steep with a feminine hand.
While he wearies the Italian leader and the Trojans by craft,
a Sidonian fleet swam up broadly with a hundred sails
to the rescue and clove the blue with its beaks.
The offspring of Arethusa rises to sudden hopes
and, going forth from the harbour, joins its own keels.
Nor on the other side does the Ausonian hesitate to fit
his arms to the oars and swiftly furrows the seas with sunken oars.
With strokes they twisted the strait: struck by the frequent
beat of the brine, its surface whitens, and far over the seas
the path foams, furrowed with a hoary flood.
They leap upon the sea alike, and the Neptunian realms
tremble at the new tempest. Then the sea
resounds with voices, and the echo of the shout cries from the crags.
And now the warrior, spread out on the empty water,
had surrounded the broad billows with his horns for battle,
enclosing the watery field with a naval net:
and with a like fold curved, the opposing fleet rushed
and narrowed the blue in a crescent ring.
No delay: with the terrific shriekings of savage bronze,
the song resounding far through the empty deep,
the trumpets sounded, at which Triton, roused from the sea,
trembled at the rival murmurs of his twisted conch.
Scarce do they remember the sea: with such vast effort to battle
they lean forward, and, their feet set on the edge of the stern's
furthest part, swaying, they whirl their darts.
The middle space of the sea is strewn with hurled weapons,
and the lofty alder, rising with panting strokes,
cleaves the blue foam with a darkening furrow.
But other ships, by the stroke of dashed timber,
sweep away the oars from the side, others, the beaks passed
through the vitals of the pine, are held by the very wound
where they hold. Loftier amid the midst went
a stern terrible to behold, than which none through all
the age went greater from the Libyan shipyards.
But this beat the sea with four hundred numerous oarsmen
at the oars, and, proud with its capacious sail,
when it drank the swift Boreas and gathered all the blasts
in its horns, drove itself with sluggish timber,
as if it entered the waves driven by arms alone:
the ships run forward with nimble lightness, and, with the Latin
soldier, taught to heed the hand of the helmsman.
When Himilco saw these coming across the leftward sea
on their flank and the prow bidden to charge sidelong,
quickly, the gods of the sea called to in his vows,
in his wonted manner he fits the winged arrow
to the taut string, and when he had balanced it on the enemy with his eyes
and shown the path to the shaft, slackening his arms
in opposite ways, he drew it down, his face following, through the air
into a wound, and fixed to the quill the right hand of the helmsman
sitting on the stern. Nor thereafter had the hand strength
to guide the ship, dead upon the bending helm.
And while the sailor runs to help, as if his ship were taken,
behold, again, with like fate and bowstring, the arrow,
gliding into the middle of the throng, transfixes with its stroke
Taurus, who took up the duties, robbed of the helm.

A Cumaean ship breaks in, which the leader Corbulo
and a chosen youth from the Stabian shore filled.
The divinity of the lofty stern was neighbouring Dione.
But, because, set lower, it was nearer to the weapons heaped upon it,
as it took up the wars, it sank in the midst of the wave
and divided the strait. Foaming Nereus fills
the mouths of those crying out with his waters, and their palms,
the deep dragging them, struggling in vain, stand out on the topmost billows.
Here Corbulo, bold in anger, having crossed the blue with a great leap
(for triremes, bound by an iron joint, had brought up
a tower woven of oak),
escapes upon the decks and from the high summit shakes
the pine glowing and many-cloven with flame.
Then, eager, the South Wind driving the thong, he hurls
black firebrands fed with bitumen at the Carthaginians' stern-ornaments.
The Vulcanian plague enters, spread everywhere,
and fills the scattered gangways: there is panic, the rowing
abandoned by those above, but indeed in such a strait of affairs
the report of so great an evil had not yet reached those below.
But the swift heat, gliding through the torches greasy with pitch,
roars with victorious flames through the hull.
Yet where the Dardan brand had not yet brought its force
and the vapour spared, dire Himilco with a hail of stones
kept it off and held back the fate of the ship.
Here a wretch, while he fans the fire-bearing pine in the air,
struck by a wall-stone over the timbers slippery with blood,
Cydnus, rolls into the waves with the wound of Lycchaeus.
The torch with its heavy stench fouled the air close by,
hissing on the scorched sea. Then fierce Sabratha, having adored
its stern, hurls a swift missile
(Ammon was the native divinity of the Libyan keel,
and, sitting, gazed upon the blue with horned brow):
"Bring, father, bring to our afflicted cause," he cries, "Garamantian seer,
your help, and grant us to pour sure weapons upon the Italians."
Amid these words the cornel-spear came with quivering flight
and transfixed the face of Neptune-worshipping Telon.

No lighter did they press, now on the threshold of death,
those whom flight of the headlong ship had gathered into one
part, still free of the brand. But the inevitable blaze,
having ravaged the nearest in its lightning course,
seized the alder and wrapped it in exulting flames.
First, slipping into the waves by the help of a sea-rope,
where Mulciber did not yet gather his Stygian heats,
the scorched Himilco is borne off by his comrades' oars.
The nearest fates laid bare the ship of pitiable Bato,
deserted by its leader. He was good by skill
to wrestle with the raw sea and to ride out the storms.
He too foreknew what Boreas, what tomorrow's South Wind
would want, nor could you, Cynosura, deceive his ever-watchful
gaze, though by however dark a course.
He, after there was no measure to his adversities, "Receive our
blood, Ammon," he said, the witness of an unjust disaster.
And, the sword driven into his breast, then with his right hand
he catches the gore and lavishly pours it between the sacred horns.

Among these was Daphnis, his name drawn from an ancient
origin, the unlucky one, to whom it seemed good to leave the glades
and to change his huts for the faithless marble of the sea.
But the first of his race, how much greater names did he prepare
for himself within the bounds of a shepherd! The Sicilian Muses
loved Daphnis, propitious Phoebus gifted him with the Castalian
reed-pipe and bade that, cast down on the grass,
if ever he sang, the glad flocks should hasten to Daphnis
through the meadows, through the fields, and the streams be silent.
He, when, having tuned his song on the sevenfold reed,
he soothed the woods, never at the same time
did the Siren pour her wonted songs upon the sea,
the dogs of Scylla were silent, black Charybdis stood still,
and the Cyclops gladly heard the jubilation on the crags.
The flames consumed both his offspring and his lovely name.
Behold, over the smoking benches floats fierce
Ornytus, and makes for himself a long death on the sea,
as the son of Oileus, when Minerva hurled the thunderbolt,
mastered the rising waves with burning arms.
Marmaridian Sciron is run through the middle by the strong
point of an alder, as he raises himself. Part of his limbs
swims beneath the wave, part stands out from the waters, and over the whole sea
he is borne, dead — pitiable — on the rigid beak.
The ships hasten on both sides, and bloody dews of the oars'
spray strike the faces of those who rush on.
The Rhoetean leader himself went amid the foremost
by his strokes and overcame the winds with a strong oarsman.
While Lilaeus tries to rein in the ship with swift
hands, his limbs are lopped off by the savage axe,
and the swift ship bears his palms clinging to the timbers.
The Sicanian benches bore Podaetus, son of Aeolus.
He, though he had not yet passed in age the ephebes,
whether the spiteful gods drew him, or his fervid heart
and his desire for praise and war, not ripe enough,
had fitted painted arms to his snowy arms, a boy,
and rejoiced to trouble the straits in the lofty Chimaera.
And now over the Rutulian, over the Garamantian pine too
he went exulting, better at the oar and better at the arrow,
and now had sunk towered Nessus in the sea.
Alas, that new glory of battle ill-counsels the raw boy!
While he prays to the gods for the crest of a fierce helmet and the spoils
of the leader Marcellus, rashly, a swiftly loosed
spear caught him with a deadly wound,
ah what a one! Whether by his effort he should drive
the gleaming discus beneath the stars, or with the javelin rise
above the clouds, or bear his winged feet over dust
scarce touched, or, swift, cross with a leap the measured-out spaces
of the wide field, his labours became him.
Enough surely, enough there was of glory in safe contest,
enough of praise. Why, boy, did you seek greater deeds?
Him, when the weapons drove him gliding beneath the waves,
his shipwrecked bones cheated of a Syracusan tomb,
the straits wept, the Cyclopian rocks wept,
and Cyane and Anapus and Ortygian Arethusa.

In another part Perseus (Tiberinus drove this ship)
and Io, on which Sidonian Crantor was borne,
clash together. Bonds of iron, thrown on, bind them here
and there, and the ships stood, joined, for battle.
Nor do they contend with javelin or with reed shot from afar:
hand to hand and with the sword they mingle land-battles.
The Italians break through, where the first slaughter opened
and showed the way; but with vast effort he urges his comrades
to break the chains and the bonds of iron,
and prepares, the hostile ship released, to carry off the captives
and to draw them apart on the sea from their equal arms —
Polyphemus had been reared in the Aetnaean cave
and from that loved the name of ancient savagery.
A she-wolf had given him her udders as a babe; a terrible
mass of lofty body, a harsh mind, a face ever in anger,
and Cyclopian lust in his heart for slaughter.
And he, the bonds loosened from the weight of his limbs,
had driven on the ship and was sinking the oars in the flood
and would have led the ship away, had not Laronius swiftly
fixed him with his pressed spear to the hard cross-bench
as he strove to rise. Scarce does he, his death begun, release it:
for while his languid hand keeps its wonted motions,
he drew the idle oar slack over the surface of the sea.

The Carthaginians, struck by the wedge, are crowded into one
side, which is free of the enemy, when, conquered by the sudden weight,
the sea leaping in, the hull is submerged beneath the waves.
The shields of the men and the crests and the darts with idle steel
and the guardian gods float. Here, the timber broken,
one fights bereft of steel and arms himself again for battle
with the wreck; that one, ill-fervid, with oars
hastens to despoil the ship, with no distinction
sometimes whirling the wrenched-up benches of the sailors.
No quill of a ship, no prow to be broken for wounds
is spared, and the floating weapons are sought again from the sea.
With gaping wounds the sea enters. Soon, his life
sobbing into the deep, he is given back, driven by his own wave.
Nor are there lacking those who, seizing men in close
embraces, plunge them in the sea and, the javelins ceasing, destroy
the enemy by their own death. The minds of those returning
through the flood grow savage, and in place of steel they resolve to use the waves.
The bloody whirlpool swallows the twisted corpses.
Here a shout, there groans and deaths and flights
and the crash of oars and beaks sounding with collision.
The sea, drenched in war, seethes. Weary, with bitter
loss, Himilco turned his back to the Libyan shores in swift
flight, stealing himself away in a slender keel.

At last both Greek and Libyan yielded the sea,
and now the captive ships are led to the shore in bonds
in a long line. Others stand in the deep with their brands
blazing. Mulciber shines on the gleaming deep,
and the sea is set quivering with a trembling image.
Cyane, known to the straits, burns, and the winged Siren,
Europe too burns, borne by Jove in the form of a snowy
bull and crossing the seas, her horn grasped,
and she who, her hair flowing, over the curving blue a Nereid
guides the dewy fish with a wet rein.
The sea-roaming Python is burned, and horned Ammon,
and she who bore the Sidonian face of Elissa,
ranging through the waves in twice three banks of oars.
But Anapus is dragged in bonds to his kindred shores,
and Pegasus, bearing the Gorgonian wings to the stars.
The ship marked with the shape of Libya is led,
and captive Triton and Aetna steep with its crags,
the pyre of breathing Enceladus, and Cadmean Sidon.

Then there would have been, with this disaster, no delay
to break into the trembling walls and bear the gods' standards
to the temples now at any moment, had not suddenly an unseasonable
sickness and a hostile plague, prepared by the envy of the gods
and the toil of the sea, snatched away the wretched men's joys
with a polluted sky.
The hairy Titan with summer fervours filled the air
and the broad Cyane, stagnant far and wide with its marshy
waters, with the Stygian smell of Cocytus,
and fouled the season of autumn flowering with glad gifts
and kindled it with the swift fire of a thunderbolt.
The thick air smoked with the mists of gloom,
the burning earth lay foul with its tainted surface
and gave neither sustenance nor any shade to the languishing,
and a black vapour breathed out into the pitchy air.
The dogs first felt the force. Soon, beneath the black clouds,
the bird, failing, fell with sinking wing.
Then the beasts were laid low in the woods. Then the Tartarean
blight crept on and ravaged the camp, its companies drained.
The tongue grew dry, and a cold sweat through the vitals
flowed from the trembling body. The dry throats
refused to let down the sustenance of food commanded.
A harsh cough shakes the lung, and through the panting mouths
of the thirsty a fiery breath is exhaled.
The eyes, scarce able to bear the heavy light,
lie under a hooked nostril, and gore mixed with blood
is spat out, and the skin covers the bones on the consumed limbs.
Alas the grief! The warrior, marked in famous arms,
is snatched away by an inglorious death. Into the fire are thrown
the proud gifts of the men, won by much war.
Medicine yielded to the evils. They are heaped in a pile
of the falling, and the ashes rise in a great mound.
Everywhere too lie bodies deserted and unburied far and wide,
those who fear to have touched the plague-bearing limbs.
The Acherusian plague creeps on, growing by feeding,
nor with lighter grief does it shake the Trinacrian walls
and bears an equal labour, black, to the Carthaginian camp.
The destruction is made equal, and everywhere common
is the anger of the gods, and the same image of death is turned about.

Yet no harsh force of the evils broke the Latin
men, their leader unharmed, and one head, safe amid the slaughter,
made amends for the disaster. So when grievous
Sirius first checked its death-bringing heats,
and the plagues lessened the contagions of greedy death,
as, when the South Wind sinking, the deep seas settle again,
the fisherman drives his boat and enters the blue,
so at last Marcellus arms the youth snatched from the ravaging
diseases, his companies duly purified.
Eager, they stand round the standards, and at the heard clangour
of the trumpets they breathe again, glad. They march against the enemy,
and, if the fates should bring it, it is a joy amid battles to be able
to die by the steel. They pity their comrades, who by a shameful lot,
in the manner of cattle, poured out in dark beds
an unpraised life. They look back on the mounds and dishonoured tombs,
and it pleases them to have lain even in no sepulchre
rather than to be vanquished by diseases. The leader first
snatches the steep standards to the walls. Their faces, thinned
by lying ill and by leanness, they hide in their helmets, and the evil
pallor is veiled by the helmet, lest there be hope for the enemy.
They pour the swift column upon the shattered walls
and rush packed together: roofs untrodden by so many wars
and so many citadels are taken by one entrance of the soldiery.

Through the whole world, where Titan is borne, no
towns then would have matched themselves to the Isthmian dwellings.
So many shrines of the gods and so many harbours within the walls,
add the forums and the theatres raised on lofty columns
and the moles contending with the sea, add in a long line
the countless houses, and the proud country fields equalling them
in space. What of the sacred groves enclosed by long
porticoes on a stretched-out boundary, for the contest of the young men?
What of the summits gleaming with so many captured beaks
and the arms fixed for the gods, or those which the Marathonian enemy
lost or which were brought from conquered Libya?
Here the seat adorned with the trophies of Agathocles,
here the mild wealth of Hieron; here the sacred antiquity
of the craftsmen's hands. Nowhere brighter than in that
age was the glory of painting; nor did the bronze delight
which Ephyra knew; the cloth that brought back breathing faces
in its woof would have vied with tawny gold —
the cloth that Babylon engraves with the shuttle, or that
glad Tyros with painted purple, and those which, varied by art
in Attalic hangings, are written with the needle or on Memphite loom.
And now together cups gleaming with silver, with which
mixed gems sought their honour, images of the gods
keeping the divinity given by art; besides, the gifts of the Red
sea and the fleeces combed from the boughs,
a womanish vanity. Having gained these roofs and riches,
the Ausonian leader, after, lofty from the high
rampart, he had looked down on the city trembling with clangour,
and saw that it was placed in his nod whether the walls of kings should stand
or the dawning light tomorrow should see no walls,
groaned at his excessive power and shuddered that so much
was permitted, and, the soldier's anger quickly recalled,
bade the houses stand, granting that the temples be dwelt in
by the ancient gods and inhabited. So to spare the conquered
was in place of plunder, and Victory, content with herself
and polluted by no blood, clapped her wings.
You too bore the leader's tears, you to be remembered,
defender of your country, meditating figures in the dust,
nor troubled in mind, though so great a ruin struck.
But the rest of the crowd, their minds loosed into joy,
the conquered vied with the conquerors. He himself, a rival
of the gods in genius, by his saving founded the city.
Therefore there stands for the ages, and shall stand, a famous trophy
and will give men to know the ancient ways of leaders.
Happy peoples, if, as once wars were wont,
so now too our peace would leave the cities unexhausted!
But, did not the care of the man who has now given leisure to the world
ward off the unbridled fury of laying all things waste,
greedy plunderings would have stripped the lands and the sea.
