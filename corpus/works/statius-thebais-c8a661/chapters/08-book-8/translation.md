8. When the seer suddenly fell among the pale shades,
and broke into the death-bringing homes and the secrets of the buried world,
and troubled the ghosts with an armed funeral,
horror holds them all; on the Stygian shores they marvel
at his weapons and his horses and his strange body; for not with limbs
buried by fire nor black did he come from the mournful urn,
but warm with the sweat of war, his shield sprinkled with the bloody
gates and the dust of the riven plain,
nor yet had the Eumenis with a lopped yew met and purified him,
nor had Proserpina marked him at the dark doorpost
as taken into the company of the dead; nay, the very distaff
of the Fates, caught at close hand, and the Parcae, terrified
at the seen augur, then at last were breaking their threads.
Him even the untroubled Elysians looked round at, that crash,
and those whom, far off in the deeper pit,
a second night and a region blind with other darkness weighs down,
then the sluggish lakes groan again and the burnt marshes,
and the pale ferryman of the shadow-bearing wave roars
that Tartarus has burst wide open with a new chasm of the earth
deep within, and that ghosts have entered not by his streams.

By chance, sitting in the midst of the citadel of his unhappy realm,
the lord of Erebus was demanding of the peoples the crimes of their life,
pitying men in nothing and wrathful at all the shades;
the Furies stand around, and various Deaths in order,
and savage Punishment thrusts out her many-sounding chains;
the Fates bring the souls and condemn them with the same thumb:
the work overwhelms. Beside him good Minos with his revered brother
advises better laws and tempers the bloody king;
they stand by, swelling with tears and fire,
Cocytus and Phlegethon, and Styx convicts the perjuries
of the gods. But he, when the upper frame was loosed,
unwont to feel fear, was terrified at the stars that arose,
and, offended at the pleasant light, declares:
'What downfall of the gods has driven against Avernus the hostile
upper air? Who has burst the darkness and warns the silent ones
of life? Whence the threats? Which of you brings me these battles of brothers?
I close with him; come, let the distinctions of things perish,
for to whom are they sweeter? A third Fortune cast me down,
conquered, from the great pole, and I keep the guilty
world: nor is even that mine, and lo, pierceable to the dread stars
it is looked into. Does the swollen ruler of Olympus
explore my strength? I have already the shattered chains of the Giants
and the Titans eager to go out beneath the upper
axle, and my wretched father: why does savage rest
forbid me to endure my mournful ease and unquiet calm,
and to hate the lost day? I will open all my realms,
if it please, and screen Hyperion with a Stygian heaven.
The Arcadian, too — for why does my messenger go and come back
to both houses? — I will send out, and hold both
sons of Tyndareus. But why do I break Ixion
on the greedy wheels? Why do the waters not wait for Tantalus?
Or shall I endure chaos so often profaned by a living
guest? The rash ardour of Pirithous tries me,
and Theseus, sworn to his daring friend,
and fierce Alcides, then when, the guardian removed,
the iron thresholds of the Cerberean gate fell silent;
to Odrysian complaints, too — for shame, alas! — Tartarus
lay open: I myself saw, amid beguiling songs, the base
tears of the Eumenides and the renewed spinning of the Sisters;
me too — but better is the violence of a hard law.
Yet I scarce dared one journey by theft, and not to the lofty
stars, and snatched my marriage on the Sicilian plain:
they say it was not even lawful; and at once unjust laws
came from Jove, and a mother reckons against me the year cut in two.
But why do I say these things? Go, avenge the Tartarean abodes,
Tisiphone; if ever fiercest with new monsters,
bring forth a sad, unwonted, vast crime, which the aether has not yet seen,
that I may marvel at it and the Sisters envy.
And indeed the brothers — and let these be the first omens
of my hatred — let the brothers rush upon each other's wounds in glad
War; let there be one who, after the manner of raging beasts,
fiercely chews the hostile head, and one who keeps the dead
from the last fire and defiles the upper air with naked
ghosts: let it delight me to watch the fierce Thunderer at these.
Besides, that my frenzy may not vex my realms alone,
seek one who bears war against the gods, who repels the fires of the bolt
and hostile Jove with a smoking shield.
I will make the dread of moving black Tartarus no lighter to all
than to join leafy Pelion to Ossa.'
He had spoken; and to him for long now the gloomy palace
trembled as he asked, and the earth, his own and that which presses
from above, was nodding: not more strongly does Jupiter
twist the upper air with his face and tilt the star-bearing axles.

'But what ghosts,' he says, 'are you, who headlong by an unlawful
path rush through the void?' That one approaches, threatening him,
now thin to sight, now with vanishing arms,
now on foot: yet in his quenched face the unplucked
honour of his augury endures, and on his brow the dim
fillet remains, and he holds a branch of dying olive.
'If it is allowed, and here it is right for the holy ghosts to open
their mouths, O greatest finisher of all things,
yet to me, who once knew the causes and the elements,
and sire, I pray, soothe your threats and your goaded heart,
nor deem worthy of your wrath a man who fears your laws too,
for not to Herculean — whence have I these battles? — rapines,
nor to unlawful love — believe these insignia — daring
do we enter Lethe: lest grim Cerberus flee into his cave,
or Proserpina fear my chariot,
an augur, but lately most beloved at Apollo's altars,
I call to witness empty chaos — for why should Apollo here be sworn by? —
by no crime do I undergo a new fate, nor have I so
deserved to be snatched from the kindly light; the urn of the Dictaean
judge knows it, and Minos can detect the truth,
sold by my wife's snares and unjust gold,
the Argive lines — whence comes to you this throng of fresh
shades, and the dead of my right hand come too —
not ignorant I entered: a sudden whirl of the world —
my mind still shudders — drank me down from the midst of the thousands,
your night. What thoughts were mine, while through the hollow bowels of the earth
I went, long hanging, and was rolled in the covered air?
Ah me! nothing of me was left to my comrades and country,
nor taken at Thebes; now I shall not see the Lernaean
roofs, nor even as ash shall I go to my astonished parent,
not by tomb, not by fire, wretched, brought forth with the tears
of my own, I have come to you in a whole funeral at once,
about to dare nothing with these horses; nor do I beg
to be received as a shade and to remember my tripods no more.
For what use is there now of a foreseeing augur to you,
when the Parcae draw your commands? But may you bend your heart
and be kinder, I beg, than the gods. If ever the unspeakable
wife shall come here, for her reserve the deadly
torments: she, good ruler, is worthier of your wrath.'
He receives the prayers and is indignant at being moved.
As a lion, when the light has stood opposite, meeting the steel,
then he rouses his wraths, then his arms; if the foe has fallen,
it is enough to go above him and leave life to the vanquished.

Meanwhile, distinguished with fillets and rich laurel,
the chariot, and he but lately dreaded in his excellent arms
openly in the light, scattered by none and put to flight by none,
is sought: the squadrons stand off, and the ground is suspect
to all, the soldier circles the tracks of the faithless
plain, and that sad spot of greedy ruin
they avoid, and shun it with the honour of an infernal tomb.
A messenger to Adrastus, urging on his companies in another part,
Palaemon, scarce himself believing he had seen it,
flies up and, trembling — for by chance he had stood next to the falling man
and, wretched, grew pale at the chasm he had looked upon —
'Turn your step, flee, ruler,' he says, 'if at least the Doric
land remains in its place and our fathers' citadels stand where we left them,
there is no need of arms, no need of blood: why do we draw the useless
steel against Thebes? The impious earth swallows chariots
and arms and warring men; behold, even this field
on which we stand seems to flee. I myself saw the journey
of the deep night and, the frame of the soil broken, rushing down
that man, alas, to whom none was friendlier to the foretelling stars,
the son of Oecles, and in vain I stretched out my hands with my voice.
A wonder I tell: the ruler of horses left even now his furrows
and the smoking place and the fields wet with foam.
Nor is the evil shared: the earth recognizes its nurslings,
the Theban line stands.' Adrastus is amazed at this and hesitates
to believe; but Mopsus told the same, and trembling
Actor the same, now the rumour, bold with new terrors,
reports that not one has fallen; of their own accord the columns backward,
not waiting for the manner of the recalling trumpets,
hurry: but the journey is numb, and their failing knees deceive
the rushing men; the steeds themselves — you would think they had felt it —
resist, and grim obey at no urging,
nor hasten their pace nor lift their eyes from the ground.
More bravely the Tyrians charge, but shadowy evening
now leads the moon's horses; given on a small compact,
sad rest is granted the men, and night to increase their fears.

What is now your aspect, after leave to mourn
was granted? What weeping fell from the loosened helmets?
Nothing accustomed pleases the weary; they cast away their dripping
shields, just as they were, nor did anyone wipe his spears,
nor praised his horse, nor combed and adorned
the tall plume of his bright helmet; scarce does it please them to wash
their great wounds and to bind up the gaping gasps:
so great is the grief everywhere; tables and the nourishment owed
to war neither fear nor the battle counselled: all,
Amphiaraus, recall your praises and your breast fertile
in truth with tears, and through the tents there is one
talk: that the gods have departed and the powers slipped from the camp.
'Alas, where now the laurel-bearing chariot and the solemn arms
and the filleted crest of the helmet? Is this the caverns and the lakes
of Castalia and the faith of the tripods? Is Apollo thus grateful?
Who for me will read the falls of the stars and the meaning of the sinister
lightning, or what power leaps in the slain entrails,
when to journey, whence the delays, what hour is useful for savage arms,
what would rather wish for peace? Who now will set forth all
the future, or with whom will the birds speak my fates?
These chances of war too, both to us and to you,
you had foreknown, and — how great the valour beneath that sacred breast! —
yet you came and were added as a companion to our wretched arms,
and when the earth and the fatal hour called you,
you found time to lay low the Tyrian lines and the opposing
standards; then too, even amid death, dread
to the foes and with hostile spear we saw you departing,
and now what chance holds you? Will you be able to return
from the Stygian abodes and burst forth from the deep earth?
Or do you sit, glad, beside your own powers, the Parcae,
and with concordant turn learn the things to come and teach?
Or has the ruler of Avernus, pitying you, given you
the happy groves and the Elysian birds to keep?
Whatever you are, you will be Phoebus's eternal grief and a new disaster
always, and long mourned at silent Delphi;
this day shall mock Tenedos and Chryse and Delos bound
by birth, and the inner shrines of unshorn Branchus,
nor on this day shall any suppliant consultant approach the Clarian
doors and the Didymaean thresholds, nor the Lycian.
Nay, even the horned seer's grove and the oak,
panting for Molossian Jove, and Trojan Thymbra shall be silent,
the very rivers and the very laurels will wish to grow dry,
the very aether will foretell nothing sure by foreboding
clangours, and no clouds shall be struck by any bird.
And now there will be that day on which a temple, conscious of fate,
shall worship you too, and your priest give back responses.'
Such solemnities they perform for their oracular king,
as though they were rendering flames and gifts to a pyre and sad
obsequies, and laying his soul in the soft earth.
Thence the breasts of all were broken and turned from war:
so when sudden death robbed the brave Minyae of Tiphys,
the arms seem not to follow, the oar seems not to bear
the waters, and the very winds now lead them less.
Now wearied with groaning, little by little their hearts too were lightened,
and grief, drained by talk, eased; added night
overwhelmed their cares, and sleep, easy to creep upon their tears.

But not the same night through the Sidonian city
in the other part: with varied sport they prolong the stars
before the houses and within, and the very watches
droop at the walls; twin bronzes sound and the Idaean drums
and the boxwood pipe with its sound measured by varied breathing.
Then everywhere paeans resound the sweet gods above and every
nursling power in order, everywhere
garlands and crowned wine; now they laugh at the funeral
of the augur, ignorant of one, and in turn vie to extol in season
their own Tiresias; now they unfold the deeds
of their ancestors and sing ancient Thebes from its origin:
these the Sidonian sea and the horns worn by the hands
of the Thunderer and Nereus furrowed by the huge bull,
these Cadmus and the wearied heifer and the fields teeming
with bloody Mars, others the rocks creeping to the Tyrian
lyre and Amphion giving life to the hard crags,
these gravid Semele, those praise the Cytherean
marriage and Harmonia led down with the many torches
of the brothers: now there is no table that lacks its tale,
as though Liber, having just laid waste gem-bearing Hydaspes
and the Eastern homes with his thyrsus, the standards of his black triumph
and the Indians unknown to the peoples, were displaying them.

Then for the first time, they say, to the gatherings and to the compacts
of the shared table, ever unseen and lurking in his dread
abode, Oedipus went forth, and with serene face
loosed with his hand his black-with-squalor white hair
and his face foul with flowing locks, and endured the kindly
addresses of his companion and the consolations he had thrust off before,
nay, drank in the feasts and cast from his cheeks
the unwiped gore, and hears and tells back all,
he who was wont to assail with mournful complaints only Dis
and the Furies, and, when she governed, Antigone;
the cause lies hidden: not the favourable chance of the Tyrian war,
only wars delight him; he urges and approves his son,
nor would he wish him to conquer; but the first swords at close quarters
and the seeds of crime he searches out with a silent prayer.
Thence the feasts are sweet and joys unknown to his face,
as Phineus after the fasts of his long punishment,
when he felt the birds driven off, shrieking no more in his home —
not yet was his trust complete — , cheerful handled the tables
and the couches and the cups not troubled by wild wings.

The rest of the Greek cohort lay wearied with cares
and arms; from the high mound of the camp Adrastus
with anxious heart caught the joyful uproar,
though sick with age, but a pitiable power drives him
to keep watch over evils; the bronze clamour everywhere
and the Theban sounds gall him, and the bitter pipe
provokes, then the voices proud with excessive languor
and the wavering torches and the fire now ill-kept through the night,
so when, overwhelmed by a single sleep, a ship over the waves
has grown silent, and the youth, careless, to the peace of the sea
have entrusted their souls: alone on the stern stands the master,
ever-wakeful, and the god who sails in the inscribed pine.

It was the time when now the fiery sister of Phoebus
felt her horses yoked, and that deep beneath the prepared light
the hollow home of Ocean was bellowing, and gathers herself
wandering and lightly with shaken lash puts the stars to flight:
the king calls a sad council, and groaning they ask
who as successor shall drive the tripods, to whom the foreseeing laurel
shall pass and the bereaved honour of the fillet; without delay, all
want Thiodamas, famed and sprung from holy Melampus,
with whom Amphiaraus himself was wont to share the secrets
of the gods and to associate with him alone the birds he saw,
and, not grudging so great an art,
rejoiced to be called his like and second next to himself.
A vast honour confounds that man and unlooked-for glory
troubles him, and lowly he adores the offered foliage,
and denies he is equal to the burden and deserves to be compelled:
just as an Achaemenian boy, if by chance he has received
the throne and his father's nations, for whom it were safer
that his father lived, weighs his joys with uncertain fear,
whether the chiefs are faithful, lest the crowd fight with the reins,
to whom he should entrust the bank of the Euphrates, to whom the Caspian gates;
then he fears to take up the bow and to load
his father's very horse, and seems to himself neither to support the sceptre
with capacious hand nor yet to fill out the tiara.

And when, his hair marked with the twisted fillet,
he had assembled before the gods, with glad uproar through the camp
he goes exulting and, the first proof of himself, the priest
prepares to appease Earth: nor did it seem futile to the mournful
Greeks; so he bids twin altars be woven
with living trees and with grown turf,
and adds countless flowers, the goddess's own gifts,
and heaps of fruits and whatever the busy year renews,
and, sprinkling the altars with untouched milk,
he begins: 'O eternal creatress of men and gods,
who beget the rivers and the woods and the seeds of souls and all things
in the world, and the hands of Prometheus and the stones of Pyrrha,
and who first gave nourishment to the unfed
and changed them into men, you who girdle and bear the sea:
within your keeping are both the gentle race of cattle and the wrath
of wild beasts and the rest of the birds; the firm and immovable strength
of the unsetting world, you the swift engine of heaven
carries hanging in the empty air, you both chariots
circle about, O middle and undivided amid the great
brothers! Therefore at once kindly to so many nations, to so many lofty
cities and peoples, beneath and above one
you suffice, and you yourself bear star-bearing Atlas, labouring
to carry the supernal homes, with no labour:
us alone you refuse to bear, us, goddess, do you grudge?
What crime, I pray, do we, ignorant, pay for? Or is it because, a foreign
people, we come hither from the Inachian shores?
Every native soil is a man's own, nor would it befit you, best one,
to mark off the peoples with so savage and so lowly a bound,
yours on every side and everywhere; remain shared, and bear arms
on this side and that; let it be allowed, I pray, in the order of war
for fighting souls to breathe out and to be given back to heaven,
do not snatch breathing bodies to such sudden tombs,
do not hasten: for we shall come, by what path all,
by what way it is allowed to go; only, won over for the Pelasgians,
make the field firm for the light, nor outrun the swift Parcae.
But you, dear to the gods, whom no hand nor Sidonian
swords, but great Nature with loosened bosom,
as though she buried you, deserving, in a Cirrhaean chasm,
so embracing took to herself, grant, I pray, glad,
that I may know your prayers, and reconcile me to heaven
and to the altars that warn truly, and what you were preparing
to set forth to the peoples, teach me: I will bear you presaging
rites, and as interpreter of your power I will call upon you when Phoebus is absent.
He to me is more mighty than Delos and all Cirrha,
the place to which you rush is better than the shrines.' When these things were said,
he plunges in cattle black as the earth and dark
herds, and heaps up the welling sand with living things
and renders to the seer the semblance of his death.

Such things among the Greeks, when now against the Martial
horns, when already the bronze crash rouses the savage swords,
Tisiphone adds a bitter sound, shaking her hair
from the height of Teumesus, and sharpens the trumpets and mixes hisses:
Cithaeron is dazed at the unwonted clang,
drooping, and the towers that followed no such song;
now Bellona beats the trembling gates and the armed
thresholds, now Thebes is unbarred at her many hinges.
The horseman troubles the foot, the chariots block the hastening men,
as if the Greeks pressed at their backs: so for all the wings
are crowded at the gates and cling at the seven outlets.
By lot Creon goes to the Ogygian gate, the Neistan send
Eteocles, Haemon takes the lofty Homoloidan,
the Proetian Hypseus, the Electran poured forth tall
Dryas, the Hypsistan the band of Eurymedon shakes,
great-souled Menoeceus crowds the Dircaean summits.
As when Nile, secret in the pastures of the averted sky,
drinks with great mouth the Eastern frosts,
he splits the wealth of his springs and into seven open fields
bears his storms to the sea; deep within, the Nereids, put to flight,
have withdrawn and fear to meet the sweet brine.

But sad from there the Inachian youth move their step slowly,
chiefly the Elean and Lacedaemonian cohorts
and the Pylians; for they follow the sudden Thiodamas,
cheated of their augur, and have not yet come to their guide,
nor do your columns alone seek you, prince of the tripods:
the whole phalanx thinks itself bereft; lesser through the wings
that seventh crest stands forth. As in the clear aether a cloud
envious withdraws one star from the Parrhasian stars,
the honour of the Wain is maimed, nor does the axle stand stiff
with the same fire, its blaze cut off, and uncertain the sailors count their stars.

But now the wars call: supply other and new strength, Calliope,
and may a greater Apollo bend the lyre for me,
the fatal hour for the peoples who of their own accord demand it
the black day brings near, and, sent forth from the Stygian darkness,
Death enjoys the heaven and, flying, covers the warrior
plain and invites the men with her black chasm,
choosing nothing common, but those funerals worthiest
of life, the chief in years and in spirit she marks with bloody
nail;
and now upon the wretched all the Sisters' allotment
is torn, and the Furies have snatched the threads from the Parcae.
In the midst of the fields still with dry spear-point stands
the War-mighty one, and now to these, now to those he turns
his shield, rousing arms, and abolishes homes, marriages, sons.
Driven out is the love both of country and — what last leaves the mind —
of light; on the hilts and the spears wrath holds the hands
made ready, and the panting spirit strives beyond
the corselets, and the helmets tremble with the bristling of their plumes.
What wonder that the men grew hot? The steeds are inflamed
against the foe and wet the rotten ground with a snowy shower,
as if, mingled with their masters, they had put on the wraths
of those who sit them: so they chew the bridles, so they demand battles
with neighing, and lift their shoulders and rear the riders back.

And now they rush, and the first dust of the men begins
to clash, and driven on equal spaces from both sides
they approach and see the mid plain dwindle,
now shield by shields, boss is repelled by boss,
sword by threatening sword, foot by foot and spear-point by spear-point:
so the lines press struggling; alike the breaths smoke,
and the crests, brought near, gleam on the enemy's helmet.
Still fair the face of war: the cones stand on the heads,
the herds full of men, no chariot without its driver,
the arms in place, the shields gleam and the comely quivers
and the belts and the gold not yet made foul with gore.
But after rage and valour, prodigal of life,
sent forth their souls, not when the Kids set so greatly
does the Bear lash airy Rhodope with solid snow,
nor so great is the crash of Ausonia, when Jupiter from all
his citadel thunders, nor with so great a hail is the Syrtis shaken,
when black Boreas has brought the Italian rains to Libya,
they shut out the day with weapons, iron clouds stand
in the sky, nor does the air, crammed with javelins, suffice.
These perish by missiles cast, those by spears returning,
through the void run stakes and exchange mutual
wounds, spears run together, the sling rains hissing
stones, the swift bullets imitate the lightnings,
and the arrows, dread with more than one death,
and there is no room for weapons to fall to the ground: into bodies all
the iron falls; often, unknowing, they slay and fall.
Chance does the work of valour: now the throng recedes,
now presses, and by turns loses the ground and takes it,
as when threatening with winds and storms Jupiter loosens
the reins and afflicts the world with alternate whirlwind:
the line of heaven stands divided, now stronger is the storm
of the South Wind, now of Aquilo, until, the tempest fighting,
either this conquers with excessive waters, or that with clear sky.

The beginning of the battle: Asopian Hypseus repulsed
the Oebalian squadrons — for these, swelling with the great and ancestral
name, break with hard bosses the Euboean line —
the leader of the wedge, Menalcas, torn away.
Here a Laconian also in mind, nursling of a rough torrent —
nor did he shame his forebears — the spear that went beyond his breast,
lest the shame be in his back, drew back through both bone and bowels,
and with falling right hand returned it bloody to the foe:
the beloved Taygetus wanders over the cheeks of the dying man
and the breast praised in battle to its mother.
Phaedimus son of Iasus Dircaean Amyntas marks down
with his bow: alas, swift Parcae! now Phaedimus quivers
on the fields, and the sure bow of Amyntas is not yet silent.
From the shoulder Calydonian Agreus took the right hand
of Phegeus: that hand, wicked, holds its own steel in the earth
and moves it; passing by, Acoetes feared it lying scattered
among the weapons, and yet struck the maimed body.
Iphis fierce Acamas, Argus wild Hypseus drove down,
Pheres laid Abas low, and weeping their diverse wounds
Iphis the horseman, Argus the foot, Abas the charioteer lay.
Two Inachidae two of the blood of Cadmus,
hidden by helmets — the savage ignorance of war —
had struck down with the steel; but while they strip all the spoils
from the slain, they saw the horror, and each, mournful,
looks back at his brother, and alike they lament that they had erred.
Ion, dweller of Pisa, laid low Daphneus, dweller of Cirrha,
with their horses confounded; this one Jupiter praises from on high,
this one slow Apollo pities in vain.

Fortune lights up huge men on both sides
in opposing blood: Cadmean Haemon lays low and drives
the Greeks, raging Tydeus follows the Tyrian lines;
Pallas is present to this one, the Tirynthian fills that one,
as when in winter two streams on the highest mountains
are broken and fall in twin ruin into the plains:
you would think they contended which should lift fields and groves
higher, or overtop the bridges; lo, one valley
now confounds the gathered waters; each, proud,
wishes to go for himself, and mingled they refuse to descend to the sea.

Onchestian Idas went shaking his smoking
torch through the midst and troubled the Greek lines,
breaking a way with fire; him at close quarters with a great blow
the spear of fierce Tydeus, the helmet driven apart, fixed,
he falls huge upon his back, the lance stands surviving
on his brow, the flames come, collapsing, upon his temples,
Tydeus pursues: 'Do not call Argos savage;
with your own fire, Theban — we grant a pyre — burn!'
Thence, as a tigress, rejoicing in her first blood,
longs to go through the whole flock, so Aon with a stone,
Pholus with the sword, Chromis with the sword, the two Helicaons with the spear
he runs through, whom Maera, priestess of Aegaean love,
had borne, the goddess forbidding; you are the prey of bloody
Tydeus, and your mother goes even now to the savage altars.

No less on the other side does wandering blood lead
Herculean Haemon: with insatiate steel he is carried through the thousands,
now the swollen wealth of Calydon, now the grim lines
of Pylene, now pouring out the nurslings of mournful Pleuron,
until upon Olenian Butes with his now-wearied spear-point
he falls; him, turned toward the squadrons and forbidding him to depart,
he attacks; a boy was he, a boy whole in cheeks and hair,
to whom, unaware, then the Theban axe,
poised against his helmet, came: his temples are cleft
on both sides and the divided hair falls upon his arms,
and, not fearing this, by an unlooked-for threshold his life
leapt out. Then to the conquered he joins blond Hypanis and blond Polites —
that one fed his cheeks for Phoebus, this his hair for Iacchus:
savage is each god — Hyperenor, and Damasus turned in flight;
but the spear, slipped through the shoulders of the man,
goes across his breast and from the holder

Ismenian Haemon would still be laying low the opposing
Inachidae — for the son of Amphitryon guides his weapons
and supplies his strength — but against him savage Tydeus
Pallas drives. And now they have come with adverse favour
to close quarters, and first the Tirynthian with placid mouth:
'Faithful sister, what fortune in the war's darkness has brought
this meeting upon us? Surely royal Juno has not
contrived this abomination? Sooner let her behold me rush
against the bolts — unspeakable! — and war against my great
parent; his lineage — but I forbear to know it, since
you favour the other side, nor, if your Tydeus' spear
should seek Hyllus himself at close quarters, or Amphitryon
sent back from the Stygian world; I hold and shall hold forever
how much this divine hand has done, how often that aegis
has sweated for me, while as servant through hard mishaps I roam,
a wanderer over all lands; you yourself, alas, my companion,
would have gone with me into trackless Tartarus, did not Acheron
shut out the gods. You are country and heaven to me; who could equal
such things in the telling? Have all Thebes, if you have set your mind to it.
I yield indeed, and pray for pardon.' So having begun, he was departing.
The honour soothes Pallas: her face returned with ardour
slackened, and the serpents of her upright breast sank down.

Cadmean Haemon felt the god absent; more lightly
he whirls his weapons and knows his hand in no stroke.
Then more and more his strength and spirit recede,
nor is there shame to go back; the Acheloian hero attacks him
as he yields, and, balancing the missile weapon for himself alone,
directed his cast where, at the margin of the upper shield,
the lower helmet sits and the vital parts of the throat gleam.
Nor did the hand fail, and the spear had found death;
but Tritonia hinders it, and granted it to graze a little
the left shoulder, and deservedly spares her brother;
yet he dares neither to stand in his place nor to go to close quarters
any more, nor to endure the face of bloody
Tydeus; his strength, sick at heart, and his confidence gave way:
as a boar, grazed on his bristly brow by a Lucanian spear-point,
whose wounds are not sunk deep into the brain,
nor the aim of the right hand happy, breaks his wraths
into his side, and no longer comes to meet the proven spear.

Lo, indignant that the leader of a squadron with sure
darts aimed against the foe was hurling them, Prothous, with happy arm,
the tumultuous son of Oeneus with a single pine
strikes two bodies, both steed and rider: that one falls upon the falling
Prothous, and seeking the reins slipped from his hand
he tramples the helmet into his face and the shield into his breast,
until, wounded, with his last blood he spurns
the bridles and sinks with his neck joined to his master,
so an elm and a vine, a double loss to the husbandman,
fall from the Gauran mountain, but the elm, sadder,
mourns both the grove, nor does it grieve so much for its own falling
arms as for the accustomed and unwilling grapes it crushes.
Heliconian Corymbus had taken up arms against the Greeks,
once companion of the Muses, to whom, conscious of his Stygian doom,
Urania herself had long foretold his death from the stars laid by.
Yet he longs for battles and feats of strength,
perhaps that he might sing them; he himself lies to be sung
with long praise, but the mute sisters wept for him lost.

Betrothed from his earliest years to Agenor's daughter
Ismene, Atys went, a youth no stranger to the Tyrian war,
though his home was Cirrha, nor had he turned from his future kin
in their sad doings; nay, his chaste squalor commends the bride
to her lover, and the grace of an unworthy grief,
he himself too excellent, nor was the maiden's heart toward him
diverse, and in turn, would fortune allow, they pleased each other.
The wars forbid the bridal torches, and hence the youth's wrath
against the foes is greater; he rushes mingled with the foremost, and the lines
of Lerna now on foot with wandering sword, now lofty with seized reins,
as if to be watched, he drives. With triple purple his mother had veiled
his shoulders still rising and his smooth
breast; now with gold his trappings and with gold his arrows
and his belts and his sleeves, lest he go meaner than his bride,
she had wreathed, and crisped the gold mingled on his cone-crest,
trusting, alas, in such things, of his own accord he calls the Greeks to battle.
And first, having harried with his spear-point the easy squadrons,
he brings back arms to his comrades and to the trusty lines, the slaughter
accomplished, returns, as a Caspian lion in Hyrcanian shade,
still bare and with no honour of his tawny mane
terrible, and still guiltless of great blood,
not far from the stalls, the guard removed, catches
the sluggish flock and spends his hunger on a tender lamb.
Then, measuring Tydeus, unknown in arms, by his body alone,
he feared him not, and to provoke him with a fragile weapon,
grim against others and pursuing others,
he had often dared. At last the Aetolian, by chance,
turns his eyes to the feeble strokes, and with a formidable smile:
'Long since I see, wicked one, that you desire the great
name of death,' he says; at once, deigning the bold one
neither with sword nor spear, lightly with loosened fingers
he flung his unwarlike javelin: yet the missile drank deep
the hidden parts of the groin, as though twisted with all his strength,
and the son of Oeneus passes by, no doubt of his fate, and disdains
to despoil him. 'For these spoils we will not bear to Mars
or to you, warlike Pallas,' he says; 'let shame keep him afar
from bearing them; scarce, had she followed the war, her bridal chamber left,
would Deipyle have brought them to him to mock at.'
So he speaks, and to greater prizes of war in mind
he is led: as a lion, by chance master of countless
slaughters, passes by the unwarlike calves and soft heifers:
his frenzy is to be plunged in great blood,
nor to sink upon any neck but the reigning bull's.
But Atys, falling, does not, with his half-dead cry,
escape Menoeceus: he outstrips the horses and from his swift chariot
leaps down: the Tegean youth pressed upon the fallen man,
nor do the Tyrians hold them back. 'Be ashamed, Cadmean youth,
who feign earth-born fathers! Whither do you bend,' he says,
'degenerate ones? Does the guest Atys lie better for our blood?
Only a guest as yet, and avenger of a wife
not yet his, unhappy one; do we betray such great
pledges?' They rise up, strengthened by just shame,
in column, and to each his own cares returned into his breast.

Meanwhile in a secret part of the chamber the sisters,
another pair of character, and the harmless offspring of wretched
Oedipus, mingle in talk their various complaints,
not the evils that are near, but those from the long origin of fate,
this one their mother's marriage-torches, but that their father's eyes,
one mourns the reigning, the other the exiled brother,
both the wars, hence the heavy hesitation of a wretched prayer:
their fear nods both ways, whom in this contest conquered,
whom they would wish to have conquered: silently the exile preponderates.
So the Pandionian birds, when they seek again their faithful
lodgings and the home left when winter knocked,
and stand above their nests and tell to the roofs
the beginnings of their old fate, a maimed and tearful murmur goes;
they think them words, and yet that voice is not unlike words,
and there, after tears and long silences, again
Ismene begins: 'What error is this for mortals?
What deceived faith, that care should watch over rest
and that bright images of the mind should return through sleep?
Behold, I, who would not, even if deep peace remained,
handle thoughts of marriage — alas, for shame! — saw my wedding
by night, sister; whence did frantic sleep bring me a betrothed
scarce known by sight? Once in these abodes him,
while by I know not what compact my treaties were pledged,
I looked at, not willingly, sister, and suddenly troubled
I saw all things, and a sudden fire fell between,
and his mother followed me with raging cry, demanding back
her Atys. What omens are these of doubtful disaster?
Nor do I fear, so the house be safe and the Doric soldier
withdraw, and it be allowed to compose the swollen brothers.'
Such things they were weaving, when suddenly with an uproar the sluggish
house was terrified, and with much sweat, taken up,
Atys is borne, keeping his soul now with no blood,
his hand upon the wound, his languid neck hangs
outside the shield, and his hair thrown back from his brow.
First Jocasta sees him, and trembling called dear
Ismene: for this alone the dying voice begs,
this alone of her son-in-law, this name alone now wanders
on his cold jaws; the handmaids cry out, the maiden
was raising her hands to her face, savage shame held her; yet to go
she is compelled, Jocasta indulges the fallen man this last thing,
and shows and offers her. Four times now beneath death itself
at the name he raised his sight and his face faint,
yet bravely; her alone, the light of heaven neglected,
he beholds, and with the beloved face is not sated.
Then, because neither his mother is near nor his father, set
in blessed death, they hand to his betrothed the pitiable office
of closing his eyes; there at last, the witness removed,
she confessed her pious groans and poured tears into his eyes.

And while these things through Thebes, burning with other serpents
and with changed torch Enyo was renewing the war.
They want arms, as though they had but now taken the first strokes
at close quarters and every sword still gleamed,
the son of Oeneus stands forth, though both with sure arrow
Parthenopaeus may drive, and the faces of the dying
Hippomedon may trample with his raging horse, and the Capanean pine
already from afar fly, to be recognized by the Aonian bands:
that was the day of Tydeus, him they both flee and tremble at
as he cries: 'Whither do you give your backs? Lo, you may avenge
your slain comrades and repay the mournful night.
I am he who alone with insatiate slaughters drank
fifty lives: as many, as many, come, in flocks
bring your hands! Are there no fathers, no like-souled brothers
of the fallen? What so great forgetting of grief?
How it shames me to have departed content to Inachian Mycenae!
Hence upon Thebes? Are these the strengths of the king? But where
is that excellent leader of mine?' At once on the left wing
he sees him exhorting the wedges and marked by the gleam
of his proud head; nor less ardent
he runs to meet him than the fiery bird hangs over a snowy
swan and binds the trembler about with a great shadow.
Then he first: 'O most just king of the Aonian race,
do we go to arms openly and show our swords at last,
or does it please to await the night and the accustomed darkness?'
That one nothing in answer, but the hissing cornel-spear against the foe
goes bearing the leader's commands, which the provident hero,
now at the journey's end, struck askew, and himself
a huge weapon eagerly and with a stronger arm than ever before
hurled: the dread lance went, about to set an end to the duel;
on both sides the favouring gods turned their eyes,
Sidonian and Greek; the cruel Erinys
withstands it and defers Eteocles for his unspeakable brother:
the point erred against the armour-bearer Phlegyas; there a vast
battle of men, for the Aetolian rushed in fiercer with drawn
sword, and the Theban arms covered the leader given to the rear,
so a thick throng of shepherds, now under the dark night,
keeps off the wolf from the bullock it has seized;
the wicked beast rises against them, nor cares to attack
those who forbid him: him, him, against whom he had come, he presses.
Not otherwise the opposing lines and the lesser throng
he dissembles and passes by with his hand; yet the face of Thoas,
the breast of Deilochus, the side of Clonius, the flank of grim
Hippotades he pierces; sometimes he returns their limbs to the trunks
and whirls full helmets through the clouds.
And now with the bodies and spoils of the fallen
he had shut himself in; round one alone the line is consumed, against one
all the weapons make vows: these stick in the surface bones,
part fall frustrated, part Tritonia plucks away,
many stand stiff in the shield, now sown with thick spears
the boss shakes an iron grove, and on the back gapes
and on the shoulders the ancestral boar; nowhere the lofty
glory of the cone, and the crest of the grim helmet
which Gradivus held falls, no glad omen to its master: scorched
on his temples the bare bronze sits, and round about, sounding,
the millstones roll upon his arms, his head struck.
Now blood is in the helmet, now the dark torrent, mingled
with sweat and gore, washes his wounded breast,
he looks back at his exhorting comrades and faithful Pallas,
hiding her eyes farther off behind her opposed buckler:
for she was going to bend her great father with tears.

Lo, the ash-spear cuts the zephyrs, bearing huge wrath
and fortune; the weapon's author stands not forth:
Astacian Melanippus it was, nor did he himself betray it,
and he would have wished his hand to lie hidden, but the joy of the squadron
showed the trembler; for Tydeus, bent at the flank,
had loosened his sunken side and the orb of his shield.
The Aonians mix a shout and the Pelasgians a groan,
and throw out their hands and watch the indignant man.
He, far off through the men opposing, having searched out the bitter
Astacian, forces himself into the stroke with all
the remnants of his soul, and casts the weapon which next him Hopleus
had offered: the blood, pressed out by the effort, perishes,
then his sad comrades, as he longs to war — what ardour! —
and demands spears and in the midst of death refuses
to expire, drag him away, and at the margin of the high field
they lay him, propped, his sides on either hand leaning
against a twin buckler, and that he will return to the battles
of savage Mars they promise, weeping; but he himself felt heaven
recede and his vast spirits slip away in the last
cold, and, leaning on the earth, 'Pity me,' he cries,
'Inachidae: I pray not that my bones be borne back to Argos
or to my Aetolian home; for I have no care of the last
funeral: I hate these limbs and this fragile use of the body,
the deserter of the soul. A head, O a head, O if someone
would bring me yours, Melanippus! for you are rolled on the fields,
I trust indeed, nor has my last valour deceived me.
Go, I pray, if Atreus' blood is anything to you ever,
Hippomedon; go, O boy famed in your first wars,
Arcadian, and you, Capaneus, now greatest of the Argive squadron.'
All are moved, but first goes and first the found
Astacian Capaneus lifts from the midst of the dust,
breathing, and bears him back above on his left shoulder,
bloodying his back with the wave of the shaken wound:
as the Tirynthian returned from the Arcadian cave
and brought the captive boar to clamouring Argos.
Tydeus rises and runs to meet him, frantic
with joy and with wrath, when he saw the sobbing
face and the eyes dragged, and recognized himself in him,
he bids the severed head be held out, and, taken in his left hand,
fierce, he watches the hostile head, and his joy swells as he sees
the grim eyes of the warm thing and yet hesitating to be fixed.
The unhappy man was content: more the avenging
Tisiphone demands; and now, her father bent over,
Tritonia had come, and was bearing the wretched man immortal glory,
and she beholds him, drenched with the gore of the broken brain
and defiling his jaws with living blood —
nor can his comrades take it away — : the rough Gorgon stood
with hair sent forth, and the serpents, upright before her face,
veiled the goddess; she fled, turning from the prostrate man,
nor went up to the stars before the mystic torch and the guiltless
Elisos with much water had cleansed her offended eyes.
