1. Phoebus, and Diana, mistress of the woodlands,
bright glory of the sky, O ever to be worshipped
and ever worshipped, grant what we pray for
at this sacred season,

at which the Sibylline verses have admonished
chosen maidens and unblemished boys
to sing a hymn to the gods
to whom the seven hills are dear.

Kindly Sun, who in your shining chariot
bring forth the day and hide it, and rise again
another and yet the same — may you never
look upon anything greater than the city of Rome!

Gentle to bring to term the births now ripe,
Ilithyia, watch over our mothers,
whether you choose to be called Lucina
or Genitalis:

Goddess, raise up our offspring, and prosper
the decrees of the senators upon the wedding
of women and the marriage law,
fruitful of new children,

so that the fixed cycle of ten times eleven years
may bring back the songs and the games,
thronged three times by clear day and as often
by welcome night.

And you, Fates, truthful in your singing
of what has once been spoken — and let the steadfast
boundary of things preserve it — to fortunes now accomplished
join good fortunes still to come.

May the earth, rich in crops and cattle,
present Ceres with a crown of grain,
and may healthful waters and the breezes of Jove
nourish the new growth.

Mild and gentle, your weapon laid aside,
hear the suppliant boys, Apollo;
queen of the stars, two-horned,
hear, O Moon, the girls:

if Rome is your work, and if Trojan companies
held the Etruscan shore,
that band bidden to change their homes and city
in a saving voyage,

for whom, through burning Troy, with no treachery,
chaste Aeneas, surviving his fatherland,
made ready a free road, destined to give
more than was left behind:

Gods, grant to the teachable young an upright character,
gods, grant to peaceful old age its rest,
and to the race of Romulus give wealth and offspring
and every glory.

And whatever the renowned blood of Anchises
and of Venus entreats of you with white oxen,
let him obtain it — mightier than the warring foe,
gentle to the one laid low.

Now the Mede fears our hands, powerful
by sea and land, and the Alban axes;
now the Scythians, lately so proud, and the Indians
seek our answers.

Now Faith and Peace and Honour and old-fashioned
Modesty, and Virtue too long neglected,
dare to return, and blessed Plenty appears
with her full horn.

May Phoebus, the augur, splendid with his gleaming bow
and dear to the nine Muses,
who by his healing art relieves
the weary limbs of the body,

if he looks with favour upon the Palatine heights,
prolong the Roman state and prosperous Latium
into another cycle and ever
to a better age.

And may Diana, who holds the Aventine and Algidus,
heed the prayers of the fifteen men,
and lend friendly ears
to the vows of the boys.

That Jove and all the gods feel thus,
I carry home a good and certain hope,
I, a chorus trained to sing
the praises of Phoebus and Diana.
