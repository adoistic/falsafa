THE GHOST OF TANTALUS, A FURY

GHOST OF TANTALUS
Who drags me out from the accursed seat of the dead,
as I snatch with greedy mouth at the food that flees my reach?
What god, to my hurt, shows Tantalus the halls
of the gods once seen again? Has worse been found
than the parching thirst amid the waves, worse than the hunger
that gapes forever? Must Sisyphus' slippery stone
be carried on my shoulders now? Or the wheel
that tears the limbs apart in its swift career?
Or the punishment of Tityos, who, spread open in his vast cave,
feeds the black birds upon his torn-out entrails,
and by night repairing whatever he lost by day
lies, a full feast made fresh again for the monster?
To what evil am I transferred? O whoever you are,
harsh arbiter of shades, who deal out new
torments to the dead, if anything can be added to my pains
that the very keeper of the grim prison would dread,
that mournful Acheron would fear, at the terror of which
even we should tremble—seek it out: now from my stock
rises a throng that shall outdo its kind,
that shall make me innocent and dare deeds undared.
Whatever space lies idle in the impious region
I shall fill—never, while the house of Pelops stands,
shall Minos rest.

FURY
Go on, detestable
shade, and drive the impious household to madness.
Let them strive in every crime, and in turn
let the sword be drawn; let there be no measure to their wrath,
no shame; let blind frenzy goad their minds,
let the parents' rage endure, and let the long
sin pass down to the grandchildren; let none have leisure
to hate the old crime: ever let a new one rise,
and not one in one man, but while one crime is punished
let it grow. Let kingdoms fall from the proud brothers
and call the exiles back; let the uncertain fortune
of the violent house waver between unstable kings:
let the powerful become wretched, the wretched powerful,
and let chance bear the kingdom on its restless tide.
Driven out for their crimes, when a god gives back their country
let them return to crime, and be as hateful to all men
as to themselves; let wrath think nothing forbidden:
let brother fear brother and father his son,
and son his father; let children perish foully,
yet be born more foully still; let a hostile wife
threaten her husband; let them carry wars across the sea;
let blood, poured out, drench every land,
and over the great captains of the nations
let conquering Lust exult; in this impious house
let adultery be the lightest sin; let right and faith
and all law perish; let heaven itself not be
exempt from your evils—why do the stars gleam in the pole,
why do their fires keep the world its due splendour?
Let another night come, let day fall from the sky.
Confound the household; summon hatreds, slaughters, deaths,
and fill the whole house with Tantalus.
Let the high pillar be adorned, let the doors
grow green and joyful with laurel; let a fire worthy
of your coming blaze—let the Thracian crime be done
on a greater scale. Why is the uncle's right hand idle?
Not yet does Thyestes weep for his sons—
and when will he raise them up? Already the fires are lit;
let the cauldrons foam; let the limbs go piece by piece,
torn apart; let the blood pollute the ancestral hearths;
let the banquet be prepared—as guest to no new crime
you shall come; we have granted you a free day
and loosed your hunger to those feasts:
glut your fasting; let blood mixed in wine
be drunk while you look on; I have found dishes
that you yourself would flee—stop! where do you rush headlong?

TANTALUS
To the pools, the streams, the receding waters,
and the fleeing branches of the laden tree from my very lips.
Let me go back to the black bed of my prison,
let me, if I seem too little wretched,
exchange my banks: in the midst of your channel,
Phlegethon, let me be left, ringed round by your fiery flood.
Whoever you are who are bidden to suffer the pains
appointed by the law of fate, whoever lie
trembling beneath the gnawed-out cave and dread
the coming ruin of the mountain, whoever, entangled,
shudder at the savage gaping jaws of greedy lions
and the dread ranks of the Furies, whoever, half-burned,
beat back the hurled torches—hear the voice of Tantalus
who hastens to you: believe one who has known it,
love your pains—when shall it fall to me
to flee the world above?

FURY
First throw the household into confusion,
and bring with you battles and the cursed love
of the sword for kings; shake their savage breasts
with mad tumult.

TANTALUS
It is fitting that I suffer punishment,
not that I be a punishment. I am sent like a baleful vapour
through the broken earth, or like a plague scattered
to bring grievous pestilence on the peoples—shall I, the grandfather,
lead my grandsons into horrible crime? Great father of the gods
and ours—though it shame you—though my chattering tongue
be racked with mighty punishment for its words,
yet this I will not keep silent: I warn you, do not defile
your sacred hands with slaughter, nor sprinkle the altars
with the madness of the Furies. I will stand and bar the crime—
why do you thrash my face with the lash, and fiercely
threaten me with your twisted serpents? Why do you stir
the hunger fixed deep in my marrow? My heart is on fire,
kindled with thirst, and the flame flickers in my scorched entrails—
I follow.

FURY
This, this frenzy spread through the whole house!
So, so let them be carried on, and thirst, in mutual hatred,
each for the other's blood. The house feels your entry
and shudders, all of it, at your unspeakable touch.
Enough has been done; go to the infernal caverns
and the well-known stream; already the mournful earth
is weighed down by your tread: do you see how the spring's water,
driven inward, deserts its bed, how the banks lie empty,
and the fiery wind drives the scattered clouds?
Every tree grows pale, and the bared branch thirsts,
its fruit gone fleeing; and the Isthmus, which on this side
roars with the near waves, and on that—
the neighbouring sea, divided by a narrow strip of land—
now hears those sounds far off, in safety.
Now Lerna has shrunk back, the streams of Phoroneus
have hidden themselves; the holy Alpheus
brings forth its waves no more; the ridges of Cithaeron
stand nowhere white with their fallen snow,
and noble Argos dreads its old thirst.
See, Titan himself hesitates whether to bid the day go on
and force, with the reins, the day that is doomed to die.

CHORUS
If any of the gods loves Achaean Argos
and the Pisan halls famed for their chariots;
if any loves the realms of Corinth on the Isthmus,
the twin harbours and the parting sea;
if any loves the conspicuous snows of Taygetus,
which, when the Sarmatian North Wind in the cold season
has piled them on the highest ridges,
summer dissolves with the sail-bearing Etesian winds;
whom the bright Alpheus touches with its cold stream,
famed for the Olympic course—
let him turn his peaceful favour here, and forbid
that the alternating turns of crime return,
that no grandson succeed worse than his grandsire,
and that the lesser take no pleasure in greater guilt.
At last let the impious progeny of Tantalus,
weary, lay aside its savage impulses.
Enough has been sinned; right availed nothing,
nor common crime: betrayed, Myrtilus fell,
the betrayer of his lord; carried by that faith
he had kept, he gave to the sea, with its name changed,
a name renowned: no tale is better known
to the Ionian ships.
The little son, received upon the impious sword
as he ran to his father's kiss,
fell an untimely victim at the hearth
and was carved up by your right hand, Tantalus,
that you might set out feasts for the guest-gods.
Eternal hunger pursues this food,
eternal thirst; nor for the savage banquet
could a fitter penalty be decreed.
Weary Tantalus stands, his throat empty:
above his guilty head there hangs much spoil,
more fleeting than the birds of Phineus;
on this side and on that, with laden branches
the tree leans over him, and bowed with its own fruit
and trembling, it mocks his gaping mouth.
Though he is greedy and impatient of delay,
so often deceived, he neglects to touch it,
turns away his eyes, presses his lips together,
and binds his hunger fast with closed teeth.
But then the whole grove lets down its riches
nearer, and the ripe apples from above
make mockery among the drooping leaves
and kindle his hunger, which bids him stretch
his hands in vain—but when he has put them out
and it has pleased the fruit to cheat him, all the autumn
is snatched up on high, and the shifting woodland.
Then thirst presses, no lighter than the hunger;
when his blood has grown hot with it and burned
with fiery brands, the wretch stands reaching with his mouth
for the waters that come to meet him, which the fleeing stream
turns away, failing in its barren channel,
and deserts him as he tries to follow; here he drinks
deep of the dust from the rushing whirlpool.

CHORUS

ATREUS, AN ATTENDANT

ATREUS
Spiritless, sluggish, nerveless, and—what I count
the greatest reproach to a tyrant in supreme affairs—
unavenged: after so many crimes, after your brother's tricks
and all right torn away, you spend yourself, Atreus,
in idle complaints, though angry? By now the whole world
ought to be roaring with your arms, and a double sea
to be driving your fleets on either side; by now
it were fitting that the fields blazed with fire and the cities,
and the drawn sword flashed everywhere; let the whole
Argive land resound beneath our horsemen; let no woods
shelter the enemy, nor citadels built on the high
mountain ridges; let the whole people leave Mycenae
and sound the war-trumpet; whoever hides
and protects that hateful head, let him fall in deadly ruin.
Let this very mighty house of glorious Pelops
crash down even upon me, so it crash upon my brother.
Come, my soul, do what no posterity may approve,
but none keep silent: some crime must be dared,
atrocious, bloody, such as my brother
would rather have for his own—crimes are not avenged
except by surpassing them; and what can be so cruel
as to outdo that man? Does he lie cast down?
Does he keep, in prosperity, any measure,
or rest when weary? I know the man's temper,
untameable: it cannot be bent—but it can be broken.
Therefore, before he steadies himself or gathers strength,
let him be attacked first, lest he attack me while I rest.
He will either destroy or perish: between us the crime
lies ready for whoever seizes it first.

ATTENDANT
Does the unfavourable repute of the people
not frighten you at all?

ATREUS
This is the greatest good of kingship,
that the people are forced to bear the deeds of their lord
as much as to praise them.

ATTENDANT
Those whom fear compels
to praise, the same fear makes into enemies;
but he who seeks the glory of true favour
will wish to be praised by the heart rather than the voice.

ATREUS
True praise often falls even to a humble man;
false praise to none but the powerful—what they would not, let them wish.

ATTENDANT
Let the king will honourable things: everyone will will the same.

ATREUS
Wherever only honourable things are allowed to the ruler,
he reigns on sufferance.

ATTENDANT
Where there is no shame,
no care for right, no holiness, piety, or faith,
the kingdom is unstable.

ATREUS
Holiness, piety, faith
are the goods of private men: let kings go where it pleases them.

ATTENDANT
Count it a crime to harm even a wicked brother.

ATREUS
Whatever is a crime against a brother is right against him.
For what has he left untouched by crime, or where
has he spared from wickedness? He stole my wife by adultery,
my kingdom by theft; the ancient token of empire
he gained by fraud, by fraud he troubled my house.
There is in the high stalls of Pelops a noble beast,
a secret ram, the leader of a wealthy flock,
all through whose body a fleece of poured-out gold
hangs down, from whose back the new
kings of Tantalus bear their gilded sceptres;
its owner reigns, the fortune of so great a house
follows him, in safety. Set apart, the sacred beast
crops the meadows, which a wall of stone
encloses, guarding the fated pasture in its rocky bound.
This—daring a monstrous deed—my faithless brother
carried off, having taken the partner of my bed into the crime.
From this flowed all the evil of our mutual ruin:
through my own realms I wandered, a trembling exile;
no part of my line is safe and free from snares,
my wife corrupted, the faith of empire shaken,
my house sick, my blood in doubt—nothing certain
but that my brother is my enemy. Why do you stand amazed?
At last begin and take heart: look on Tantalus and Pelops;
their example summons my hands to such deeds.
Tell me, by what means may I slaughter that dread head.

ATTENDANT
Let him, struck down by the sword, breathe out his hostile life.

ATREUS
You speak of the end of his punishment; I want the punishment.
Let a gentle tyrant slay: in my kingdom
death is begged for as a favour.

ATTENDANT
Does no piety move you?

ATREUS
Depart, Piety, if ever you were
in our house at all; let the dread band of Furies come,
and discordant Erinys, and Megaera
brandishing her twin torches: my breast does not yet
burn with great enough frenzy; it pleases me to be filled
with a greater monstrousness.

ATTENDANT
What new thing do you raving devise?

ATREUS
Nothing that the measure of accustomed grief could hold;
I will leave no crime undone, and none is enough.

ATTENDANT
The sword?

ATREUS
Too little.

ATTENDANT
What of fire?

ATREUS
Even that is too little still.

ATTENDANT
With what weapon, then, will so great a grief make use?

ATREUS
With Thyestes himself.

ATTENDANT
This is an evil greater than wrath.

ATREUS
I confess it; an astounding tumult shakes my breast
and stirs it to the depths; I am swept along, and whither I know not,
but I am swept. The ground bellows from its lowest depths,
the clear day thunders, and the house, as if its roofs
were shattered, crashes, and the household gods, set moving,
turn away their faces: be it so, let this crime be done
which you gods would fear.

ATTENDANT
What at last do you prepare to do?

ATREUS
Some greater thing, beyond what I am wont,
swells in my soul, larger than usual,
and beyond the bounds of human custom,
and urges on my sluggish hands—what it may be I do not know,
but it is something great. So be it. Seize on this, my soul.
It is a deed worthy of Thyestes, and worthy of Atreus:
let each do it. The Odrysian house once saw
such unspeakable feasts—I confess, it is a monstrous crime,
but it has been done before: let my grief find
some greater thing. Inspire my soul, mother
and sister of Itys; my cause is like yours: stand by me and drive
my hand on. Let the eager father in his joy
rend his children and devour his own limbs.
It is well, it is more than enough. This manner of punishment
pleases me—for a while. But where is he now? Why so long
does Atreus walk innocent? Already before my eyes
the whole image of the slaughter moves; the loss of his children
heaped up into the father's face—my soul, why again
do you fear, and sink back before the deed? It must be dared, come:
that which is the chief horror in this crime,
that he himself shall do.

ATTENDANT
But by what guile, lured into our snares,
will he set his foot? He thinks all things hostile to him.

ATREUS
He could not be caught,
unless he wished to catch. He hopes now for my kingdom:
in this hope he would meet Jove threatening with his thunderbolt,
in this hope he would face the menaces of the swollen flood
and enter the doubtful straits of the Libyan Syrtis,
in this hope—what he reckons the greatest evil—
he will see his brother.

ATTENDANT
Who will give him assurance of peace?
To whom will he trust so great a thing?

ATREUS
Wicked hope is credulous.
Yet I will give my sons commands to bear to their uncle:
that, leaving his wandering exile's lodgings,
he exchange his misery for a kingdom and rule over Argos
as lord in part; if Thyestes, too hard,
scorns the prayers, his children, untried
and worn out by heavy ills and easy to be caught,
will be moved by the plea: here the old madness for the kingdom,
there grim want and harsh toil,
will subdue the man, however stiff with so many woes.

ATTENDANT
Now time has made his hardships light to him.

ATREUS
You are wrong: the sense of evils grows with the day.
It is light to bear miseries; to bear them through is heavy.

ATTENDANT
Choose other agents for your grim design.

ATREUS
The young hear the worse precepts readily.

ATTENDANT
They will do to their father what you teach them against their uncle:
crimes have often returned upon their teacher.

ATREUS
Though no one teach them the ways of fraud and crime,
kingship will teach them. Do you fear they will turn out wicked?
They are born so. That which you call savage and harsh,
and believe is done too cruelly and too impiously,
is perhaps being done over there as well.

ATTENDANT
Will the sons know
that this deceit is being prepared?

ATREUS
Silent faith
is not in years so untried; they would perhaps disclose the tricks:
to keep silent is learned through many ills of life.

ATTENDANT
And will you deceive the very ones through whom
you plan to deceive another?

ATREUS
That they themselves may be free
of crime and guilt. For what need is there
to mix my children into the crime? Through us
let our hatreds work themselves out.—You do ill,
you draw back, my soul: if you spare your own,
you will spare his as well. Let Agamemnon become
the knowing minister of my design, and Menelaus
knowingly aid his brother. From this crime let the truth
of their doubtful birth be sought: if they refuse the wars
and will not wage the hatreds, if they call him uncle—
he is their father. Let them go.—But the troubled face
often betrays much; great designs, even of one unwilling,
give themselves away: let them not know how great a thing
they minister to. And you—hide what I have begun.

ATTENDANT
I need no warning: that secret shall lie
within my breast through faith and through fear—but faith shall keep it more.

CHORUS

What madness drives you on,
to give blood in alternation
and to seize the sceptre by crime?
You do not know, you who covet citadels,
in what place a kingdom lies.
Riches do not make a king,
nor the colour of Tyrian raiment,
nor the mark of a royal brow,
nor beams gleaming with gold:
a king is he who has laid aside his fears
and the evils of a cruel heart,
whom unbridled ambition
and the never-stable favour
of the headlong crowd do not move,
nor whatever the West digs up
or the golden Tagus brings down
in its bright channel,
nor whatever the threshing-floor grinds,
hot with Libyan harvests;
whom the falling course
of the slanting thunderbolt will not shake,
nor the East Wind that sweeps the sea,
nor, raging in the savage strait,
the swell of the windy Adriatic;
whom no soldier's lance,
no naked steel has tamed,
who, set in a safe place,
sees all things below himself
and gladly goes to meet his fate,
and does not complain to die.
Though kings should gather
who harry the scattered Dahae,
who hold the shoals of the Red Sea's shore
and the sea, bright with shining gems,
broad and crimson;
or those who unlock the Caspian heights
to the brave Sarmatians;
let him who dares to walk on foot
across the ford of the Danube
contend; and (wherever they lie)
the Seres, famed for their fleece—
there is no need of any horses,
no need of arms and of the idle
weapons that the Parthian hurls from afar
when he feigns flight;
no need to bring up engines
to lay cities low,
hurling rocks from a distance.
A good mind possesses the kingdom.
This kingdom each man gives himself.
Let whoever wishes stand powerful
on the slippery summit of the court:
let sweet quiet sate me;
set in an obscure place,
let me enjoy soft leisure,
known to none of the citizens,
let my life flow on in silence.
So, when my days have passed
with no uproar,
let me die an old plebeian.
Death lies heavy upon the man
who, too well known to all,
dies unknown to himself.

THYESTES, with TANTALUS, PLISTHENES, and a third son (silent)

THYESTES
The longed-for roofs of my country and the wealth of Argos,
and—the highest good to the wretched and above all to exiles—
the soil of my birthland and my native gods
(if there are gods, after all) I behold; the sacred
towers of the Cyclopes, a glory greater than human toil,
the stadia thronged in my youth, through which, renowned,
I bore the palm more than once in my father's chariot.
Argos will come to meet me, the people will come in throngs—
but, no doubt, Atreus too. Seek again the woodland flights
and the dense thickets, rather, and a life mingled
with the beasts and like to theirs; this bright splendour of royalty
is not such that it should rob the eyes with false glitter:
when you look on what is given, look also on the giver.
Lately, amid those things which all men think harsh,
I was brave and glad; now, on the contrary, into fears
I am rolled back: my soul stands still and longs to bear
my body backward; I move my unwilling step.

TANTALUS
With sluggish pace (what is this?) my father stands amazed
and turns his face about and holds himself in doubt.

THYESTES
Why, my soul, do you waver, why so long
do you torment a choice so easy? Most uncertain things—
a brother and a kingdom—do you trust, and fear evils
already conquered, already grown tame, and flee hardships
well placed? It pleases you now to be wretched.
Turn back your step, while you may, and snatch yourself away.

TANTALUS
What cause compels you, father, to turn your step
from your country once you have seen it? Why from such great goods
do you draw back your lap? Your brother returns, his anger cast aside,
and gives back your share of the kingdom, and to the torn house
fits the limbs again, and restores you to yourself.

THYESTES
You ask the cause of a fear that I myself do not know.
I see nothing to be feared, yet I fear nonetheless.
I wish to go, yet my limbs falter on sluggish knees,
and I am borne, drawn aside, otherwise than where I strive.
So a ship, driven on by oar and sail,
the tide, resisting oar and sail, carries back.

TANTALUS
Overcome whatever stands in the way and hinders your mind,
and see what great rewards await you, returned.
Father, you can reign.

THYESTES
Yes—since I can die.

TANTALUS
Power is the highest thing—

THYESTES
It is nothing, if you crave nothing.

TANTALUS
You will leave it to your sons.

THYESTES
A kingdom holds not two.

TANTALUS
Does he choose to be wretched, who can be happy?

THYESTES
Believe me, great things please by false names,
and hard things are feared in vain. While I stood exalted,
I never ceased to be afraid, and to fear the very
sword at my own side. O what a good it is
to stand in no man's way, to take untroubled feasts
lying on the ground! Crimes do not enter cottages,
and all one's food is taken at a narrow table;
poison is drunk from gold—I speak from experience:
one may prefer bad fortune to good.
No lowly city trembles at a house set
on the high mountain-top and towering up,
nor does bright ivory gleam in lofty ceilings,
nor does a watchman guard my sleep;
we do not fish with fleets, nor put the sea
to flight with a hurled mole, nor do we feed
our shameless belly with the tribute of nations;
no field is measured out for me beyond the Getae
and the Parthians; we are not worshipped with incense,
nor, with Jove shut out, are my altars adorned;
no woodland sways, set on my rooftops,
nor do pools smoke, kindled by many a hand,
nor is the day to be joined to sleep
and the night, sleepless, to Bacchus; but we are not feared:
the house is safe without a weapon,
and great quiet is granted to small possessions—
it is a vast kingdom to be able to do without a kingdom.

TANTALUS
One must not refuse it, if a god gives the rule,
nor seek it: your brother asks you to reign.

THYESTES
Asks? Then it is to be feared. Here some trick goes astray.

TANTALUS
Piety is wont to return whence it was banished,
and true love repairs the strength it lost.

THYESTES
Does my brother love Thyestes? Sooner shall the sea
flood the ethereal Bears, and the rapacious tide
of the Sicilian wave grow still, and on the Ionian sea
a ripe harvest rise, and black night
give light to the lands—sooner shall water join faith
and treaty with flame, life with death, the wind with the sea—

TANTALUS
Yet what fraud do you fear?

THYESTES
Every fraud: what measure shall I set to my own fear?
He can do as much as he hates.

TANTALUS
Against you, what can he do?

THYESTES
For myself I fear nothing now: it is you who make
Atreus to be feared by me.

TANTALUS
Do you fear, wary, to be deceived?

THYESTES
It is too late a time for caution in the midst of evils:
let us go on. Yet this one thing, my sons, I bear witness to:
I follow you, I do not lead.

TANTALUS
A god will look with favour
on what is well-purposed; go on with no doubtful step.

ATREUS, THYESTES, TANTALUS, PLISTHENES, and the silent sons

ATREUS
The wild beast is held, shut in the snares set round it:
both himself and, joined to the parent, the brood
of the hated race I behold; now in a safe place
my hatreds turn. At last into my hands
has come Thyestes—he has come, and the whole of him indeed.
Scarcely do I rein in my soul, scarcely does my grief take the curb.
So, when the keen Umbrian hound tracks the game
and is held on a long leash, and with nose pressed down
scours the trails, while still far off he scents the boar
by its faint odour, he obeys, and with silent muzzle
ranges over the ground; but when the prey is nearer,
he fights with all his neck and calls with a whine
the lingering master, and tears himself from the one who holds him:
when wrath hopes for blood, it cannot be hid;
yet let it be hid. See how, heavy with much
squalor, his hair buries his sad face,
how foully his beard lies. Let faith be shown.—
It is good to see my brother; give me back the embraces
I have longed for. Whatever wrath there was,
let it have passed away; from this day let blood and love
be cherished, let the hatreds, condemned, fall from our souls.

THYESTES
I could have washed all this away, were you not such a one.
But I confess, Atreus, I confess, I committed all
that you believed; my cause this day
your love has made most wretched: he is wholly guilty
who has seemed guilty to so good a brother.
The matter must be done with tears: you are the first to see me a suppliant;
these hands, untouched by any feet, beg you:
let all wrath be laid aside, and the swelling, erased
from my soul, depart. Take these innocent ones
as pledges of my faith, my brother.

ATREUS
Take your hand
from my knees, and seek rather my embrace.
You too, defences of the old, so many young men,
hang upon my neck. Put off your squalid garment,
and spare our eyes, and take adornments
equal to mine. And gladly take your share
of your brother's rule. This is my greater glory,
to give back to my brother his ancestral honour, unharmed:
to hold a kingdom is chance; to give one is virtue.

THYESTES
May the gods, my brother, repay you equal rewards
for such great deserts. My squalor refuses
the royal mark upon my head, and my ill-omened hand
shrinks from the sceptre. Let me be allowed
to hide amid the crowd.

ATREUS
This kingdom welcomes two.

THYESTES
I count whatever is yours, my brother, to be mine.

ATREUS
Who refuses the gifts of inflowing fortune?

THYESTES
Whoever has known how easily they flow away.

ATREUS
Do you forbid your brother to gain great glory?

THYESTES
Your glory is now achieved; mine remains:
my fixed resolve is to refuse the kingdom.

ATREUS
I will leave my own share, unless you take yours.

THYESTES
I accept: I will bear the name of rule laid upon me,
but the laws and arms shall serve me along with you.

ATREUS
Wear the bands set upon your venerable head;
I will give the appointed victims to the gods above.

CHORUS
Could anyone believe this? That fierce, that keen,
that ungovernable, savage Atreus
stood stupefied at the sight of his brother and held still:
no force is greater than true love.
Quarrels with strangers endure as hatreds,
but those whom true love has held, love will hold.
When wrath, stirred by great causes,
broke their goodwill and called up war,
when the light squadrons clattered on their bits,
and the sword, brandished here and there, flashed—
the sword which raging Mars, craving fresh blood,
drives on with blow upon blow—
Love seizes the steel and, with joined hands,
leads to peace those who refuse.
What god has made this sudden calm
out of so great a tumult? Lately through Mycenae
the arms of civil war clattered:
pale mothers held their sons fast;
the wife feared for her armed husband,
when the sword, against his will, followed his hand,
foul with the rust of peaceful quiet;
one strove to restore the crumbling walls,
another to make firm the towers shaken with decay,
to bar the gates with iron bolts,
and the trembling sentinel, awake on the battlements
of the anxious night, kept watch:
the very fear of war is worse than war.
Now the threats of the savage sword have fallen,
now the grave murmur of the trumpets is silent,
now the shrill clamour of the war-horn is stilled:
deep peace is recalled to the joyful city.
So, when from the deep the waves have swollen,
the West Wind striking the Bruttian sea,
Scylla resounds from her battered caverns,
and the sailors in the harbour have feared the sea—
the sea that rapacious Charybdis swallows and spews up again—
and the fierce Cyclops, sitting on the rock
of seething Aetna, has feared his father,
lest the violator, with the overflowing waves,
quench the fire that resounds in his eternal furnaces,
and poor Laertes thinks his realms
can be sunk while Ithaca trembles:
if the winds' own force has fallen,
the sea sinks more gently than a still pool;
the deep, which the ship feared to cleave,
spread out here and there with billowing sails, made fair,
lies open to the sporting skiff,
and there is leisure to count the fish below,
here where but now, beneath a mighty storm,
the Cyclades feared the sea, set in motion.
No lot is lasting: grief and pleasure
give way in turn; pleasure is the briefer.
The light hour exchanges lowest things for highest:
he who sets the diadem on his brow,
before whom the nations trembled on bended knee,
at whose nod the Mede laid down his wars,
and the Indian nearer the rising sun,
and the Dahae who threatened the Parthians with their horse,
holds his sceptre in anxiety, and divines
that all things shift, and fears the changeful chances of affairs
and the doubtful hour.
You to whom the ruler of sea and land
has given the great right of death and of life,
lay aside your puffed-up, swollen looks:
whatever a lesser man dreads from you,
a greater lord threatens against you;
every kingdom is under a heavier kingdom.
Whom the rising day has seen proud,
the setting day has seen laid low.
Let no one trust too much in prosperity,
let no one despair of better things when weary:
Clotho mingles these with those, and forbids
Fortune to stand still, and turns every fate.
No one has had the gods so favouring
that he could promise himself a tomorrow:
god whirls our affairs, hurried on,
in a swift eddy.

MESSENGER
What whirlwind will carry me headlong through the air
and wrap me in a black cloud, to snatch
so great a horror from my eyes? O house
shameful even to Pelops and to Tantalus!

CHORUS
What do you bring of news?

MESSENGER
What land is this? Argos and Sparta, that drew by lot
holy brothers, and Corinth pressing the jaws
of the twin sea? Or the Hister, granting flight
to the fierce Alani? Or, beneath eternal snow,
the Hyrcanian land? Or the Scythians wandering everywhere?
What place is this, the witness of an unspeakable monstrous deed?

CHORUS
Speak out and disclose that evil, whatever it is.

MESSENGER
If my mind stand firm, if my body, stiff with fear,
release my limbs—the image of the grim deed
clings in my face. Carry me far off, you mad
storms, carry me there where the day is borne,
snatched from this place.

CHORUS
You hold our uncertain spirits the more in suspense.
Tell what it is you shudder at, and point out the author:
I ask not who he is, but which of them. Speak quickly.

MESSENGER
On the highest citadel of the house of Pelops there is a part
turned toward the south, whose furthest flank
rises as high as a mountain and presses on the city,
and holds beneath its stroke the people stubborn
against their kings; here gleams a hall, vast,
able to hold a multitude—whose gilded beams
the noble columns bear, spotted with various marks;
beyond these, well known to the people, where the throngs resort,
the rich house parts into many spaces;
a secret region lies in the deepest recess,
restraining in a deep valley an ancient grove,
the inmost sanctuary of the realm, where no tree
is wont to offer joyful branches or be tended with the knife,
but yew and cypress and the dark
woodland sway with black ilex, dim,
above which, towering high, an oak
looks down from on high and overtops the grove.
From here the sons of Tantalus are wont to take auspices for the realm,
from here to seek aid in weary and doubtful affairs.
Offerings hang fastened there: sounding trumpets
and chariots conquered, their axles fallen,
and broken cars, spoils of the Myrtoan sea,
and every crime of the race; here, in this place, is fixed
the Phrygian tiara of Pelops, here the spoil of enemies
and the cloak painted from a barbarian triumph.
A spring stands grim beneath the shade and lingers slow
in a black marsh: such is the misshapen wave
of the dread Styx, by which the gods swear faith to heaven.
From here, in the blind night, the gods of death
are said to groan; the grove rings with rattled chains,
and the shades howl; whatever it is fearful to hear
seems to be seen there: an ancient throng,
loosed from the old tombs, wanders, and there leap up
monsters greater than the known; nay, the woodland
is wont to flicker with flame, and the lofty beams
burn without fire. Often the grove resounds
with a threefold barking, often the house is struck
with mighty phantoms; nor does day allay the fear:
the grove has its own night, and the dread of the dead
reigns in the middle of the light. From here, to those who ask,
sure answers are given, when, with a mighty sound,
the fates are loosed from the shrine and the cavern bellows
as the god looses the voice. Into this place, raging,
Atreus came, dragging his brother's children;
the altars are adorned—who could worthily tell it?
Behind their backs he binds the noble youths' hands,
and binds their heads with the mournful purple fillet;
no incense is lacking, nor the sacred liquor of Bacchus,
nor the knife touching the victim with the salted meal.
Every rite is kept, lest so great a crime
be done not duly.

CHORUS
Who lays his hand to the steel?

MESSENGER
He himself is the priest, he himself with deadly prayer
chants the death-song with violent mouth;
he himself stands at the altars, he himself the doomed to death
handles and sets in place and brings to the steel;
he himself attends: no part of the sacred rite is lost.
The grove trembles; the whole hall, with the ground shaken,
nodded, doubtful where it should give its weight,
and like a thing tossing on the waves; from the left sky
a star ran, drawing a black furrow.
The wines, poured to the fires, flow changed,
bloody, the Bacchus altered; the royal crown
fell from his head twice and thrice; the ivory wept in the temples.
The portents moved all men, but Atreus alone,
unmoved, stands firm to himself, and even terrifies
the threatening gods. Now, all delay dismissed,
he stands at the altars, looking grim and askance.
As, fasting, a tigress in the Ganges' woods
wanders between two bullocks,
greedy for either prey, uncertain to which she should first
carry her bite (she turns her jaws this way,
turns them back that, and holds her hunger in doubt),
so dread Atreus eyes the doomed heads of his
impious wrath: whom he should first slaughter for himself
he hesitates, then with which second slaughter he should offer next.
It makes no difference, but he hesitates, and it pleases him
to set his savage crime in order.

CHORUS
Whom, then, does he take first with the steel?

MESSENGER
The first place (lest you think there was no piety)
is given to the grandsire: Tantalus is the first victim.

CHORUS
With what spirit, with what face did the youth bear his death?

MESSENGER
He stood untroubled for himself, and did not suffer
his prayers to be lost in vain; but the savage one
hid the sword in his wound and, pressing deep,
joined his hand to the throat: the corpse, the steel drawn out,
stood, and when it had wavered long
whether to fall this way or that, it fell upon the uncle.
Then the savage one drags Plisthenes to the altars
and adds him to his brother; he severs the struck neck;
the trunk, the neck cut, falls forward,
and the head ran off, murmuring with uncertain plaint.

CHORUS
What does he do next, the double slaughter accomplished?
Does he spare the boy, or heap crime on crime?

MESSENGER
As a maned lion in the Armenian woods,
victor in much slaughter, lies upon the herd
(his jaws wet with gore, and, his hunger driven off,
he does not lay aside his rage: pressing the bulls this way and that
he threatens the calves, sluggish now with weary tooth),
just so Atreus rages and swells with wrath,
and holding the steel drenched with the double slaughter,
forgetting against whom he raged, with hostile hand
he drove it on through the body—so that at once
the sword, taken in the boy's breast, came out at his back;
he falls, and quenching the altars with his own blood,
dies through both wounds.

CHORUS
O savage crime!

MESSENGER
Do you shudder? If the wickedness stop here,
he is pious.

CHORUS
Does nature admit
anything greater or more atrocious beyond this?

MESSENGER
Do you think this the end of the crime?
It is a step.

CHORUS
What could he do beyond it? Did he cast the bodies, perhaps,
to the beasts to be torn, and keep them from the fire?

MESSENGER
Would that he had kept them from it! That the earth might not cover the dead,
nor the fire dissolve them! Let him drag them, a grim food,
to be banqueted upon by the birds and the savage beasts—
the prayer here is for what is wont to be a punishment:
that the father look upon them unburied! O crime
credible in no age, which posterity will deny—
the entrails, torn from the living breasts, quiver,
and the veins breathe, and the still-fearful heart leaps;
but he handles the lobes and inspects the fates
and marks the still-warm veins of the inner parts.
After the victims pleased him, free of care he has leisure
now for his brother's banquet: he himself cuts the body,
divided into limbs, lops off at the trunk
the spreading shoulders and the hindrances of the arms,
bares the joints, harsh, and cuts off the bones;
only the faces he keeps, and the hands given in faith.
These entrails cling on the spits and, set
to the slow embers, drip; those the boiling water
tosses in the complaining cauldron; the fire leapt over
the dishes set upon it, and into the trembling hearths,
thrust back twice and thrice and bidden to endure delay,
it burns unwilling; the liver hisses on the spits;
nor could I easily say whether the bodies or the flames
groaned the more. The fire passes into pitch-black smoke;
and the smoke itself, grim and a heavy fog,
goes not straight up, nor lifts itself on high:
it besets the very household gods with its hideous cloud.
O patient Phoebus, though you have fled backward
and plunged the broken day in the middle of the sky,
you set too late. The father mangles his sons
and chews his own limbs with his deadly mouth;
he gleams, his hair wet with flowing unguent,
and heavy with wine; often his closed throat
held back the food—in your evils this one good
remains, Thyestes, that you do not know your own evils.
But even this will perish, though Titan turn his car
backward, leading his path to meet itself,
and night, sent from the east at an alien hour, heavy,
overwhelm the foul deed with new darkness,
yet it must be seen. All the evils will be laid bare.

CHORUS
Whither, ruler of lands and gods, do you flee,
at whose rising all the glory of dark night
takes flight? Whither do you turn your course
and lose the day in mid-Olympus?
Why, Phoebus, do you snatch away your face?
Not yet does Vesper, the herald of the late hour,
summon the nocturnal lights;
not yet does the turning of the Hesperian wheel
bid loose the chariots that have earned their rest;
not yet, as day inclines to night,
has the third trumpet given the signal:
the ploughman, his oxen not yet weary,
stands amazed at the time of a sudden supper.
What has driven you from your ethereal course?
What cause has thrown your horses
from their certain path?
Are the conquered Giants, the prison of Dis
thrown open, attempting wars anew?
Does wounded Tityos, with weary breast,
renew his ancient wrath?
Has Typhoeus, the mountain cast aside,
freed his flank?
Is a high road being built through the Phlegraean foes,
and is Thessalian Pelion pressed
by Thracian Ossa?
Have the wonted changes of the world perished?
Will there be no setting, no rising?
The dewy mother of the first light,
wont to hand the reins to the god of dawn,
stands amazed at the overturned thresholds of her realm;
she knows not how to bathe his weary
chariot, nor to plunge in the sea
his manes smoking with sweat.
The Sun himself, new to the strange lodging,
setting, sees the Dawn,
and bids the shadows rise though
the night is not yet ready: no
stars come on, nor does the pole gleam with any fire,
no heavy Moon disperses the shadows.
But whatever it is, would that it were night!
They tremble, they tremble,
our hearts, struck with great fear:
lest all things, shattered, fall in fatal ruin,
and again deformed chaos press down
the gods and men,
and again Nature cover the lands
and the sea that girds them
and the wandering stars of the painted world.
No more shall the leader of the stars,
guiding the ages by the rising of his eternal torch,
give the marks of summer and of winter;
no more shall the Moon, meeting Phoebus' flames,
take their fears from the night
and outrun her brother's reins,
running a shorter course in her path;
the crowded throng of gods will go
heaped into one bay;
he who, a path among the sacred stars,
cuts the zones by his slanting track,
the Zodiac-bearer guiding the long years,
will see the constellations fall, as he himself falls;
he who, the kindly spring not yet come,
gives not back the sails to the warm Zephyr,
the Ram, will go headlong into the waves
through which he had carried trembling Helle;
he who, the Bull, with shining horn
leads on the Hyades, will drag with him
the Twins and the arms of the curving Crab;
the Lion, blazing with fiery heats,
will fall again from the sky, the Lion of Hercules;
the Virgin will fall to the lands she forsook,
and the just weights of the Scales will fall
and drag with them the keen Scorpion;
and the old Chiron, who holds on his Haemonian string
the feathered arrows, will lose his arrows,
his string snapped;
the cold Goat-horn, bringing back sluggish winter,
will fall and break your urn,
whoever you are; with you will depart
the Fishes, the last stars of heaven,
and the Wain, never washed by the sea,
the gulf, hiding all things, will sink;
and the slippery Serpent, that divides
the two Bears, like a river,
and the Lesser, joined to the great Dragon,
cold Cynosura with its hard frost,
and the slow guardian of his own wain,
the Bear-keeper, now no longer steady, will fall.
Have we, of so great a people, been seen
worthy to be crushed, the hinge
of the world overturned?
Has the last age come upon us?
O we, created under a hard lot,
whether we have wretchedly lost the sun,
or driven it away!
Away with complaints, depart, fear:
he is too greedy of life who is unwilling
to die with the world perishing along with him.

ATREUS
Equal to the stars I stride, and above all men,
touching the high pole with my proud head.
Now I hold the glories of the realm, now my father's throne;
I dismiss the gods: I have reached the summit of my prayers.
It is well, it is more than enough. Now it is even enough for me.
But why should it be enough? I will go on, and, the father glutted
with the death of his own, lest any shame should stand in the way,
the day has departed: go on while the sky is free.
Would, indeed, that I could hold the fleeing gods
and drag them all together, that all might see
my avenging banquet—but it is enough that the father see it.
Even with the day unwilling I will scatter for you
the darkness, beneath which your miseries lie hid.
Too long you lie at the feast, a guest untroubled
and of cheerful face; now enough has been given to the tables
and enough to Bacchus: for such great evils there is need
of a sober Thyestes. Servants of the household, loose
the doors of the temple, let the festal house be thrown open.
It pleases me to see, gazing on the sons' faces,
what colours he shows, what words his first grief
pours out, or how, stunned, his breath driven out,
his body grows stiff. This is the fruit of my work.
I do not wish to see him wretched, but while he is becoming wretched.
The opened halls gleam with many a torch.
He himself reclines, leaning back on purple and on gold,
propping with his left hand his head, heavy with wine.
He belches. O me, the highest of the heavenly ones,
and king of kings! I have surpassed my prayers.
He is full, he draws the unmixed wine in the capacious silver—
do not spare the drink: there still remains the blood
of so many victims; the colour of the old Bacchus
will hide it—with this, with this cup let the table be closed.
Let the father drink the mingled blood of his own:
he would have drunk mine. Lo, now he stirs up song
and festal voices, nor governs his mind enough.

THYESTES
My heart, blunted by long ills,
lay aside now your anxious cares.
Let grief flee, let dread flee,
let the companion of trembling exile flee,
grim want,
and shame, heavy in afflicted things:
it matters more whence you fall than whither.
It is a great thing, fallen from a high summit,
to set a steady step upon the level ground;
a great thing, pressed by a mighty heap of ills,
to bear the weight of a broken kingdom
with neck unbowed,
and, not degenerate or conquered by ills,
upright, to bear the ruins laid upon one.
But now drive off the clouds of savage fate
and put away all the marks
of the wretched time;
let the looks of good fortune return to joy,
send the old Thyestes from your mind.
This vice ever follows the wretched:
never to trust in joyful things;
though happy fortune return,
yet the afflicted are loath to rejoice.
Why do you call me back, and forbid me to keep
the festal day? Why bid me weep,
grief rising from no cause?
Why do you forbid me to bind my hair
with seemly flowers? It forbids, it forbids!
The spring roses, fallen from my head,
my hair, dripping with rich balsam,
stood up amid sudden shudders,
a rain falls from my unwilling face,
a groan comes in the midst of my words,
grief loves the accustomed tears,
the wretched have a dread desire to weep.
It pleases me to send forth ill-omened laments,
it pleases me to rend my robes, soaked
with Tyrian purple; it pleases me to wail.
My mind sends signs of future grief,
foreboding its own evil before it comes:
a fierce storm threatens the sailors,
when the calm swells without wind.
What griefs, what tumults do you imagine
for yourself, madman? Show a trusting
heart to your brother: now, whatever it is,
either without cause or too late you fear.
I do not wish it, unhappy man, but a wandering
terror roams within; my eyes pour out
sudden tears, and no cause lies beneath.
Is it grief or fear?
Or has great joy its tears?

ATREUS
Let us celebrate the festal day, my brother,
in equal accord: this is the day to make my sceptre firm
and bind the solid pledge of sure peace.

THYESTES
Surfeit of feasting holds me, and no less of Bacchus.
This addition can increase my pleasure,
if it be granted me to rejoice with my own amid this happiness.

ATREUS
Believe that your sons are here in their father's embrace;
here they are and shall be; no part of your offspring
shall be taken from you. The faces you long for I will give,
and fill the whole father with his own throng;
you shall be sated, fear not. Now, mingled with mine,
they keep the pleasant rites of the youthful table;
but they shall be summoned. Take the cup of your kindred,
poured full with Bacchus.

THYESTES
I take the gift of my brother's feast;
let wine be poured to our ancestral gods,
then let it be drunk. But what is this? My hands will not
obey; the weight grows and burdens my right hand;
the wine, brought to my very lips, flees,
and flows round my mouth with deceived lips,
and the table itself leaped up from the trembling floor.
Scarcely does the fire gleam; nay, the very heavy air,
between day and night, stands deserted and amazed.
What is this? More and more the vaults of heaven,
shaken, totter; a thicker darkness gathers
with dense shadows, and night has hidden itself in night;
every star has fled. Whatever it is, I pray it spare
my brother and my sons; let all the storm
fall upon this worthless head. Now give me back my sons!

ATREUS
I will give them back, and no day shall ever snatch them from you.

THYESTES
What tumult is this that stirs my entrails?
What trembled within? I feel an impatient burden,
and my breast groans with a groan not mine.
Come, my sons, your unhappy father calls;
come. At the sight of you this grief will flee—
whence do they answer back?

ATREUS
Make ready your embraces, father:
they have come. Do you at all recognize your sons?

THYESTES
I recognize my brother. Do you endure to bear
so great a crime, O Earth? Do you not, broken,
plunge yourself into the infernal Styx and the shadows,
and by a vast road snatch the realm with its king
to the empty chaos? Do you not, tearing up the whole house
from its lowest ground, overturn Mycenae? Both of us
ought by now to stand about Tantalus: here, with the joints
torn apart on this side and on that, if anything lies below Tartarus
and below our forefathers, into your monstrous bosom
let down this valley, and bury us, sunk deep,
beneath all Acheron. Above our head let the guilty
souls wander, and let the fiery Phlegethon,
driving all its sands with its burning flood,
flow violent above our exile—
Unmoved earth, do you lie, a sluggish weight?
The gods have fled.

ATREUS
Now rather receive these gladly,
long longed-for—there is no delay through your brother:
enjoy them, kiss them, share embraces among the three.

THYESTES
Is this your compact? This your favour, this a brother's faith?
Do you so lay aside your hatreds? I do not ask
to have my sons, a father unharmed; what could be given
with crime and hatred safe, a brother of his brother I ask:
let me be allowed to bury them; give back what you may see
burned at once; I ask of you nothing that a father shall keep,
but shall destroy.

ATREUS
Whatever remains of your sons, you have;
whatever does not remain, you have.

THYESTES
Do they lie as food for the savage birds?
Or are they kept for the beasts? Or do they feed the wild creatures?

ATREUS
You yourself have banqueted on your sons in an impious feast.

THYESTES
This is what shamed the gods, this drove the day
turned back to its rising. What words shall I, wretched, utter,
what laments? What words will suffice me?
I behold the severed heads and the torn-off hands
and the footsteps broken with the shattered legs—
this is what the greedy father could not take in.
The entrails turn within, and the shut-in crime,
having no way out, struggles and seeks escape.
Give me, brother, your sword (it has much of my blood):
let a way be given to my children by the steel.
Is the sword refused? Let my breast resound,
bruised, struck by the blow—stay, unhappy man, your hand;
let us spare the shades. Who has seen such a crime?
What Heniochus, dwelling on the rough rock
of inhospitable Caucasus, or what Procrustes, terror
to the Cecropian lands? Behold, a father, I press my sons,
and am pressed by my sons—is there any measure to crime?

ATREUS
A measure is owed to crime when you do the crime,
not when you repay it. Even this is too little for me.
From the very wound I ought to have poured the warm blood
into your mouth, that you might drink the gore
of the living—my wrath was cheated of its words
while I hurried; I dealt the wounds with the pressed-in steel,
I felled them at the altars, I appeased the hearths
with the votive slaughter, and the limbs, cutting the lifeless bodies
into small pieces, I plunged some in the boiling
cauldrons, others I bade drip on slow fires;
I cut off the limbs and sinews while they yet lived,
and saw the entrails, pierced on the slender spit,
bellow, and heaped on the flames with my own hand—
all this the father could have done better;
my grief has fallen out in vain:
he rent his sons with impious mouth, but unknowing,
but they unknowing.

THYESTES
Hear, you seas enclosed by wandering shores;
hear, you too, this crime, wherever, gods,
you have fled; hear, you of the underworld;
hear, you lands, and Night, heavy with Tartarean
and black cloud, give heed to our voices
(to you I am left, you alone behold the wretch,
you too without your stars)—I will make no wicked prayers,
for myself I will beg nothing—and what now can be
for me? My prayers shall look to your good.
You, supreme ruler of heaven, lord of the ethereal hall,
wrap the whole world in dreadful clouds,
join battles of the winds on every side,
and from every quarter thunder violently,
and with the hand—not that with which you seek
the roofs and undeserving homes with the lesser bolt,
but that with which the threefold mass of mountains
fell, and the Giants who stood matched with the mountains—
ready those arms and hurl your fires,
avenge the lost day, hurl your flames,
fill with thunderbolts the light snatched from the pole.
The cause—lest you doubt long—let it be the guilt of us both;
or if not, let mine be guilty:
seek me, send through this breast the flaming brand
on your three-forked weapon—if I, a father,
wish to bury my sons and give them to the final fire,
it is I who must be burned. If nothing moves the gods above,
and no divine power seeks the impious with its bolts,
let eternal night remain and cover with darkness
crimes vast and long; I make no complaint, Titan,
if you persevere.

ATREUS
Now I praise my hands,
now the true palm is won. I would have lost my crime,
had you not grieved so. Now I believe my children
are born to me; now I believe faith is restored to a chaste marriage-bed.

THYESTES
What did my children deserve?

ATREUS
That they were yours.

THYESTES
Sons to a father—

ATREUS
I confess it, and—what delights me—
sure ones.

THYESTES
I call to witness the gods, guardians of the pious.

ATREUS
Why not the gods of marriage?

THYESTES
Who pays back crime with crime?

ATREUS
I know what you complain of: you grieve that the crime was snatched from you,
nor does it torment you that you drank the unspeakable feast—
but that you did not prepare it! This was your intent:

THYESTES
The avenging gods will be present;
to them my prayers deliver you to be punished.

ATREUS
To be punished I deliver you to your sons.
